I now have enough to give a solid, verified list of primary studies (not reviews/guidelines) that directly compared venipuncture and heel lance for blood sampling in full-term newborns.

## Summary

There's a fairly consistent body of individual primary studies (mostly RCTs, one large observational/randomised study) on this question, and they point the same direction: **venipuncture, when done by a skilled operator, is generally less painful and more often successful on the first attempt than heel lance in healthy, full-term newborns.** Sample sizes are modest (most 100–120 infants; one Spanish study had 607), and outcomes are mostly behavioral/physiological pain scores (crying duration, NFCS, NIPS, PIPP) plus procedural efficiency (number of punctures, time to complete).

## Primary studies (individual, not reviews/guidelines)

1. **Larsson BA, Tannfeldt G, Lagercrantz H, Olsson GL.** Venipuncture is more effective and less painful than heel lancing for blood tests in neonates. *Pediatrics.* 1998;101(5):882–886.
 — RCT, 120 healthy full-term infants (PKU testing); NFCS scores and crying significantly lower with venipuncture.

2. **Shah VS, Taddio A, Bennett S, Speidel BD.** Neonatal pain response to heel stick vs venepuncture for routine blood sampling. *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed.* 1997;77(2):F143–F144.
 — RCT, 27 term infants; NIPS scores higher (more pain) with heel stick.

3. **Eriksson M, Gradin M, Schollin J.** Oral glucose and venepuncture reduce blood sampling pain in newborns. *Early Hum Dev.* 1999;55(3):211–218.
 — RCT, 120 healthy full-term infants, 2×2 design (venipuncture vs heel stick, with/without glucose); crying time and PIPP scores lower with venipuncture, especially without glucose.

4. **Ogawa S, Ogihara T, Fujiwara E, et al.** Venepuncture is preferable to heel lance for blood sampling in term neonates. *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed.* 2005;90(5):F432–F436.
 — RCT, 100 healthy full-term infants, 2×2 design (venipuncture vs heel lance, with/without sucrose); NFCS scores lower with venipuncture.

5. **Kvist LJ, Jonsson K, Tornestrand BM, Edwinson Månsson M.** Can venepuncture reduce the pain of neonatal PKU-sampling? A randomised study. *Vård i Norden.* 2002;22(4):27–30.
 — RCT comparing the two methods for PKU screening blood sampling.

6. **Correcher Medina P, Pedrón Marzal G, Rey Simón R, Calvo Rigual F.** Venopunción en el dorso de la mano. ¿Una alternativa a la punción del talón? [Back-of-the-hand venepuncture: an alternative to heel puncture?]. *An Pediatr (Barc).* 2012;77(6):381–385.
 — Observational, randomised study, 607 consecutive full-term newborns (280 venipuncture, 327 heel puncture); lower NIPS pain scores, shorter crying, faster and fewer repeat punctures with venipuncture, no increase in false-positive hypothyroid screens.

7. **Logan PW.** Venepuncture versus heel prick for the collection of the Newborn Screening Test. *Aust J Adv Nurs.* 1999;17(1):30–36.
 — Study comparing the two collection methods for the newborn screening (Guthrie) test.

A couple of notes for your own appraisal: study #7 (Logan) I could confirm by title/journal/year via multiple reference lists but couldn't independently verify all methodological details from open-access sources — worth pulling the full text before relying on it. Also, several of these trials used sucrose/glucose as a co-intervention arm (studies 3 and 4), so when reading them you'll want to isolate the no-sweetener comparison if you're purely interested in venipuncture vs. heel lance itself.
