Here are individual primary studies (randomized/controlled trials) that directly compare venepuncture with heel lance for blood sampling in full-term newborns. I excluded the Cochrane systematic review, narrative reviews, and guideline documents, and focused only on the original research trials — these are, in fact, the core primary studies that the Cochrane review (Shah & Ohlsson) itself synthesized.

**Summary of key studies found:**

- Larsson et al. (1998) randomized 120 healthy full-term infants undergoing PKU screening into venipuncture, small-lancet heel lance, and large-lancet heel lance groups, assessing pain reactions with the Neonatal Facial Coding System.

- Eriksson, Gradin, and Schollin (1999) randomized 120 full-term newborns to venepuncture or heel stick, with or without oral glucose, measuring crying duration, the Premature Infant Pain Profile (PIPP), and heart rate changes. Without glucose, crying time was 57.3 s in the heel-stick group versus 26.8 s in the venepuncture group (P=0.0041).

- A 2005 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Ogawa and colleagues allocated 100 healthy full-term newborns screened for inborn errors of metabolism to one of four groups comparing venepuncture and heel lance with or without sucrose, assessing pain via the Neonatal Facial Coding System and crying.

- Saththasivam et al. (2009) studied 66 term neonates undergoing blood glucose monitoring, randomized to venipuncture or heel prick, using the Neonatal Facial Coding System score, first-cry duration, total cry duration, and procedure duration as outcomes, with number of skin punctures as a secondary outcome. The total duration of the procedure was significantly longer for venipuncture (27.5 s) than heel prick (7 s; p<0.001), and three babies in the venipuncture group needed more than one skin puncture versus none in the heel-prick group.

- An earlier trial, Shah VS, Taddio A, Bennett S, Speidel BD — "Neonatal pain response to heel stick vs. venepuncture for routine blood sampling" (Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed 1997;77:F143–4) — is also part of this primary evidence base.

- Logan PW — "Venepuncture versus heel prick for the collection of the Newborn Screening Test" (Aust J Adv Nurs 1999;17:30–36) — is another original comparative study in this literature.

A note on completeness: these six trials are the ones most consistently identified as the original comparative studies in this specific literature (venepuncture vs. heel lance, term neonates). I did not find additional head-to-head primary trials published after 2009 restricted specifically to full-term infants — more recent neonatal pain research in this area has tended to compare devices (e.g., automatic lancets vs. needles) or analgesic adjuncts rather than venepuncture vs. heel lance directly.
