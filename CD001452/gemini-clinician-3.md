Based on the clinical evidence from primary randomized controlled trials, **venepuncture is generally more effective and significantly less painful than a heel lance** for drawing blood in full-term newborns, provided it is performed by a skilled clinician.

However, there is a notable exception regarding very small-volume tests. Here is a breakdown of what the primary trials demonstrate:

## Pain and Distress

Studies consistently show that drawing blood from a vein (typically on the dorsum of the hand) causes less physiological and behavioral distress than lancing the heel.

* **Lower Pain Scores:** Trials utilizing standardized neonatal pain scales—such as the Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS) or the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS)—routinely record lower pain scores for venepuncture. For example, a 2012 trial recorded a median NIPS score of 3.5 for venepuncture compared to 7.0 for heel lancing.
* **Crying Duration:** Venepuncture is associated with a significantly shorter duration of the first cry and total crying time.
* **Synergy with Sweet Solutions:** Administering oral sucrose or dextrose before a heel lance helps reduce pain, but a 2005 trial found that venepuncture *without* sucrose was still less painful than a heel lance *with* sucrose.

## Effectiveness and Repeat Punctures

For standard blood tests that require more than a single drop of blood (such as newborn metabolic screening or serum bilirubin levels), venepuncture is significantly more efficient.

* **Fewer Punctures:** A 1998 trial found that 86% of infants in the venepuncture group required only one puncture to obtain an adequate sample. In contrast, only 19% to 40% of the heel lance groups yielded enough blood on the first try. Heel lances frequently require further skin punctures or intense squeezing of the heel, both of which exacerbate tissue damage and pain.
* **Sample Quality:** Venepuncture generally yields a larger volume of blood and carries a lower risk of hemolysis (rupturing of red blood cells) or clotting, which can ruin a sample and force a redraw.

## The Exception: Single-Drop Glucose Testing

While venepuncture is superior for larger volumes, heel lancing remains the preferred method when only a single drop of blood is required (e.g., for bedside capillary blood glucose monitoring). A 2009 trial specifically comparing the two methods for glucose monitoring found no significant difference in pain scores, but noted that the venepuncture procedure took significantly longer (a median of 27 seconds versus just 7 seconds for the heel prick).
