Congratulations on your newborn! It is completely natural to feel anxious when your baby needs a medical procedure. Wanting to minimize their discomfort and find the gentlest option makes you a wonderful advocate for your child.

While it might seem counterintuitive, research consistently shows that drawing blood from a vein with a needle (venepuncture) is actually **less painful and more effective** than pricking the heel (heel lance) for full-term babies.

Here is what the primary clinical trials reveal about the two methods.

### Why Venepuncture is Less Painful

Most parents assume a heel prick is a tiny, quick stick, similar to a finger prick for an adult. However, a heel lance involves cutting slightly deeper into the tissue. Because blood doesn't always flow freely from the heel, medical staff often have to gently squeeze and "milk" the heel repeatedly to gather enough blood for the test. It is this repeated squeezing of the bruised tissue that causes the most discomfort and prolongs the baby's crying (Ogawa, 2005).

In contrast, venepuncture—which is often done on the back of the baby's hand—requires a single needle stick. Once the needle is in the vein, the blood flows naturally into the tube without the need for painful squeezing. Studies measuring newborns' pain indicators (such as facial expressions, heart rate changes, and crying) consistently show that babies experience significantly less pain with venepuncture (Shah et al., 1997).

### Faster and More Effective

Because blood flows more easily from a vein, venepuncture is generally a faster procedure than a heel lance. Trials show that it takes less time overall, and the blood sample is usually larger and of better quality—meaning there is a lower chance the blood will clot or haemolyze, which would require the baby to be poked a second time. In one clinical trial, babies who underwent venepuncture cried for less than half the time of those who received a heel lance (Eriksson et al., 1999).

### The Important Catch: Training Matters

There is one very important caveat to this research: venepuncture is only the superior option **when the person performing it is highly trained and experienced** in drawing blood from tiny newborn veins. Heel lances are standard practice in many hospitals because they are easier for general medical staff to perform. If the nurses or phlebotomists available are not specifically confident with infant veins, a heel prick might be the quicker, safer choice for that specific team.

**A tip for your baby's appointment:** Regardless of which method the hospital uses, you can ask the medical team to give your baby a few drops of a sweet sugar-water solution (oral sucrose) on their tongue a couple of minutes before the poke. You can also ask to hold your baby skin-to-skin or breastfeed them during the draw. Research shows these non-pharmacological comfort measures drastically reduce a newborn's perception of pain for both procedures.
