Based on primary randomized controlled trials, venepuncture is generally shown to be **less painful, faster, and more effective** than heel lancing for routine blood sampling in full-term neonates.

While heel lancing has historically been the default capillary sampling method due to its perceived ease of use, direct comparisons challenge its superiority when adequate sample volumes are needed. Here is a breakdown of what the primary literature reveals about how the two methods compare.

## Pain and Distress

Across multiple primary studies, infants undergoing venepuncture demonstrate significantly lower pain responses compared to those undergoing heel lances.

* **Objective Pain Scales:** Venepuncture consistently yields lower scores on validated neonatal pain assessments, including the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS), the Premature Infant Pain Profile (PIPP), and the Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS) (Eriksson et al., 1999; Ogawa, 2005; Shah et al., 1997).
* **Crying Duration:** Without the use of sweetening agents, the total crying time for infants receiving a heel stick is roughly double that of infants receiving venepuncture (Eriksson et al., 1999; Ogawa, 2005).

## Procedural Efficacy and Repeat Sampling

Venepuncture often requires fewer attempts and takes less total time than heel lancing.

* **Sample Integrity:** Heel lancing relies on squeezing the tissue to extract blood, which can lead to hemolysis and clotted samples, sometimes necessitating a second prick. In contrast, venepuncture typically secures the sample in a single pass (Shah et al., 1997).
* **Time to Completion:** Venepuncture has been shown to reduce total procedural time, largely because of the continuous venous flow compared to the intermittent capillary flow and repeated squeezing required during a heel lance (Ogawa, 2005).

## The Role of Analgesia

Using oral sucrose or glucose significantly blunts the pain response for *both* procedures, changing the comparative dynamic.

* When 30% oral glucose is administered prior to the skin puncture, the pain scores and crying times between heel lancing and venepuncture become statistically similar, suggesting that effective analgesia can offset the inherently higher pain of a heel lance (Eriksson et al., 1999).
* However, when no sweet solution is given, venepuncture remains clearly superior for pain control (Ogawa, 2005).

## Parental Perception

You might encounter parental resistance to venepuncture because a needle draw often looks more invasive to lay observers than a prick to the heel. However, when mothers observed both procedures in a clinical trial, they initially reported higher anxiety about the venepuncture but ultimately rated it as *less* painful for their infants after watching the blood draw occur (Shah et al., 1997).

**Clinical Takeaway:** If you have trained staff proficient in neonatal venepuncture, it is the preferable choice over heel lancing for routine screening tests—especially when larger blood volumes are required. If you must use a heel lance, pairing it with an oral sweet solution and an automated lancing device is highly recommended to manage the pain.
