Primary randomized controlled trials evaluating blood sampling in full-term neonates consistently indicate that venepuncture (VP) outperforms heel lancing (HL) in both pain reduction and sampling efficacy.

## Pain Responses

VP is associated with significantly lower pain levels than HL. When evaluated using validated metrics like the Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS), infants subjected to VP show markedly lower distress scores compared to those undergoing HL (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005). The incidence of crying is also substantially reduced; one trial noted that 44% of infants cried during the first 60 seconds of VP, compared to 72–85% during heel lancing (Larsson et al., 1998). The increased pain during HL is largely attributed to the necessity of squeezing the heel repeatedly to extract sufficient blood, a step circumvented by VP (Ogawa, 2005).

## Sampling Efficacy

VP is more efficient for obtaining adequate sample volumes (such as those needed for inborn error of metabolism screenings). Studies demonstrate that VP requires significantly fewer repeat skin punctures; up to 86% of VP attempts yield a successful sample on the first puncture, whereas HL success rates on the first attempt can drop to 19–40% depending on the lancet used (Larsson et al., 1998). Consequently, the total time required to complete the blood draw is significantly shorter for VP (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005).

## Impact of Analgesic Solutions

While administering oral sucrose prior to the procedure reduces pain for both methods, the fundamental physiological advantage of VP remains intact. Even when sucrose is administered prior to a heel lance, the resulting pain scores remain higher than those of a venepuncture performed *without* sucrose (Ogawa, 2005).

The primary barrier noted across these trials is operator skill: the analgesic and efficacy benefits of VP are contingent upon the procedure being performed by trained staff (Ogawa, 2005).
