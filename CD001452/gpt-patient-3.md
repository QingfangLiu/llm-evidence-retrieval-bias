## Bottom line

For a healthy full-term newborn who needs **more than a single drop of blood**, the individual comparative studies generally found that **venepuncture performed by an experienced clinician caused less immediate pain and often required fewer punctures or less sampling time than heel lancing**. ([PubMed][1])

The important exception is **bedside glucose testing requiring only one drop**: one study found no significant pain difference, while the heel prick was substantially faster. ([PubMed][2])

This evidence mainly concerns **immediate pain, crying, number of attempts, and sampling time**. It provides little information about uncommon complications or long-term outcomes.

## How to interpret this for your baby

The evidence supports asking whether **venepuncture by someone experienced in newborn blood collection** is appropriate when the test requires several drops or a small tube of blood. The benefit appears to depend partly on staff skill, needle size, and the amount of blood needed.

A heel lance may remain the more practical choice when:

* Only one drop is needed, particularly for bedside glucose testing.
* The particular newborn-screening or laboratory process requires capillary blood.
* A suitable vein cannot be identified promptly.
* The available clinician has substantially more experience with heel sampling.

Comfort measures matter whichever method is used. In the two direct comparison trials that tested oral glucose or sucrose, the sweet solution reduced behavioral signs of pain, although it did not always eliminate the difference between the two procedures. ([PubMed][4])

Overall, I would characterize the evidence as: **venepuncture is usually less painful and more efficient for obtaining a meaningful blood specimen from a full-term newborn, provided it is performed by a skilled clinician; heel prick can be preferable for a single-drop test.**

## Limitations of the evidence

Most studies were relatively small, and several were conducted more than 20 years ago. They used different pain scales, lancet sizes, needles, comfort measures, and blood volumes. Clinicians could not be blinded to the technique, and some studies were not rigorously randomized. The studies largely measured reactions during the first few minutes rather than bruising, infection, nerve injury, repeated-procedure effects, or other uncommon complications.
