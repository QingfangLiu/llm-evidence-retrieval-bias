Here are individual primary studies (RCTs) I found comparing venepuncture with heel lance for blood sampling in full-term newborns. These are original research reports, not reviews or guidelines.

## Primary studies identified

**1. Larsson BA, Tannfeldt G, Lagercrantz H, Olsson GL (1998).** *Venipuncture is more effective and less painful than heel lancing for blood tests in neonates.* Pediatrics, 101(5):882–886.
This was a randomized trial of 120 healthy, full-term infants undergoing testing for phenylketonuria, randomized into a venipuncture group, a heel-lance group using a small lancet, and a heel-lance group using a large lancet, with pain reactions assessed using the Neonatal Facial Coding System.

**2. Shah VS, Taddio A, Bennett S, Speidel BD (1997).** *Neonatal pain response to heel stick vs venepuncture for routine blood sampling.* Archives of Disease in Childhood – Fetal and Neonatal Edition, 77(2):F143–F144.
Twenty-seven infants were randomly allocated to venepuncture or heel stick, with pain assessed by nurses using the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale and by mothers using a three-point scale; NIPS scores were higher in the heel stick group, maternal anxiety was higher before the procedure, and the study concluded venepuncture is less painful than heel stick for routine blood sampling in newborns.

**3. Ogawa S, Ogihara T, Fujiwara E, et al. (2005).** *Venepuncture is preferable to heel lance for blood sampling in term neonates.* Archives of Disease in Childhood – Fetal and Neonatal Edition, 90(5):F432–F436.
This was a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 100 healthy, full-term newborns being screened for inborn errors of metabolism, allocated to one of four groups (venepuncture or heel lance, with sucrose or water), with pain assessed using the neonatal facial coding system and crying; without sucrose, facial coding scores were higher in the heel lance group than the venepuncture group.

**4. Larsson BA, Tannfeldt G, Lagercrantz H, Olsson GL (1998).** *Alleviation of the pain of venepuncture in neonates.* Acta Paediatrica, 87(7):774–779.
This controlled, randomized, double-blinded study allocated 120 neonates to EMLA cream or placebo applied to the dorsum of the hand before venepuncture, following on from the group's earlier finding that venepuncture was more effective and less painful than heel-lancing.

**5. Saththasivam P, Umadevan D, Ramli N, Voralu K, Naing NN, Ilias MI, et al. (2009).** *Venipuncture versus heel prick for blood glucose monitoring in neonates.* Singapore Medical Journal, 50(10):1004–1007.
Identified as a randomized comparison of venipuncture versus heel prick for neonatal blood glucose sampling (referenced as an included trial in the Cochrane review's search).

**6. Logan PW (1999).** *Venepuncture versus heel prick for the collection of the Newborn Screening Test.* Australian Journal of Advanced Nursing, 17(1):30–36.
A randomized comparison of the two sampling methods for the newborn screening test.
