When diving into the primary trial data, the evidence strongly points to **venepuncture** being the superior method for blood sampling in full-term neonates—provided it is performed by a skilled practitioner.

Here is a breakdown of what the primary clinical trials reveal about the two methods:

### Pain Outcomes

Across multiple randomized controlled trials, venepuncture consistently generates significantly lower pain scores than heel lancing. Using validated neonatal pain measures like the **Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS)**, the **Premature Infant Pain Profile (PIPP)**, and the **Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS)**, infants subjected to venepuncture demonstrate less distress, shorter crying times, and lower physiological pain markers (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022; Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005; Shah et al., 1997).

Even when controlling for pain relief interventions (like oral sucrose), heel pricks have been shown to remain significantly more painful at the 60-second mark compared to venepuncture (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022).

### Efficacy and Repeat Punctures

Venepuncture is generally shown to be more effective at obtaining an adequate blood sample on the first attempt (Larsson et al., 1998).

* **Heel lancing** frequently requires prolonged squeezing of the heel to expel blood, which extends the procedure time and exacerbates the infant's pain. It also carries a higher risk of yielding a hemolyzed or clotted sample, often necessitating a second skin puncture (Shah et al., 1997).
* **Venepuncture** requires significantly less total procedure time to yield an adequate sample volume, dramatically reducing the need for repeated needle sticks (Larsson et al., 1998).

### The Primary Caveat

If venepuncture is so clearly superior, why isn't it the universal standard? The primary barrier is **technical expertise**. Heel lances are ubiquitous in neonatal units precisely because they require minimal training and can be performed easily by general staff. Venepuncture, however, must be performed by specially trained phlebotomists or nurses to achieve these faster, less painful outcomes.
