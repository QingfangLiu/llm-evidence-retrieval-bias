It is completely natural to feel anxious about your newborn having a blood test. Watching your baby go through an uncomfortable procedure is one of the hardest parts of those early days.

To answer your question directly: when performed by a skilled professional, **venepuncture (drawing blood from a vein, usually on the back of the hand) is consistently shown to be less painful and more effective than a heel lance for full-term newborns.**

Here is what individual, randomized controlled trials (primary studies) have found when comparing the two methods:

## Less Pain and Crying

In clinical trials where the two techniques were compared side-by-side, full-term newborns who underwent venepuncture cried for significantly less time and scored much lower on standardized neonatal pain assessments than those who received a heel prick (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005). More recent data corroborates that under controlled conditions, a heel prick triggers a significantly higher pain response than a vein draw (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022).

## Faster Procedures and Fewer Attempts

While a heel lance seems like a smaller poke, actually collecting the required amount of blood often requires a nurse to repeatedly squeeze and massage the baby's heel. This squeezing is uncomfortable and prolongs the procedure. Venepuncture is generally much faster and requires fewer repeat punctures to collect an adequate blood sample, particularly for standard newborn metabolic screening tests (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005).

## The Catch: Practitioner Skill

The main caveat to these findings is that venepuncture requires a practitioner who is highly experienced in finding and drawing from tiny neonatal veins. If the staff isn't confident in neonatal venepuncture, a heel prick might be their safer default. Additionally, if only a single, tiny drop of blood is needed—such as for a quick bedside blood sugar check—a heel prick is still commonly preferred.

> **Expert tip:** No matter which method the clinical team uses, you can advocate for pain relief. Providing your baby with a few drops of a sweet sucrose (sugar water) solution or allowing you to breastfeed them about two minutes before the procedure significantly reduces their pain response (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022; Ogawa, 2005).
