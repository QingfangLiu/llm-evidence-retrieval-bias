It is completely understandable to be worried about your newborn baby experiencing pain. When it comes to taking a blood sample from a full-term infant, it is natural to assume that a quick heel prick is the gentler option. However, research points in the opposite direction.

Individual clinical trials have consistently found that **venepuncture** (drawing blood from a vein with a needle) is actually **less painful and more effective** than a **heel lance** (pricking the heel) for full-term babies.

Here is what the primary research studies tell us about why this is the case:

### 1. Venepuncture Causes Less Pain

Several randomized controlled trials have compared how babies react to the two procedures by measuring pain scores—which track facial expressions like squeezing eyes shut or furrowing the brow—and crying duration.

* **Lower Pain Scores:** Research has repeatedly found that pain scores are significantly lower for babies who undergo venepuncture compared to those who have a heel lance (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005).
* **Less Crying:** Babies are much less likely to cry, and cry for a shorter duration, when blood is taken from a vein. A recent study confirmed that even when babies are given a sweet sucrose solution to soothe them, the heel prick is still significantly more painful than venepuncture (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022).
* **Parent Perception:** In one study, mothers who watched the procedures initially felt more anxious about the needle. However, after seeing the procedure performed, they rated venepuncture as less painful for their baby than the heel prick (Shah et al., 1997).

### 2. Venepuncture is Faster and Needs Fewer "Do-Overs"

One of the biggest issues with a heel lance is getting enough blood for the required tests.

* **First-Try Success:** With a heel prick, a nurse often has to repeatedly squeeze or "milk" the baby's heel to get enough drops of blood, which prolongs the discomfort. If the blood clots or stops flowing, the baby must be pricked a second time.
* **Speed:** Venepuncture is generally much quicker and usually provides enough blood on the very first try without the need for repeated squeezing (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005).

### Why do hospitals still use heel lances?

You might wonder why heel lances are still so common if they are more painful. The main reason is practicality: a heel prick is technically easier to perform and requires less specialized training. Venepuncture needs to be done by a nurse or phlebotomist who is specifically trained in finding tiny newborn veins.

If your hospital has staff experienced in newborn blood draws, venepuncture is generally the kinder, faster option for a full-term baby.
