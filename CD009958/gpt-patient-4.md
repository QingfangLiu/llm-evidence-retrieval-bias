## Bottom line

The primary studies do **not establish one universally best repositioning interval**. Their most consistent finding is that **turning every 2 hours is not clearly superior to every 3 or 4 hours when an effective pressure-redistributing mattress is used and the turns are actually performed**. Six-hour intervals are less well supported, particularly when combined with direct 90° side-lying.

For body position, a **supported 30° lateral tilt** appears biomechanically preferable to lying directly at 90° on the hip, but clinical trials have produced mixed results. “30° lateral tilt” means tilting the entire body slightly to one side with pillows or wedges—not raising the head of the bed.

### Frequency interpretation

Based on these studies:

* **Two hours is not an evidence-proven universal requirement.**
* **Three to four hours may be adequate for many moderate/high-risk adults using high-density foam or powered pressure-redistributing mattresses**, provided positioning is performed consistently and the skin is monitored.
* These results should **not automatically be applied to people with severe hemodynamic instability, very poor perfusion, active skin discoloration, current pressure injuries, inability to tolerate a position, or pressure from medical devices**.
* The evidence does not establish that six-hour intervals are safe for everyone. The clearest trial favoring a shorter interval compared **3-hour 30° tilting with 6-hour 90° side-lying**, so interval and position effects could not be separated.

## Primary physiological studies supporting position choice

These measured pressure, oxygenation, or blood flow rather than actual pressure-injury incidence:

* **Colin et al., 1996:** In 20 healthy volunteers, direct 90° lateral positioning markedly impaired trochanteric skin oxygenation, whereas 30° lateral positioning did not. ([PubMed][12])
* **Källman et al., 2015:** In 25 nursing-home residents, interface pressure was higher in flat supine and 90° lateral positions than in corresponding 30° tilted positions; tissue perfusion was most favorable in the 30° supine-tilt position. ([PubMed][13])
* **Peterson et al., 2008:** In 15 volunteers, raising the head of the bed to 30° or higher increased peak sacral pressure; pressure-loaded area increased further at 45° and above. ([PubMed][14])
* **Peterson et al., 2010:** Standard lateral turns did not consistently unload every high-pressure area. Wedge positioning left larger persistently loaded areas than pillow-supported turning in this small healthy-volunteer study. ([PubMed][15])
* **Kim & Shin, 2021:** In 64 healthy adults observed for two hours, 30° lateral tilt produced lower peak pressure than 90° side-lying. The authors’ suggested timing thresholds were derived from interface pressure—not from actual ulcer development—so they should not be treated as clinical proof. ([PubMed][16])

## Practical interpretation of the positions

The most defensible positioning approach from these primary studies is:

1. **Alternate pressure-bearing areas rather than remaining continuously supine or on one side.**
2. When side-lying, use a **supported approximately 30° lateral tilt** rather than placing the person directly at 90° on the greater trochanter, when medically and physically feasible.
3. Use a sequence such as **left 30° tilt → back/supine or slight opposite tilt → right 30° tilt**, rather than repeatedly returning pressure to the same exact area.
4. Keep the **head of the bed at the lowest angle that is medically safe**, because steeper elevation increases sacral pressure and shear. This must be balanced against breathing, aspiration, reflux, feeding and cardiac considerations.
5. A position is only useful when it genuinely unloads the threatened area. Pressure mapping studies show that an apparent “turn” may leave part of the sacral or buttock area continuously loaded.

## What this means for you

The research supports asking your clinician for an **individualized repositioning prescription**, rather than assuming everyone requires exactly two-hour turns. The plan should specify the mattress or overlay, permitted positions, lateral angle, maximum time in each position, heel and device offloading, skin-check frequency, and what findings require immediately shortening the interval.

Do not lengthen your current interval without your treating team’s agreement—especially if you have persistent redness or purple discoloration, reduced circulation or sensation, incontinence/moisture exposure, vasopressor use, poor nutrition, a current wound, or medical devices pressing against the skin.
