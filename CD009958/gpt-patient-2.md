## Bottom line

The primary studies do **not establish one universally best turning interval**.

In nursing-home residents at moderate or high risk who were lying on modern high-density foam mattresses, turning every **3 or 4 hours did not produce more pressure injuries than every 2 hours** during relatively short trials. A similar ICU trial on alternating-pressure air mattresses found no significant benefit from 2-hourly rather than 4-hourly turning. These findings **do not prove that every at-risk patient can safely wait 4 hours**: people at very severe risk, with unstable circulation, existing deep tissue damage, poorly fitting medical devices, or inadequate mattresses were often excluded or underrepresented. ([PubMed][1])

For body position, the most studied alternative is a **30-degree lateral tilt** rather than lying fully on the hip at 90 degrees. One trial favored a 30-degree regimen, but it also used more frequent repositioning, so the benefit cannot confidently be attributed to the angle alone. Another small trial found no significant advantage. ([PubMed][2])

## Primary studies of repositioning frequency

### Defloor et al., 2005

**Population:** 838 older nursing-home residents followed for 28 days.

**Comparison:**

* Every 2 or 3 hours on a standard mattress
* Every 4 or 6 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress
* Usual care

**Results:** Stage 2 or worse injuries occurred in approximately **3%** of residents turned every 4 hours on viscoelastic foam, versus roughly **14%–24%** in the other groups. Rates of nonblanchable redness were similar.

**Interpretation:** The favorable result involved a **combination of 4-hour turning and a pressure-reducing mattress**. Because mattress and frequency changed together, the study does not prove that 4-hour turning alone was responsible. ([PubMed][3])

### Bergstrom et al., TURN Study, 2014/2015

**Population:** 942 nursing-home residents aged 65 or older, at moderate or high risk but without pressure injuries; all were on high-density foam mattresses.

**Comparison:** Repositioning every 2, 3 or 4 hours for three weeks.

**Results:** Pressure injuries occurred in:

* 2-hour group: **8/321, 2.49%**
* 3-hour group: **2/326, 0.61%**
* 4-hour group: **9/295, 3.05%**

The difference was not statistically significant, and no deep stage 3 or 4 injuries occurred.

**Interpretation:** The trial found no detectable advantage to 2-hourly turning in this particular nursing-home population. It excluded some extremely high-risk residents and should not be generalized to patients without comparable mattresses. ([PubMed][1])

### Manzano et al., 2014

**Population:** 329 mechanically ventilated ICU patients using alternating-pressure air mattresses.

**Comparison:** Turning every 2 hours versus every 4 hours.

**Results:** Stage 2 or worse pressure injuries occurred in **10.3% versus 13.4%**, respectively; the difference was not statistically significant. Device-related adverse events were more frequent with 2-hourly turning—**47.9% versus 36.6%**—and it required about twice as much nursing time.

**Interpretation:** On an alternating-pressure surface, the study did not show that 2-hourly repositioning prevented more injuries than 4-hourly repositioning. The wide confidence interval means a clinically important difference could not be completely excluded. ([PubMed][4])

### Vanderwee et al., 2007

**Population:** 235 Belgian elder-care residents who already had nonblanchable redness, corresponding to early stage 1 injury.

**Comparison:**

* Two hours in a lateral position followed by four hours supine
* Repositioning every four hours
  All participants used pressure-reducing mattresses.

**Results:** Progression to stage 2–4 injury occurred in **16.4% versus 21.2%**; this was not statistically significant.

**Interpretation:** A more frequent asymmetric schedule did not clearly reduce progression. Because participants already had early skin damage, this is more a study of preventing worsening than of preventing the first injury. ([PubMed][5])

### Pickham et al., LS-HAPI Trial, 2018

**Population:** 1,312 ICU patients.

**Comparison:** Standard turning reminders versus a wearable sensor providing staff with real-time feedback about whether prescribed turns had actually occurred.

**Results:** Hospital-acquired pressure injuries occurred in **5/659, 0.7%**, with sensor feedback versus **15/653, 2.3%**, with standard care. Repositioning compliance was approximately **67% versus 54%**.

**Interpretation:** This study did not compare specific time intervals. It suggests that **reliably completing the prescribed repositioning plan** may matter as much as selecting a nominal interval. ([PubMed][6])

### Yap et al., TEAM-UP Trial, 2022

**Population:** 992 analyzed residents in nine nursing homes; participants were at risk but not in the most severe risk category and used 7-inch high-density foam mattresses.

**Comparison:** Facility-wide 2-, 3- or 4-hour repositioning schedules, supported by wearable monitoring and staff cues, for four weeks.

**Results:** No resident developed a new pressure injury in any of the three groups. Turning compliance was highest in the 4-hour facilities.

**Interpretation:** The absence of injuries is encouraging but makes the intervals difficult to compare statistically. Because there was no simultaneous usual-care control and only three facilities per interval, this does not prove that 4-hour turning is equivalent for all patients. ([PMC][7])

## Primary studies of body position

### Moore, Cowman and Conroy, 2011

**Population:** 213 older hospital and long-term-care residents in Ireland.

**Comparison:**

* **30-degree lateral tilt every 3 hours overnight**
* Routine **90-degree lateral/supine repositioning every 6 hours overnight**

**Results:** Pressure injuries developed in **3/99, 3%**, with the 30-degree regimen versus **13/114, 11%**, with routine positioning. All were stage 1 or 2.

**Interpretation:** The combined 30-degree/every-3-hour regimen performed better. However, both **angle and frequency differed**, so the trial cannot determine whether the improvement came from the smaller tilt, the shorter interval, or both. ([PubMed][2])

### Young, 2004

**Population:** 46 hospitalized adults, with 39 completing the study.

**Comparison:** A 30-degree tilt versus routine 90-degree lateral and supine positions on a similar 2- to 3-hour schedule.

**Results:** Five participants developed nonblanchable redness, with no statistically significant reduction from the 30-degree position. Approximately **78% had difficulty adopting or maintaining the 30-degree tilt**.

**Interpretation:** This very small, approximately one-night study did not confirm that the 30-degree tilt was superior and identified practical positioning difficulties. ([PubMed][8])

### Ghezeljeh et al., 2017

**Population:** 120 mechanically ventilated ICU patients.

**Comparison:** Head-of-bed elevation at 30 degrees, 45 degrees or routine positioning for three days. Participants were also lifted/repositioned approximately every two hours.

**Results:** No group developed a pressure injury, and pressure-injury risk scores did not differ significantly.

**Interpretation:** The study was short, pressure injury was a secondary outcome, and zero events occurred. It therefore cannot establish whether 30 or 45 degrees is safer for the sacrum. ([ResearchGate][9])

## Specialized operating-room studies

These studies concern patients who cannot be conventionally turned during surgery, so they should not be directly applied to routine bed care.

### Jin et al., 2023/2024

**Population:** 200 adults undergoing surgery while supine.

**Comparison:** Standard prevention versus alternating the operating table **15 degrees left and right every hour for five minutes**; both groups received a sacral silicone dressing.

**Results:** Intraoperative pressure injuries occurred in **2% versus 10%**.

**Interpretation:** Very small, frequent weight shifts may help during long operations, but this does not establish an interval for nursing-home, hospital-ward or home care. ([PubMed][10])

### Micromovement during prone lumbar surgery, 2024

**Population:** 277 overweight adults undergoing posterior lumbar surgery in the prone position.

**Comparison:** Scheduled small positional movements versus silicone foam dressing prevention.

**Results:** Intraoperative pressure injuries occurred in **4/137, 2.9%**, with micromovement versus **12/140, 8.6%**, with the dressing strategy.

**Interpretation:** Small movements reduced injuries in this highly specialized prone surgical setting. It does not mean that prone positioning itself is preferable for routine prevention. ([PMC][11])

## What these studies support—and do not support

A reasonable reading of the primary evidence is:

* **Two-hourly turning is not proven superior in every setting.** Three- or four-hour intervals have performed similarly in selected adults using high-specification foam or alternating-pressure mattresses.
* **The support surface is part of the intervention.** A trial result involving a modern pressure-redistributing mattress should not be applied to an ordinary mattress.
* **A 30-degree lateral tilt is a reasonable pressure-distributing position, but its clinical superiority remains uncertain.** The strongest favorable study changed both the tilt angle and the repositioning interval.
* **No one position should be maintained continuously.** Supine, lateral and medically necessary prone positions transfer pressure to different anatomical areas rather than eliminating it.
* The trials provide little direct evidence for people at **extreme risk**, those receiving palliative care, patients with severe circulatory instability, individuals with spinal cord injury managing prolonged wheelchair sitting, or unsupervised patients at home.

For an individual patient, the turning interval should therefore be treated as a **personalized care prescription**, taking into account the mattress, ability to make independent weight shifts, skin response, medical devices, pain, breathing needs and whether certain positions are medically unsafe. A useful question for your clinician or wound-care nurse is: “Given my mattress and risk factors, what is my documented repositioning schedule, and what findings would require it to be shortened?”
