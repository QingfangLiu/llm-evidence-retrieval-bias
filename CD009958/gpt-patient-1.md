## Bottom line from individual primary studies

No single repositioning interval has been shown to be best for every at-risk adult. In several randomized studies, **repositioning every 3 or 4 hours was not clearly worse than every 2 hours when patients were using an appropriate pressure-redistributing mattress**. This does **not** establish that 4-hour turning is safe for every patient, particularly someone with very high risk, poor perfusion, rapidly changing illness, an existing pressure injury, or an inadequate support surface. ([PubMed][1])

For body position, the **approximately 30° lateral tilt** is the most directly studied preventive position. Results conflict: one trial favored a 30°-tilt regimen, whereas a smaller trial found no advantage and considerable difficulty maintaining the position. Importantly, the favorable trial also repositioned patients more frequently, so it could not separate the effect of angle from the effect of frequency. ([PubMed][2])

## What these studies imply for a patient

The studies support an **individualized schedule rather than an automatic assumption that every adult must be turned exactly every two hours**. For some moderate- or high-risk nursing-home residents on verified high-density foam mattresses, 3- or 4-hour schedules performed similarly to 2-hour schedules. Evidence is much less reassuring for very-high-risk patients, people without an appropriate mattress, or those whose skin is already changing. ([PubMed][1])

For position, a practical studied sequence is **small lateral tilts—approximately 30°, alternating left, back and right—rather than prolonged full 90° side-lying**, provided the person can tolerate and maintain the position. However, the direct trial evidence is insufficient to declare 30° definitively superior. Heels and medical-device contact points may also require separate offloading; changing torso position alone may not unload them.

Do not extend your current turning interval independently. A clinician or wound-care nurse should set the schedule based on your mobility, sensation, circulation, mattress, skin response, medical devices and whether redness disappears after pressure is relieved. Persistent nonblanching redness, purple/maroon discoloration, warmth, firmness, blistering or skin breakdown warrants prompt clinical assessment.
