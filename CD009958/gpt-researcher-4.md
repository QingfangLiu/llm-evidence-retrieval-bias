## Bottom line from the primary studies

There is **no well-supported universal repositioning interval** for all at-risk adults.

* In nursing-home residents on high-density foam mattresses, repositioning every **3 or 4 hours was not demonstrably worse than every 2 hours**.
* In mechanically ventilated ICU patients on alternating-pressure air mattresses, **every 2 hours did not significantly reduce pressure injuries compared with every 4 hours** and increased nursing workload and device-related complications.
* Evidence for the **30° lateral tilt** is mixed: one small trial was negative, while another favored it but simultaneously changed the turning interval from every 6 to every 3 hours.
* **Prolonged prone positioning** in severe ARDS appears to increase pressure-injury risk relative to supine positioning, although it may be required for its respiratory and survival benefits.
* In operating rooms, **small periodic table tilts (“micromovements”) and curvilinear supine positioning** have produced promising reductions in intraoperative pressure injuries, but these findings come from specialized, mostly single-center trials.

The studies below are individual primary studies. I have not included systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee documents, editorials, or narrative reviews.

### Frequency synthesis

The most defensible conclusion is that **a rigid every-2-hour rule is not supported as universally superior**. Three- or four-hour intervals may be reasonable research candidates for selected adults using effective pressure-redistributing surfaces, but the studies do not establish safety for patients with severe risk, poor perfusion, rapidly changing physiology, inadequate support surfaces, or existing tissue damage. ([PubMed][3])

The support surface is an important effect modifier: the Defloor trial should not be interpreted simply as “4 hours is best,” because the apparently favorable 4-hour group was also assigned a viscoelastic mattress. ([PubMed][1])

### Position synthesis

**No single static body position has been shown to be universally best.**

The 30° lateral tilt has a stronger biomechanical rationale than full 90° lateral positioning, but direct clinical results are inconsistent. The positive Moore trial tested an entire regimen—30° tilt **and** more frequent turning—rather than angle alone. ([PubMed][6])

For severe ARDS, prolonged prone positioning increases pressure-injury risk relative to supine positioning. That finding should be used to intensify site-specific prevention during proning—not to withhold a potentially lifesaving respiratory treatment. ([Springer][8])

The clearest recent positive positioning signal is in the operating room: **brief periodic micromovements or curvilinear redistribution appear more effective than leaving patients continuously fixed in standard supine or prone surgical positions**. ([ScienceDirect][14])

## 3. Primary mechanistic studies—useful, but not pressure-injury incidence evidence

These studies help explain possible positioning effects but should not be pooled with trials measuring new injuries.

* **Colin et al., 1996**, 20 healthy volunteers: 90° lateral positioning substantially impaired transcutaneous tissue oxygenation, while a 30° lateral tilt did not produce the same impairment. This supports avoiding prolonged direct loading over the greater trochanter, but it did not measure pressure injuries. ([PubMed][15])
* **Peterson et al., 2008**, 15 healthy volunteers: peak sacral interface pressure increased as head-of-bed elevation rose, becoming significantly elevated at approximately 30° and above; the area exposed to higher pressures increased particularly at ≥45°. Again, this was a laboratory surrogate study. ([PubMed][16])
* **Kim & Shin, 2021**, randomized positioning experiment in 64 healthy adults: interface pressure during a 30° lateral tilt remained below the investigators’ 60-mmHg threshold longer than during a 90° lateral position. The authors proposed position-specific intervals, but these were derived from pressure thresholds rather than observed pressure injuries. ([MDPI][17])

## Evidence-synthesis interpretation

For a clinical-effectiveness synthesis, I would keep the evidence in separate nodes:

1. **Pure or nearly pure interval comparisons:** Bergstrom and Manzano; the same-surface contrasts within Defloor.
2. **Interval-plus-technology or adherence interventions:** TEAM-UP and Pickham.
3. **Position-plus-frequency regimen:** Moore.
4. **Static angle comparisons:** Young and Ghezeljeh.
5. **Prone versus supine in ARDS:** Girard.
6. **Operating-room pressure redistribution:** Guo, Jin, Zhang, and Malekara.
7. **Surrogate physiology/interface-pressure experiments:** Colin, Peterson, and Kim.

Pooling across those categories without stratification would obscure major differences in support surface, baseline risk, setting, outcome definition, follow-up duration, and whether the intervention changed frequency, angle, mattress, dressings, or monitoring simultaneously.
