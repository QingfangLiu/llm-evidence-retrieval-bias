It is smart to be proactive about this—pressure injuries are much easier to prevent than they are to treat. Because you specifically asked for primary studies rather than clinical summaries, I have pulled individual clinical trials and cohort studies that directly test repositioning frequencies and surface types.

Here is what the primary research shows about protecting your skin.

## How Often to Reposition

Historically, the standard rule of thumb across nursing has been to turn every two hours. Primary research confirms this works, but also shows that your **mattress type** heavily dictates how often you actually need to move.

* **On standard mattresses (2 hours):** A prospective study looking at intensive care unit patients found that establishing a dedicated team to turn patients every two hours strictly and consistently led to a dramatic decrease in the incidence of new pressure ulcers (Still et al., 2013). If you are on a standard bed, the two-hour rule is still a heavily supported standard.
* **On pressure-redistributing foam (up to 4 hours):** If you are using a specialized mattress, you may be able to sleep longer without turning. A large, multi-site randomized clinical trial (the TURN study) compared repositioning patients every 2, 3, or 4 hours on high-density foam mattresses. They found no significant difference in pressure ulcer incidence between the three schedules, meaning turning every 4 hours on a high-density foam mattress was just as effective and safe as turning every 2 hours (Bergstrom et al., 2013).
* **The mattress impact:** To highlight how much the surface matters, an observational cohort study compared patients on standard mattresses versus those on viscoelastic, temperature-sensitive memory foam. The use of the pressure-redistributing foam mattress was associated with an 88% reduced risk of developing pressure injuries compared to the standard mattress (Bai et al., 2020).

## Best Body Positions

The entire goal of repositioning is to remove weight from "bony prominences"—the areas where your bones are close to the skin, which easily pinch off blood supply (like your tailbone, hips, heels, and shoulder blades).

While the studies above focused heavily on frequency and support surfaces, they employed standard offloading positions in their protocols. The most universally supported position in clinical research is the **30-degree lateral tilt**.

1. **Avoid the 90-degree side-lying position:** Lying completely on your side (at a strict 90-degree angle) is generally avoided because it places your entire body weight directly onto your hip bone (the greater trochanter), creating a high risk for a new sore there.
2. **Use the 30-degree tilt:** Instead, you want to be tilted about 30 degrees off your back. You can achieve this by placing pillows or a foam wedge under one side of your back and hips. This shifts the pressure off your sacrum (tailbone) but spreads your weight across your fleshy back muscles rather than directly onto your hip bone.
3. **Float the heels:** Your heels are highly susceptible to skin breakdown. Place a pillow length-wise under your calves so that your heels "float" completely in the air off the mattress.
