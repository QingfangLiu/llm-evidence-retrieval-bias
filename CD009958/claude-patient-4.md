Here are individual primary studies (RCTs and clinical trials) addressing repositioning frequency and positioning for pressure injury prevention — no reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines included.

## Repositioning frequency

**Bergstrom et al., 2013 — "Turning for Ulcer ReductioN" (TURN) Study**, *J Am Geriatr Soc*
Multisite RCT in 27 nursing homes (20 US, 7 Canadian; N=942 residents ≥65 at moderate/high risk on the Braden Scale) on high-density foam mattresses. Residents were randomly allocated to 2-, 3-, or 4-hour turning schedules for 3 weeks, with weekly risk and skin assessments by blinded assessors. Results supported turning at 2, 3, or 4 hours when residents are on high-density foam mattresses, with turning at 3- and 4-hour intervals performing no worse than the standard 2-hour practice. Overall pressure ulcer incidence was low (2.02%), and only superficial (stage 1–2) ulcers developed.

**Yap et al., 2022 — TEAM-UP Trial**, *Advances in Skin & Wound Care* (RCT results paper; the 2018 BMC Geriatrics paper is the study protocol)
A pragmatic cluster RCT across nine nursing homes randomly assigned to 2-, 3-, or 4-hour repositioning intervals, comparing baseline (12-month) data with a 4-week intervention period. Intervention residents had no current pressure injuries, Braden scores ≥10, and used 7-inch high-density foam mattresses, with a wireless sensor system cueing staff on repositioning needs and compliance.

**Defloor, De Bacquer & Grypdonck, 2005**, *International Journal of Nursing Studies*
RCT of 838 geriatric nursing home patients comparing four preventive regimens: frequent turning alone (2- or 3-hourly) versus a pressure-reducing (viscoelastic foam) mattress combined with less-frequent turning (4- or 6-hourly). Turning every 4 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress produced significantly fewer pressure ulcers than turning every 2 or 3 hours on a standard mattress.

**Vanderwee, Grypdonck, De Bacquer & Defloor, 2007**, *Journal of Advanced Nursing*
Two-arm RCT in 16 Belgian nursing homes (n=235) comparing an alternating schedule (2 hours lateral, 4 hours supine) against uniform 4-hourly repositioning, all on pressure-reducing mattresses. Ulcer incidence was 16.4% in the alternating group versus 21.2% in the 4-hourly group, a non-significant difference (P=0.40); severity, location, and time-to-onset were also similar. The authors concluded more frequent repositioning on a pressure-reducing mattress does not necessarily reduce ulcer incidence.

**Manzano et al., 2014 — ICU repositioning trial**, *Intensive Care Medicine*
Compared repositioning every 2 versus 4 hours for preventing pressure ulcers in mechanically ventilated ICU patients on alternating-pressure air mattresses. (This is a completed RCT, distinct from the 2021 Colombian protocol paper found earlier, which describes a trial not yet reporting results.)

## Body position

**Moore, Cowman & Conroy, 2011**, *Journal of Clinical Nursing*
Pragmatic, multicentre, cluster RCT across 12 long-term elderly care sites (Ireland) comparing two regimens: the experimental group (n=99) was repositioned three-hourly at night using the 30° tilt, while the control group (n=114) received routine care — six-hourly repositioning using 90° lateral rotation. Repositioning at 30° every three hours at night reduced pressure ulcer incidence compared with the usual six-hourly 90° care.

**Young, 2004**, *Journal of Tissue Viability*
Compared the 30° tilt position against standard 90° lateral/supine positioning for reducing non-blanching erythema in hospital inpatients. Both intervention and control groups were tilted left side, back, right side, and back, with the 90° group repositioned 2- to 3-hourly overnight (versus 6-hourly in Moore 2011).

**Zhou et al., 2014**
Compared a prone position against standard supine positioning; the intervention group was positioned prone for 10 hours, then supine for 4–6 hours, then returned to prone.

## A note on interpreting these together

Across the frequency trials (Bergstrom/TURN, Defloor, Vanderwee, TEAM-UP), a consistent theme is that on modern pressure-redistributing (high-density foam or viscoelastic) mattresses, extending intervals beyond the traditional 2 hours did not clearly increase ulcer incidence, and in Defloor's trial, 4-hourly turning on foam outperformed more frequent turning on a standard mattress. The positioning trials (Moore, Young) suggest the 30° tilt may outperform full 90° lateral rotation, independent of frequency. None of these were large enough or consistent enough to settle an "optimal" universal protocol — many report wide confidence intervals or non-significant differences — so if you're weighing this for your own care plan, it's worth discussing these specific findings with your nurse or care team, since your support surface, mobility level, and skin risk factors matter as much as the interval itself.
