When looking strictly at individual primary studies—and bypassing systematic reviews or general practice guidelines—the evidence points to a more nuanced approach than the traditional "turn every two hours" rule. The optimal frequency and physical position depend heavily on the acuity of the patient and the support surface they are on.

## Frequency of Repositioning

**The 2-Hour Interval in High-Acuity Settings**
In critical care, strict 2-hour turning remains a highly effective intervention. A primary interventional study conducted in a Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) demonstrated that when a dedicated "Turn Team" was implemented to ensure all hemodynamically stable patients were repositioned exactly every 2 hours, the incidence of pressure ulcers dropped dramatically from 42 to 12 cases over the study periods (Still et al., 2013).

**Extended Intervals on Advanced Surfaces**
Conversely, for lower-acuity populations (such as residents in long-term care) who are placed on high-density foam mattresses, the 2-hour rule can be safely extended. The TEAM-UP pragmatic cluster randomized controlled trial evaluated 2-, 3-, and 4-hour repositioning intervals for adults at risk of pressure injuries. The trial found that extending the interval to 3 or 4 hours did not result in an increase in pressure injuries. Notably, nursing staff compliance was significantly higher for the 4-hour schedule (95%) compared to the 2-hour schedule (80%), indicating that less frequent turning reduces care burden without compromising tissue integrity, provided the patient is on a viable foam mattress (Yap et al., 2022).

## Optimal Body Positions

**The 30° Tilt vs. 90° Lateral Rotation**
When selecting a position, primary clinical trials strongly favor the 30° tilt over a full 90° lateral turn. A cluster randomized controlled trial compared a 30° tilt (repositioning every 3 hours at night) against routine care using a 90° lateral rotation (repositioning every 6 hours). The incidence of pressure ulcers was significantly lower in the 30° tilt group (3%) compared to the 90° rotation group (11%) (Moore et al., 2011).

The 30° angle is highly effective because it successfully lifts the sacrum and coccyx off the mattress without placing the full weight of the body directly onto the greater trochanter (the hip bone), which is a common failure point of a full 90° side-lying position.
