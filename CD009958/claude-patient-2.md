Here are individual primary studies (RCTs and a prospective cohort) on repositioning frequency and positioning for pressure injury/ulcer prevention — no reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, or opinion pieces included.

## Repositioning frequency

**1. Bergstrom et al., "Turning for Ulcer ReductioN" (TURN Study), *J Am Geriatr Soc* 2013**
Multisite RCT in 27 US/Canadian nursing homes (N=942 residents, moderate/high risk on Braden Scale, all on high-density foam mattresses). Residents were randomized to repositioning every 2, 3, or 4 hours for 3 weeks. Participants were randomly allocated using risk stratification to a repositioning schedule of 2, 3, or 4 hours for 3 weeks, with blinded assessors evaluating skin weekly. There was no difference in pressure ulcer incidence across the 2-, 3-, or 4-hour groups, and overall incidence was very low (~2%, all Stage 1–2).

**2. Yap et al., "TEAM-UP Trial Results," *Adv Skin Wound Care* 2022**
Pragmatic cluster RCT in 9 nursing homes, each assigned facility-wide to a 2-, 3-, or 4-hour repositioning interval for 4 weeks, using a wearable sensor system to cue and document turning. The objective was to investigate the clinical effectiveness of three nursing-home-wide repositioning intervals (2, 3, or 4 hours) without compromising pressure injury incidence over 4 weeks, using baseline data compared against the intervention period.

**3. Defloor, De Bacquer & Grypdonck, *Int J Nurs Stud* 2005**
RCT in 838 geriatric nursing home patients over 28 days, comparing four regimens: turning every 2 or 3 hours on a standard mattress vs. turning every 4 or 6 hours combined with a viscoelastic foam mattress. Subjects were 838 geriatric nursing home patients; during 28 days, four turning schemes were used, including turning every 2 hours on a standard mattress compared with less-frequent turning paired with a pressure-redistributing mattress. Longer intervals combined with a good support surface performed comparably to frequent turning on a standard mattress; incidence in the standard-mattress/frequent-turning comparison arms ranged higher than the foam-mattress arms.

**4. Vanderwee, Grypdonck, De Bacquer & Defloor, *J Adv Nurs* 2007**
Two-arm RCT in 16 Belgian nursing homes testing an unequal-interval schedule (2 hours lateral + 4 hours supine, i.e., effectively less time laterally) against straight 4-hourly turning, all on pressure-redistributing mattresses. The study investigated whether repositioning patients on a pressure-reducing mattress alternately for 2 hours in a lateral position and 4 hours in a supine position reduces pressure ulcer incidence compared with repositioning every 4 hours, based on the rationale that interface pressure is higher in the lateral than the supine position.

## Positioning technique (30° tilt vs. 90° lateral/supine)

**5. Moore, Cowman & Conroy, *J Clin Nurs* 2011**
Pragmatic, multicentre, cluster-RCT across 12 Irish long-term care sites (N=213). The experimental group (n=99) was repositioned three-hourly at night using the 30° tilt; the control group (n=114) received routine care with six-hourly repositioning using the 90° lateral rotation, followed for four weeks. Three patients (3%) in the 30°-tilt group and 13 patients (11%) in the 90°-lateral group developed a pressure ulcer (p=0.035) — a large relative reduction favoring the 30° tilt.

**6. Young, "The 30° tilt position vs the 90° lateral and supine positions...", *J Tissue Viability* 2004**
Smaller RCT in a hospital inpatient population (30° tilt group n=23 vs. 90° lateral/supine group n=23), primary outcome non-blanching erythema (early pressure damage) rather than full ulcers. No subject developed pressure damage with visible epidermal breaks; damage was restricted to non-blanching erythema, seen in five of the 39 subjects who completed the study.

## Ventilated/critically ill patients (repositioning in ICU)

**7. Winkelman & Chiang, "Manual turns in patients receiving mechanical ventilation," *Crit Care Nurse* 2010**
A primary (non-review) study specifically describing turning practices and physiologic tolerance of manual repositioning in mechanically ventilated ICU patients — relevant if you want ICU-specific data rather than nursing-home data (referenced among primary repositioning-frequency studies in a 2021 scoping review's reference list).

### Practical synthesis of these primary studies (not from a review — just summarizing the trials above)
- Across the higher-quality trials that used a good support surface (high-density foam or viscoelastic mattress), extending the interval to 3 or even 4 hours did **not** increase ulcer incidence compared with 2-hourly turning (Bergstrom 2013; Defloor 2005; TEAM-UP 2022) — suggesting mattress quality interacts strongly with tolerable turning interval.
- When repositioning technique was varied instead of frequency, the **30° tilt** outperformed the traditional 90° lateral position in the one larger trial that measured actual ulcer incidence (Moore 2011), though a smaller trial using only early skin changes as the outcome found less separation between techniques (Young 2004).
- None of these individual trials is large or long enough on its own to settle the question definitively — sample sizes are modest (starting around n=46 up to n=942), follow-up windows are short (3–4 weeks in most cases), and event rates are low. That's exactly the kind of situation where a systematic review would normally be used to pool results — but since you asked me to stick to primary studies, keep in mind you're seeing the individual, unpooled data points rather than a synthesized estimate.
