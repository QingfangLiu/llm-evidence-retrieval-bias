## Bottom line

**Yes.** Individual randomized trials show that iron supplementation—especially in frequent donors or donors with low ferritin—can substantially improve ferritin/iron stores and accelerate hemoglobin recovery. Several studies also found fewer low-hemoglobin deferrals or fewer donors with hemoglobin below donation thresholds.

The distinction matters: blood centers generally determine donation eligibility using **hemoglobin**, whereas **ferritin** more directly reflects iron stores. Therefore, some studies measured actual deferral, while others measured low hemoglobin or ferritin as predictors of future deferral.

## Practical study in donors already deferred

**Bryant et al., “Iron replacement therapy in the routine management of blood donors.” Transfusion. 2012;52:1566-1575.** This was a prospective clinical program rather than an RCT. It evaluated 1,236 donors deferred for hemoglobin below 12.5 g/dL and 400 nondeferred controls. Iron-deficient donors were given ferrous sulfate 325 mg daily. Iron-related laboratory abnormalities generally normalized even while donors continued donating; adherence was 68%, and gastrointestinal adverse effects occurred in 21%. ([PMC][12])

An important finding was that low donation-screen hemoglobin was **not always caused by iron deficiency**: the program identified 13 donors with serious medical conditions, including gastrointestinal bleeding. Thus, persistent or unexplained low hemoglobin should not automatically be treated as donation-related iron deficiency without appropriate evaluation. ([PMC][12])

## Overall interpretation

The individual studies support three conclusions:

1. **Iron supplementation consistently improves ferritin and iron stores.**
2. **It accelerates hemoglobin recovery**, often from several months to several weeks.
3. **It can reduce low-hemoglobin deferral**, with the most direct evidence coming from the Gordeuk and STRIDE trials.

The studied regimens ranged widely—from approximately **19–60 mg elemental iron daily for 56–60 days** to shorter, higher-dose regimens. The trials do not establish that one regimen is appropriate for every donor, and unexplained low hemoglobin should be evaluated rather than presumed to be caused by blood donation alone.
