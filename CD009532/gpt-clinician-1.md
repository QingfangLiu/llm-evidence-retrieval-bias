## Bottom line

**Yes.** Individual donor studies consistently show that oral iron accelerates restoration of ferritin and hemoglobin after donation. Several studies also show fewer low-hemoglobin deferrals or restoration of donation eligibility. The most persuasive evidence comes from randomized trials, although modern trials more often measured ferritin, hemoglobin recovery, or study-defined low hemoglobin rather than actual donation deferral.

I searched for primary studies of allogeneic whole-blood donors through July 2026 and excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions, protocols, and autologous-donation studies.

## Additional supportive intervention study

**Pittori et al., 2011** enrolled 116 premenopausal female donors with ferritin ≤10 ng/mL but acceptable Hb into a 16-week supplementation program, with retrospective untreated controls. Among program completers, mean ferritin increased from **7.1 to 25.2 ng/mL**, no participant developed anemia, and 60% completed the program and donated again. Interpretation is limited by 40% noncompletion, the nonrandomized comparison, and the simultaneous extension of donation intervals. ([PubMed][12])

## Clinical interpretation

The evidence supports three separate conclusions:

1. **Iron stores:** Oral iron clearly restores ferritin faster than diet or waiting alone. The effect is especially large in donors starting with ferritin ≤26–30 ng/mL.

2. **Hemoglobin:** Supplementation accelerates postdonation Hb recovery and helps maintain Hb during repeated donations. Doses as low as approximately **19–40 mg elemental iron daily** were effective in several donor trials, although higher doses and shorter courses were also studied.

3. **Donation eligibility:** Actual deferral outcomes are less commonly measured in recent RCTs, but the available direct studies support an effect. The strongest controlled example found deferral rates of **8% with iron versus 36% with placebo**; a recent uncontrolled study found that **60% of initially deferred, iron-deficient first-time donors became eligible after 60 days**.

A key caveat is that **low Hb should not automatically be attributed to donation-related iron loss**. In Bryant et al., evaluation of deferred donors identified 13 serious medical conditions, including eight cases of gastrointestinal bleeding. This is particularly relevant for male, postmenopausal, infrequent, or first-time donors, in whom empiric supplementation should not replace investigation of the cause of anemia or iron deficiency. ([PMC][4])
