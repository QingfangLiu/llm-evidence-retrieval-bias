All are individual primary studies (RCTs or a prospective cohort/interventional study) — no reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines.

## Randomized controlled trials

**Kiss JE, et al. "Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA. 2015;313(6):575–583.** (REDS-III network)
Multicenter RCT testing oral iron after donation in donors with depleted iron stores; a foundational trial showing supplementation speeds ferritin/iron recovery after donation.

**Cable RG, et al. "Effect of iron supplementation on iron stores and total body iron after whole blood donation." Transfusion. 2016;56(7):1856–1862.**
215 donors randomized to iron supplementation, examining effects on iron stores and total body iron following whole blood donation.

**Radtke H, et al. "Daily doses of 20 mg of elemental iron compensate for iron loss in regular blood donors: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study." Transfusion. 2004;44(10):1427–1432.**
Early placebo-controlled RCT in regular donors testing a low daily oral iron dose.

**Waldvogel S, et al. "Clinical evaluation of iron treatment efficiency among non-anemic but iron-deficient female blood donors: a randomized controlled trial." BMC Medicine. 2012;10:8.**
154 female donors with iron deficiency without anemia were randomized to four weeks of oral ferrous sulfate versus placebo, showing significant increases in hemoglobin and ferritin with treatment.

**Gordeuk V, et al. "Prevention of iron deficiency with carbonyl iron in female blood donors." Transfusion. 1990;30(3):239–245.**
Older RCT testing carbonyl iron for prevention of iron deficiency in repeat female donors.

**Marks DC, et al. "An 8-week course of 45 mg of carbonyl iron daily reduces iron deficiency in female whole blood donors aged 18 to 45 years: results of a prospective randomized controlled trial." Transfusion. 2014;54(3pt2):780–788.**
Prospective RCT in younger female donors specifically targeting iron deficiency reduction.

**Schotten N, et al. "High-dose intravenous versus oral iron in blood donors with iron deficiency: The IronWoMan randomized, controlled clinical trial." Clinical Nutrition. 2019.**
176 donors with iron deficiency (ferritin ≤30 ng/mL) were randomized to a single dose of IV ferric carboxymaltose or oral iron fumarate; transferrin saturation and ferritin were significantly higher in the intravenous group, while hemoglobin levels were comparable between groups.

**Hod EA, et al. "A randomized trial of blood donor iron repletion on red cell quality for transfusion and donor cognition and well-being." Blood. 2022;140(25):2730–2739.**
Hemoglobin increased by a mean 1.4 g/dL and ferritin increased by 66 μg/L in the iron repletion group compared with placebo; in post hoc analyses, female subjects in the placebo group were more likely to be deferred from donation for not meeting minimum hemoglobin/hematocrit criteria than those receiving iron repletion.

**Karregat J, et al. "Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals (FORTE): a double-blind, randomised, controlled trial." Lancet Haematology. 2025;12:e694–e704.**
830 donors with ferritin ≤30 µg/L were randomized to six arms testing 0, 30, or 60 mg elemental iron, daily or alternate-day, for 56 days. All iron supplementation groups had significantly lower odds of iron deficiency at 56 days versus placebo, and daily 60 mg emerged as the strategy that significantly reduced odds of low ferritin without added side effects or adherence problems.

**Mathew (or the IV-iron-fatigue trial). "The effects of intravenous iron supplementation on fatigue and general health in non-anemic blood donors with iron deficiency: a randomized placebo-controlled superiority trial."**
203 donors were randomized to a single IV dose of 800 mg iron-carboxymaltose and 202 to placebo; ferritin rose by a mean 114.2 µg/L and hemoglobin by 5.7 g/L in the iron group versus placebo, though fatigue scores did not differ.

## Related, borderline-relevant RCT (interval-based, not supplement-based — include with caution)

**FIND'EM trial** (Lancet 2024;404:31–43) — stepped-wedge cluster-RCT of ferritin-guided **donation interval** extension (not supplementation) — hemoglobin-based deferrals decreased significantly for male donors but not female donors. Useful as a comparator/alternative-strategy study but doesn't test supplementation directly.

---

A note on scope: several of these trials (Waldvogel, the IV-iron-fatigue trial, IronWoMan) primarily measured fatigue/quality-of-life as their main endpoint, with iron-stores/deferral-relevant outcomes reported secondarily — but all report hemoglobin/ferritin data directly relevant to your question. The Kiss 2015 JAMA trial and the 2025 FORTE trial are probably your two most directly on-point references, since both were purpose-designed around iron-store recovery and deferral risk in donor populations.
