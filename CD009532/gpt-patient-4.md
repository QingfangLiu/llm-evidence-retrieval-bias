## Bottom line

Yes. Individual primary studies—mostly randomized trials—show that iron supplementation after donation can raise ferritin, accelerate hemoglobin recovery, and reduce the likelihood of having hemoglobin below a donation threshold. The most direct trial evidence found an actual repeat-donation deferral rate of **8% with iron versus 36% with placebo**. Other studies measured eligibility or low hemoglobin rather than an attempted donation.

Blood centers often refer informally to a low “iron level,” but the predonation fingerstick generally measures **hemoglobin**. **Ferritin** is the more direct measure of stored iron; a donor can have depleted ferritin while still passing the hemoglobin screen.

## Primary randomized studies

### 1. Karregat et al., 2025 — FORTE trial

**Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals.** *Lancet Haematology.*

This double-blind trial included **830 whole-blood donors with ferritin ≤30 µg/L**. Participants received placebo, 30 mg elemental iron, or 60 mg elemental iron, either daily or on alternate days for 56 days.

At day 56, iron deficiency was present in:

* Approximately **49%–51%** of placebo recipients
* **10%** with 30 mg daily
* **7%** with 30 mg on alternate days
* **1%** with 60 mg daily
* **4%** with 60 mg on alternate days

All iron regimens also reduced the odds of low hemoglobin; 60 mg daily produced the largest improvement in low ferritin. This trial measured low hemoglobin, not actual deferral at a repeat donation. ([PubMed][1])

### 2. Kiss et al., 2015 — HEIRS trial

**Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial.** *JAMA.*

The trial randomized **215 donors** to 37.5 mg elemental iron daily or no iron for 24 weeks after one whole-blood donation.

Among donors with baseline ferritin ≤26 ng/mL:

* Time to 80% hemoglobin recovery was **32 days with iron versus 158 days without iron**.
* Recovery of baseline ferritin took a median of **21 days with iron versus more than 168 days without iron**.

Across all donors, iron-store recovery took a median of **76 days with iron** and longer than 168 days without iron; **67% of unsupplemented donors had not recovered their iron stores by 168 days**. At 56 days, 92% of iron-treated donors versus 73% of controls had venous hemoglobin above 12.5 g/dL, although this was not an actual repeat-donation deferral outcome. ([JAMA Network][2])

A secondary analysis of the same randomized trial found that total-body iron rose substantially with supplementation, particularly during the first eight weeks, whereas donors receiving no iron remained below their predonation total-body iron at 24 weeks. ([PubMed][3])

### 3. Gordeuk et al., 1990

**Prevention of iron deficiency with carbonyl iron in female blood donors.** *Transfusion.*

In this double-blind trial, **99 women aged 18–40** received carbonyl iron 100 mg daily or placebo for 56 days following donation.

At day 56, iron replacement was sufficient to replace the donation-related loss in **85% with iron versus 29% with placebo**. At the attempted repeat donation, deferral occurred in **8% of the iron group versus 36% of the placebo group**, providing the most direct evidence that supplementation can reduce inability to donate. ([Wiley Online Library][4])

### 4. Marks et al., 2014

**An 8-week course of 45 mg of carbonyl iron daily reduces iron deficiency in female whole blood donors aged 18 to 45 years.** *Transfusion.*

This double-blind trial randomized **282 female donors** to 45 mg elemental carbonyl iron daily or placebo for eight weeks.

At week 12:

* Mean ferritin was **17.0 versus 10.6 ng/mL**.
* Iron deficiency was present in **51.9% versus 80.5%**.
* Mean hemoglobin was **134.6 versus 130.0 g/L**.

The iron group had significantly better eligibility to donate based on capillary hemoglobin, although gastrointestinal symptoms were more frequent. ([Wiley Online Library][5])

### 5. Mast et al., 2016 — STRIDE trial

**A randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled trial of education and iron supplementation for mitigation of iron deficiency in regular blood donors.** *Transfusion.*

A total of **692 frequent donors** were assigned to 19 mg iron, 38 mg iron, placebo tablets, ferritin-result letters, or control letters over two years. Tablet groups received 60 daily doses after each donation.

Longitudinal analyses showed improved iron status in both iron-dose groups and worsening status in placebo and uninformed controls. Iron recipients had an approximately **0.6 g/dL net hemoglobin increase relative to controls**. Among completers, the prevalence of ferritin below 12 or 26 ng/mL declined by more than half in the iron groups. Interpretation is limited by higher withdrawal from tablet groups—39% versus 7% in letter groups. ([PubMed][6])

### 6. Radtke et al., 2004

**Daily doses of 20 mg of elemental iron compensate for iron loss in regular blood donors.** *Transfusion.*

This double-blind trial included **526 regular donors** receiving 20 mg, 40 mg, or placebo daily for six months while men donated four times and women three times.

Both 20 mg and 40 mg daily compensated for donation-related iron loss. In men, supplementation maintained iron balance but did not create a positive storage balance; in women, it restored iron balance and increased storage iron. Gastrointestinal adverse effects occurred in approximately 12% of iron recipients and were only slightly more frequent than with placebo. ([PubMed][7])

### 7. Maghsudlu et al., 2008

**Short-term ferrous sulfate supplementation in female blood donors.** *Transfusion.*

The study randomized **412 women** to a seven-day course of ferrous sulfate after each donation or placebo, with possible repeat donations at four-month intervals.

Ferritin and other iron measures declined significantly in the placebo group but were largely maintained in the supplemented group. The placebo group had **3.6 times the risk of iron deficiency** compared with the iron group. Hemoglobin decreased in both groups, but the decline was substantially greater with placebo. ([Wiley Online Library][8])

### 8. Waldvogel et al., 2012

**Clinical evaluation of iron treatment efficiency among non-anemic but iron-deficient female blood donors.** *BMC Medicine.*

The trial enrolled **154 women with ferritin ≤30 ng/mL but no anemia** and randomized them to 80 mg elemental iron daily or placebo for four weeks.

Compared with placebo, iron increased:

* Hemoglobin by approximately **5.2 g/L**
* Ferritin by approximately **14.8 ng/mL**

It did not improve fatigue, aerobic capacity, mood, or quality of life over four weeks. Thus, the laboratory benefit was clear, but this study did not demonstrate a symptomatic benefit or directly evaluate donation deferral. ([PubMed][9])

### 9. Dara et al., 2016

**A Randomized Control Study to Evaluate Effects of Short-term Oral Iron Supplementation in Regular Voluntary Blood Donors.** *Indian Journal of Hematology and Blood Transfusion.*

Two hundred regular donors received approximately 98.6 mg elemental iron daily or placebo for 21 days after donation.

At one month, hemoglobin increased from 12.9 to 13.8 g/dL in the iron group, while it decreased from 13.3 to 12.8 g/dL with placebo. Depleted ferritin fell from 35% to 11.1% immediately after treatment but returned to 32.8% by the next donation, suggesting that this three-week regimen was insufficient to maintain iron stores for three months. Attrition was high, at approximately 37%. ([PMC][10])

## Primary real-world studies in donors with low hemoglobin

**Bryant et al., 2012 — Iron Replacement Therapy in the Routine Management of Blood Donors.** This prospective program evaluated 1,236 donors deferred for hemoglobin below 12.5 g/dL and 400 nondeferred controls. Iron-depleted or iron-deficient donors were given one 325-mg ferrous sulfate tablet daily. Iron laboratory values normalized even while donors continued donating; compliance was 68%, and 21% reported gastrointestinal adverse effects. Importantly, low hemoglobin was not always caused by donation-related iron deficiency: the evaluation identified 13 serious medical conditions, including eight cases of gastrointestinal bleeding. ([PMC][11])

**Magnussen et al., 2008 — The effect of a standardized protocol for iron supplementation to blood donors low in hemoglobin concentration.** This prospective study enrolled 879 donors whose hemoglobin was at or below the donation threshold. A ferritin-guided supplementation protocol increased hemoglobin and ferritin among returning donors; after two years, 79% of women and 85% of men remained active donors. Because there was no untreated randomized control group, it provides supportive implementation evidence rather than a definitive treatment comparison. ([Wiley Online Library][12])

## Overall interpretation from these individual studies

The primary evidence supports three conclusions:

1. **Iron supplements reliably improve ferritin and iron stores**, especially in donors who begin with low ferritin.
2. **They accelerate hemoglobin recovery and can reduce low-hemoglobin ineligibility.** Actual lower repeat-donation deferral was directly demonstrated by Gordeuk et al.; several newer trials demonstrated better hemoglobin eligibility or fewer donors below a prespecified threshold.
3. **Regimen duration matters.** Very short courses can produce temporary improvement without maintaining ferritin until the next donation, while regimens lasting roughly 8 weeks or repeated after each donation generally produced more durable effects.

Because low hemoglobin is not synonymous with iron deficiency—and can occasionally indicate blood loss or another condition—confirmation with a CBC and ferritin and clinical evaluation is particularly important when the result is unexpected, recurrent, or occurs in a male or postmenopausal donor. The studies do not establish a single universally appropriate dose for every donor.
