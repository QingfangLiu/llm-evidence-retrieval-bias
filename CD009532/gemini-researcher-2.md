Based on the data from primary clinical trials, iron supplementation is highly effective at accelerating the recovery of both hemoglobin and iron stores, which in turn significantly reduces the likelihood of donor deferral.

Here is a synthesis of the key primary studies investigating these outcomes.

## Improving Iron Stores and Hemoglobin Recovery

When blood is donated, the body loses between 200–250 mg of iron, which often takes longer than the standard 8-week inter-donation interval to naturally replenish.

* **Accelerated Hemoglobin Recovery:** A landmark randomized clinical trial demonstrated that daily low-dose oral iron supplementation (37.5 mg) significantly shortened the time required to recover 80% of the hemoglobin lost after donation. In donors with low ferritin, recovery took about 32 days with iron compared to 158 days without it (Kiss et al., 2015).
* **Restoring Ferritin Levels:** The same trial found that donors taking iron recovered their baseline iron stores in a median of 76 days. Conversely, 67% of donors who did not take iron failed to recover their iron stores even after 168 days (Kiss et al., 2015).
* **Mitigating Chronic Deficiency:** The STRIDE (Strategies to Reduce Iron Deficiency) trial evaluated regular donors over a two-year period. It found that providing daily iron pills (at either 19 mg or 38 mg doses) successfully mitigated the progressive worsening of iron deficiency that otherwise occurs in frequent donors without intervention (Mast et al., 2016).

## Reducing Deferral Rates and Increasing Donations

By accelerating hemoglobin recovery, iron supplementation has a direct operational benefit for blood collection centers and donor retention.

* **Decreased Hemoglobin Deferrals:** An operational analysis of the STRIDE trial data found that donors randomized to receive iron pills had significantly decreased deferral rates for low hemoglobin compared to control groups (Cable et al., 2017).
* **Increased Donation Frequency:** Because donors in the supplementation cohort were deferred less frequently, they ultimately achieved a higher rate of successful red blood cell donations over the study period (Cable et al., 2017).

## Secondary Effects: Donor Well-Being and Transfusion Quality

Researchers have also explored whether replacing lost iron improves the physical well-being of donors and the quality of the donated blood.

* **Symptom Reduction:** The IronWoMan RCT analyzed iron-deficient blood donors given either oral or intravenous iron. Alongside significant improvements in ferritin and transferrin saturation, the study recorded significant decreases in the severity of fatigue, restless legs syndrome (RLS), and sleep disturbances after 8 to 12 weeks of supplementation (Macher et al., 2020).
* **Cognition and Cell Quality:** A randomized trial examining intravenous iron repletion confirmed the expected hematological improvements but found no clinically relevant changes in the donors' self-reported well-being, fluid cognition, or the post-transfusion recovery quality of the stored red blood cells (Hod et al., 2022).
