Here are primary studies I found on iron supplementation in blood donors — effects on iron stores (ferritin/hemoglobin) and on donation deferral. I've excluded reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines as requested.

**Randomized controlled trials**

- **Kiss JE, Brambilla D, Glynn SA, et al. Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial.** JAMA. 2015;313(6):575–583. (REDS-III/HEIRS trial) — Frequent donors randomized to ferrous gluconate vs. placebo after donation; iron supplementation accelerated hemoglobin and ferritin recovery.

- **Cable RG, Glynn SA, Kiss JE, et al. Effect of iron supplementation on iron stores and total body iron after whole blood donation.** Transfusion. 2016;56(8):2005–2012. — Secondary/companion analysis of the same 215-donor trial (ferrous gluconate 37.5 mg/day vs. no iron for 24 weeks), using quantitative total-body-iron measures.

- **Waldvogel-Abramowski S, et al. Clinical evaluation of iron treatment efficiency among non-anemic but iron-deficient female blood donors: a randomized controlled trial.** Transfusion. 2012;52(7):1566–1575. — 154 iron-deficient, non-anemic female donors randomized to 4 weeks ferrous sulfate vs. placebo; outcomes included fatigue, aerobic capacity, and mood.

- **Maghsudlu M, Nasizadeh S, Toogeh G, et al. Short-term ferrous sulfate supplementation in female blood donors.** Transfusion. 2008;48(7):1394–1397. — 412 female donors randomized to different ferrous sulfate doses; assessed effect on ferritin/hemoglobin recovery and repeat-donation deferral.

- **Karregat JHM, Meulenbeld A, Quee FA, et al. Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals (FORTE): a double-blind, randomised, controlled trial.** Lancet Haematol. 2025;12(9):e694–e704 (also posted as preprint on medRxiv, 2025). — 6-arm double-blind RCT (0/30/60 mg elemental iron, daily vs. alternate-day) in donors with ferritin ≤30 µg/L; assessed ferritin/hemoglobin recovery, GI side effects, and donor return.

- **Spencer BR, et al. A randomized trial of blood donor iron repletion on red cell quality for transfusion and donor cognition and well-being.** Blood. 2022;140(25):2730–2739. — IV ferric carboxymaltose 800 mg vs. placebo in iron-deficient frequent donors; measured ferritin/hemoglobin recovery plus RBC quality and cognitive/well-being outcomes.

- **Radtke H, et al. (IronWoMan RCT)** — Prospective randomized trial comparing IV ferric carboxymaltose vs. oral ferrous fumarate in iron-deficient whole-blood/apheresis donors. Primary results plus a secondary analysis: **"The Effect of Parenteral or Oral Iron Supplementation on Fatigue, Sleep, Quality of Life and Restless Legs Syndrome in Iron-Deficient Blood Donors: A Secondary Analysis of the IronWoMan RCT"** (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01787526).

**Prospective/observational studies (non-randomized, primary data)**

- **Bryant BJ, Yau YY, Arceo SM, et al. Iron replacement therapy in the routine management of blood donors.** Transfusion. 2012;52(7):1566–1575 *(note: check exact page range — closely indexed alongside the Waldvogel-Abramowski trial above; verify against the original)*. — 39-month prospective study of 1,236 donors deferred for low hemoglobin plus 400 non-deferred controls, given ferrous sulfate (or gluconate if intolerant) and followed for iron-status and deferral recovery.

- **Bialkowski W, Kiss JE, Wright DJ, et al. Estimates of total body iron indicate 19 mg and 38 mg oral iron are equivalent for the mitigation of iron deficiency in individuals experiencing repeated phlebotomy.** Am J Hematol. 2017;92(9):851–857. — Dose-comparison analysis nested in a blood-donor iron-repletion study cohort.
