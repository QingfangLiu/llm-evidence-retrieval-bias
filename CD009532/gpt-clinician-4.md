## Bottom line

**Yes.** Individual primary studies consistently show that iron supplementation after blood donation can replenish ferritin/iron stores and accelerate hemoglobin recovery. Several trials also directly demonstrate improved donation eligibility or fewer low-hemoglobin deferrals. The clearest applicability is in repeat donors, menstruating donors, and donors with ferritin approximately ≤15–30 µg/L.

Low-hemoglobin deferral and iron deficiency are not interchangeable: in one large prospective program, many low-Hb donors were iron deficient, but substantial iron deficiency was also found among donors who passed Hb screening. ([PMC][1])

## Trials directly reporting deferral or inability to redonate

### Gordeuk et al., 1990

This double-blind trial randomized 99 women aged 18–40 to carbonyl iron 100 mg nightly or placebo for 56 days after phlebotomy. Estimated absorbed iron was sufficient to replace the donation loss in **85% with iron versus 29% with placebo**. At the repeat donation visit, deferral occurred in **8% versus 36%**, respectively. This is one of the most direct primary demonstrations that post-donation iron can reduce inability to donate. ([Wiley Online Library][10])

### Marks et al., 2014

The Australian trial above specifically evaluated Hb-based eligibility. Higher Hb after eight weeks of 45 mg/day carbonyl iron translated into significantly better eligibility at the 12-week return visit. The abstract does not provide the exact eligibility percentages, but it reports a statistically significant improvement. ([Wiley Online Library][6])

### FORTE, 2025

FORTE measured low Hb rather than an actual collection-site deferral decision. Nevertheless, every active oral-iron schedule significantly reduced the odds of being below the study's sex-specific Hb threshold at 56 days, supporting a lower likelihood of Hb-related ineligibility. ([PubMed][2])

## Studies specifically involving deferred donors or real-world programs

### Bryant et al., 2012

This was a prospective, nonrandomized NIH donor-center program involving 1,236 donors deferred for fingerstick Hb <12.5 g/dL and 400 nondeferred controls. Deferred donors received ferrous sulfate 325 mg—approximately 65 mg elemental iron—daily for 60 days, with another course after subsequent donations.

Iron-depleted and iron-deficient donors showed normalization of ferritin, transferrin saturation, Hb, MCV, and related indices despite continued donation. Compliance was 68%, and gastrointestinal effects occurred in 21%. The program **did not lower the proportion of visits resulting in low-Hb deferral**, but participating donors returned more often and had more productive donations per year. Because treatment was not randomized and return behavior differed, this is weaker causal evidence than the RCTs. ([PMC][1])

The study also found potentially serious alternative causes of low Hb or iron deficiency, including gastrointestinal bleeding. Clinically, this argues against automatically attributing marked or persistent iron deficiency—particularly in men or nonmenstruating donors—solely to donation. ([PMC][1])

### Alvarez-Ossorio et al., 2000

In a ferritin-monitoring program involving 632 long-term and 171 first-time donors, donors with ferritin <15 µg/L were given iron. Hb rose rapidly among supplemented frequent donors with initially low Hb, and the investigators reported no subsequent donor deferral in that group. The absence of randomization and incomplete intervention details limit certainty, but it provides supportive implementation evidence. ([PubMed][11])

## Intravenous-iron primary studies

These establish biologic efficacy but are less directly applicable to routine donor supplementation.

* **IronWoMan trial:** 176 iron-deficient whole-blood or platelet donors were randomized to 1,000 mg IV ferric carboxymaltose or oral iron fumarate. At 8–12 weeks, ferritin was **105 versus 25 ng/mL** and transferrin saturation **27% versus 21%**, favoring IV iron; Hb was similar. Oral iron was still considered an acceptable alternative. ([ScienceDirect][12])
* **ISUB trial:** 405 nonanemic repeat donors with ferritin ≤50 µg/L received 800 mg IV ferric carboxymaltose or placebo. IV iron increased ferritin by an estimated **114.2 µg/L** and Hb by **5.7 g/L**, but did not improve fatigue or general well-being. ([PMC][13])
* **First-time female donor trial:** 85 women received 1,000 mg IV iron isomaltoside or placebo. Hb before the second and third donations and multiple iron indices were significantly better with IV iron. ([PubMed][14])

## Clinical interpretation

The individual studies support three conclusions:

1. **Iron stores:** Oral iron reliably raises or preserves ferritin and prevents the prolonged negative iron balance that otherwise follows donation.
2. **Hemoglobin:** Supplementation accelerates Hb recovery, often by several weeks or months, particularly when predonation ferritin is low.
3. **Donation eligibility:** Direct trials show fewer repeat deferrals or improved Hb eligibility, although many modern trials use low Hb as a surrogate rather than documenting an actual attempted-donation deferral.

Regimens with demonstrated benefit range from roughly **19–45 mg elemental iron daily for 56–60 days after donation** to higher targeted treatment doses in donors with established deficiency. The newer FORTE data indicate that 30 mg daily or alternate-day is effective, while 60 mg daily produces greater restoration of iron stores. Gastrointestinal intolerance and adherence remain the principal practical limitations.
