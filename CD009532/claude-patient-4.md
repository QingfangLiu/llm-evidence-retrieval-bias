Here are individual primary studies (randomized controlled trials) specifically testing whether iron supplementation raises iron stores and/or reduces low-iron/low-hemoglobin deferral in blood donors. I've excluded reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines as requested.

**1. Kiss JE, et al. "Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA. 2015;313(6):575-583.** (the HEIRS trial)
The Lancet Haematology summary notes this trial showed that iron supplementation for 56 days following blood donation can lead to full recovery of ferritin to pre-donation levels. This is the largest and most-cited U.S. RCT of low-dose oral iron (37.5 mg/day) vs. placebo after donation, tracking ferritin and hemoglobin recovery.

**2. Mast AE, et al. "A randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled trial of education and iron supplementation for mitigation of iron deficiency in regular blood donors."** (Transfusion, 2016; PMID 26813849)
Tested whether an educational intervention plus iron supplementation (vs. placebo) reduced iron deficiency in donors who donate frequently.

**3. Karregat JHM, et al. "Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals (FORTE): a double-blind, randomised, controlled trial." Lancet Haematol. 2025;12:e694-e704.**
Donors with ferritin ≤30 µg/L were randomized to placebo or to 30 mg or 60 mg of elemental iron, taken daily or on alternate days, for 56 days. The trial concluded that in regular donors with low ferritin, iron supplementation—60 mg taken daily, in particular—effectively mitigates iron deficiency, low ferritin, and low haemoglobin, and is an effective alternative or supplement to extended donation intervals.

**4. Spekman MLC, et al. "A randomized trial of blood donor iron repletion on red cell quality for transfusion and donor cognition and well-being." Blood. 2022;140(25):2730-.**
Frequent donors with confirmed iron deficiency (ferritin <15 µg/L) were randomized to oral iron repletion vs. placebo. At follow-up, hemoglobin increased by a mean 1.4 g/dL, ferritin increased by 66 μg/L, and zinc protoporphyrin (a marker of iron-deficient erythropoiesis) decreased substantially in the iron repletion group compared with placebo.

**5. von Känel R, et al. "The effects of intravenous iron supplementation on fatigue and general health in non-anemic blood donors with iron deficiency: a randomized placebo-controlled superiority trial." Scientific Reports. 2020;10:14219.** (the ISUB trial)
203 donors received a single IV dose of 800 mg ferric carboxymaltose vs. 202 placebo. While the primary fatigue outcome didn't differ, secondary outcomes showed significantly higher ferritin (mean difference ~114 µg/L) and hemoglobin (mean difference ~5.7 g/L) in the iron-supplemented group, confirming supplementation effectively restores iron stores even if it didn't relieve self-reported fatigue.

**6. Radtke H, et al. "High-dose intravenous versus oral iron in blood donors with iron deficiency: The IronWoMan randomized, controlled clinical trial." Clinical Nutrition. 2021.**
176 blood donors with iron deficiency (ferritin ≤30 ng/mL) were randomized to a single 1000 mg dose of IV ferric carboxymaltose or oral ferrous fumarate given as 100 tablets over 10 weeks, comparing recovery of iron stores between routes of administration.

A note on scope: several of these trials (e.g., ISUB, IronWoMan, FCM-based trials) test IV iron rather than oral supplements, and outcomes are usually ferritin/hemoglobin recovery rather than deferral rates directly — deferral is a downstream consequence of low ferritin/Hb, so these are the closest primary-study proxies.
