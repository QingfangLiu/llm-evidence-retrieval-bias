Here are individual primary studies (RCTs and prospective/cohort intervention studies) evaluating iron supplementation in blood donors, excluding reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines:

**Randomized controlled trials**

1. **Kiss JE, Brambilla D, Glynn SA, et al. "Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA. 2015;313(6):575-583.** — REDS-III trial. Low-dose iron supplementation, compared with no supplementation, reduced the time needed for hemoglobin to recover 80% of its post-donation decrease, in donors with both low and higher ferritin, and the findings supported that iron supplementation reduces the high rate of low-hemoglobin donor deferrals.

2. **Cable RG, Glynn SA, Kiss JE, et al. "Effect of iron supplementation on iron stores and total body iron after whole blood donation." Transfusion. 2016.** — Companion analysis of the same 215-donor cohort randomized to receive daily ferrous gluconate (37.5 mg iron) or no iron for 24 weeks after donation, examining effects on iron stores and total body iron.

3. **Mast AE, et al. "A randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled trial of education and iron supplementation for mitigation of iron deficiency in regular blood donors." Transfusion. 2016.** — Tested education plus iron supplementation against placebo in frequent donors, building on evidence that dietary advice alone is ineffective.

4. **Bialkowski W, Kiss JE, Wright DJ, et al. "Estimates of total body iron indicate 19 mg and 38 mg oral iron are equivalent for the mitigation of iron deficiency in individuals experiencing repeated phlebotomy." Am J Hematol. 2017;92:851-857.** — Dose-comparison trial (STRIDE) in repeat donors/phlebotomy subjects.

5. **Hod EA, Brittenham GM, et al. "A randomized trial of blood donor iron repletion on red cell quality for transfusion and donor cognition and well-being." Blood. 2022;140(25):2730-2739.** — Hemoglobin increased by a mean 1.4 g/dL, ferritin increased by 66 μg/L, and zinc protoporphyrin decreased, in the iron repletion group compared with placebo, and female donors in the placebo group were more likely to be deferred for not meeting minimum hemoglobin/hematocrit criteria than those given iron repletion.

6. **Sweegers MG, et al. (FORTE trial) "Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals (FORTE): a double-blind, randomised, controlled trial." Lancet Haematology. 2025;12:e694-e704** (also posted on medRxiv 2025). — This randomized trial demonstrated that all examined dosages and frequencies of iron supplementation effectively reduced iron deficiency, low ferritin, and low hemoglobin in regular whole blood donors with low ferritin, with daily 60 mg elemental iron emerging as the strategy that significantly reduced odds of low ferritin without added side effects or adherence problems. Donors were enrolled with ferritin levels ≤30 μg/L and randomized to 0, 30, or 60 mg elemental iron, taken daily or on alternate days, for 56 days.

7. **Pittori C, Buser A, Gasser UE, et al. "A pilot iron substitution programme in female blood donors with iron deficiency without anaemia." Vox Sang. 2011;100:303-311.** — Pilot intervention study on oral iron in iron-deficient, non-anemic female donors.

**Prospective (non-randomized) intervention studies**

8. **[Danish study] "The effect of a standardized protocol for iron supplementation to blood donors low in hemoglobin concentration."** — A prospective study of 879 blood donors presenting with capillary hemoglobin at or below the acceptance limit, who received iron tablets, with ferritin, MCV, and hemoglobin tracked; iron stores were low (ferritin ≤30 μg/L) in 80% of women and 48% of men, and hemoglobin and ferritin increased in donors who returned for follow-up.

9. **[Malaysian study] "The effectiveness of iron education through a mobile application on donor return after deferral for low hemoglobin."** — Study of donors deferred for low hemoglobin who were advised to complete a course of iron supplementation, with donor return during 7 months of follow-up as the primary endpoint and hemoglobin increment as a secondary endpoint. Note: the randomized comparison here is an educational app, not iron vs. no iron (all participants received iron), so this speaks more to adherence/return than to supplementation efficacy per se.

**Ongoing/registered trial (not yet a completed primary-outcome publication as far as I could confirm)**

10. A Ghana-based trial (National Blood Supply Group) is comparing low-dose daily iron supplements against nutrition counseling alone in donors deferred for low hemoglobin, with successful return to donate within a year as the outcome measure. Worth tracking for results but flagging that it may still be recruiting/unpublished.
