As an evidence-synthesis researcher, you need the raw primary data—so I have completely filtered out secondary literature like systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines.

Looking exclusively at randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective cohort studies, the primary literature demonstrates that oral iron supplementation significantly accelerates the recovery of iron stores and reduces donor deferrals caused by low hemoglobin. Here is a breakdown of the key primary studies addressing your clinical question.

## 1. Impact on Iron Stores and Hemoglobin Recovery

Primary trials consistently show that without supplementation, standard inter-donation intervals are often insufficient to replenish lost iron.

* **The Hemoglobin Recovery Trial (Kiss et al., 2015):** This major non-blinded RCT evaluated donors given 37.5 mg of elemental iron daily for 24 weeks post-donation versus no treatment. The iron group saw a significantly shortened time to 80% recovery of their post-donation hemoglobin drop. More importantly, median recovery of iron stores took 76 days for those on iron, compared to **over 168 days** for the control group. In fact, 67% of non-supplementing donors failed to recover their iron stores entirely by 168 days.
* **Short-Term Iron Study (Dara et al., 2015):** An RCT in India evaluated regular blood donors assigned to short-term iron therapy versus a placebo. The iron group demonstrated significantly better hemoglobin, hematocrit, and RBC counts. Most notably, serum ferritin levels rebounded effectively, reducing the proportion of donors dropping into iron deficiency compared to the placebo cohort.

## 2. Impact on Donor Deferral Rates

Hemoglobin-based deferral is a massive operational hurdle for blood banks. The primary literature shows that prophylactic or post-deferral iron administration successfully keeps donors eligible.

* **The STRIDE Trial (Mast et al., 2016; Cable et al., 2017):** *Strategies to Reduce Iron Deficiency* (STRIDE) is the most comprehensive recent RCT on this topic. Donors were randomized to receive daily iron pills (19 mg or 38 mg), an educational letter, or a placebo. Mast et al. (2016) demonstrated that daily iron doses effectively mitigated iron deficiency over 60 days. Follow-up operational analysis by Cable et al. (2017) confirmed the ultimate outcome: donors randomized to the iron pill arms had **increased overall red blood cell donations and decreased low-hemoglobin deferrals** compared to control groups.
* **Management of Deferred Donors (Bryant et al., 2011):** In a large prospective study of 1,236 donors who had *already* been deferred for low hemoglobin (<12.5 g/dL), researchers initiated routine administration of ferrous sulfate (65 mg elemental iron). The supplementation successfully normalized iron-related laboratory parameters and allowed these donors to safely resume donating without recurrent deferrals.
* **Historical Nuance (Cable et al., 1988):** It is worth noting an older RCT by Cable et al. (1988), which examined female donors deferred by copper sulfate screening. While 75 mg/day of iron significantly improved their serum ferritin and hemoglobin values by the fifth visit, the researchers noted that it did not significantly increase their immediate *suitability* to donate blood compared to the placebo group. This older finding highlights that while physiological stores improve, deferral prevention also depends heavily on the specific screening thresholds and intervals utilized by the blood center.
