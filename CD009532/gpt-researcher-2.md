## Scope

I treated “unable to donate because of low iron” as either:

1. **Actual low-hemoglobin/hematocrit deferral or failure of donation eligibility**, or
2. **An eligibility-related surrogate**, such as low hemoglobin at the intended next donation.

Ferritin itself is generally not the immediate donation-eligibility test, so studies reporting only ferritin recovery do not directly establish reduced deferral.

I included primary interventional and prospective program studies in allogeneic whole-blood or red-cell donors. Reviews, guidelines, editorials, protocols, autologous-donation studies, and non-donor populations were excluded.

## Interpretation

**Iron stores and hemoglobin recovery:** The modern randomized evidence is highly consistent that oral or IV iron improves ferritin, prevents progressive depletion during repeated donation, and accelerates postdonation hemoglobin recovery. HEIRS, STRIDE, Marks, the Radtke trials, Maghsudlu, and FORTE are particularly informative.

**Actual low-Hb deferral:** The direct evidence is smaller and somewhat mixed. Gordeuk 1990 and STRIDE found substantial reductions in deferral, and Marks found improved donation eligibility. Cable 1988 improved laboratory iron measures but did **not** significantly improve suitability to donate. FORTE strongly reduced laboratory-defined low hemoglobin but did not require an actual donation attempt.

**Independence of evidence:** STRIDE’s 2016 efficacy report and 2017 deferral report are linked analyses of the same 692 participants. Similarly, later HEIRS analyses of total-body iron should not be counted as separate trials from Kiss et al. 2015.

Overall, the best-supported conclusion from the individual studies is that supplementation reliably restores iron stores and usually raises or preserves hemoglobin; the evidence that this translates into fewer low-Hb deferrals is positive but rests on fewer trials and varies by donor population, regimen, and how eligibility was measured.
