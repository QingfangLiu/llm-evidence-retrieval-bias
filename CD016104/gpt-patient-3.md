## Bottom line

I found **11 randomized primary trials** of immune-checkpoint inhibitors in resectable, non-metastatic NSCLC through **July 25, 2026**.

The evidence separates into two different strategies:

1. **Before surgery or around surgery:** checkpoint inhibitor **plus platinum chemotherapy**, compared with the same chemotherapy without immunotherapy.
2. **After surgery only:** checkpoint-inhibitor monotherapy, compared with placebo or observation.

The strongest and most consistent evidence is for adding an immune-checkpoint inhibitor to chemotherapy **before surgery**, with or without continuing it afterward. Adjuvant-only immunotherapy has produced mixed results.

I did **not** find a qualifying randomized trial of **checkpoint-inhibitor monotherapy before surgery** versus placebo or no immunotherapy.

### Understanding the outcomes

* **EFS—event-free survival:** time without progression that prevents surgery, recurrence, or death.
* **DFS—disease-free survival:** time without recurrence or death after surgery.
* **pCR—pathologic complete response:** no viable cancer found in the removed tumor and sampled nodes.
* A hazard ratio, or **HR, below 1.0** favors immunotherapy. A better pCR rate is encouraging, but it is not by itself proof that people live longer.

### What these trials establish—and what they do not

They establish that adding immunotherapy to preoperative chemotherapy can:

* Make complete tumor eradication at surgery substantially more likely.
* Delay recurrence or progression.
* Improve overall survival in at least **CheckMate 816, KEYNOTE-671, NADIM II, and RATIONALE-315**.

However, the perioperative trials tested a **package** of preoperative and postoperative immunotherapy. They do not establish how much additional benefit comes from continuing the drug after surgery. There has not yet been a definitive randomized comparison of **preoperative-only** versus **preoperative plus postoperative** checkpoint inhibition.

## What this means for an older adult

**Age alone was not an exclusion**, but these studies were not designed specifically for frail adults or people aged 75–80 and older. Most participants were relatively fit, generally had performance status 0–1, adequate organ function, and were able to receive platinum chemotherapy. Median ages were usually in the early 60s; ANVIL, with median ages of 66–67, had one of the older trial populations. ([PubMed][7])

Age-subgroup findings are not definitive. In KEYNOTE-671, the overall-survival point estimate was less clearly favorable among patients aged 65 or older, but its confidence interval overlapped the overall result; the subgroup was not powered to prove that older adults benefit differently. CheckMate 816 reported generally consistent final survival effects across examined subgroups. Therefore, evidence supports treatment for **selected fit older adults**, but it does not establish the same benefit-to-harm balance for someone who is frail, has major competing illnesses, or cannot tolerate platinum chemotherapy. ([PMC][12])

Particularly important considerations are:

* **Exact tumor stage:** Most evidence concerns tumors at least 4 cm, node-positive disease, or stage II–III disease. A small, node-negative stage IA tumor is not represented by these trials.
* **Molecular results:** Known sensitizing **EGFR or ALK alterations** were excluded from many modern perioperative trials, so these results should not automatically be applied to those cancers.
* **Chemotherapy fitness:** Every successful randomized preoperative regimen used checkpoint inhibition **with platinum chemotherapy**.
* **Recovery and surgical timing:** Across the large trials, immunotherapy did not generally lower the probability of surgery, but progression, toxicity, operative complications, and rare perioperative deaths still occurred.
* **Immune toxicity:** Immune-mediated problems may involve the lungs, bowel, liver, thyroid, pituitary, adrenal glands, kidneys, skin, heart, or nervous system. Some resolve with steroids; some endocrine effects can require lifelong replacement, and rare events can be fatal.

Severe-event percentages should not be compared mechanically from one trial to another: some investigators reported treatment-related events, others all treatment-emergent events, and the length of therapy ranged from three preoperative cycles to more than a year of perioperative treatment.
