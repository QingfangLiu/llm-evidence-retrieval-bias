## Scope

I found **12 randomized controlled trials with primary publications** comparing a perioperative immune checkpoint inhibitor with a placebo, observation, or the same chemotherapy regimen without an immune checkpoint inhibitor. I excluded single-arm trials, reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, editorials, and opinion pieces.

“Localized” varied across studies—from stage IB tumors at least 4 cm through selected resectable stage IIIB/N2 disease. Most trials required ECOG performance status 0–1 and excluded or separately analyzed tumors with actionable EGFR or ALK alterations.

## Clinical takeaways for older adults

**Neoadjuvant or perioperative chemoimmunotherapy has the most consistent benefit.** Across the larger phase 3 trials, adding a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor reduced the relative risk of an event—progression preventing surgery, recurrence, or death—by roughly 32%–60%. Pathologic complete responses generally increased from approximately 1%–9% with chemotherapy alone to 17%–37% with chemoimmunotherapy. ([PubMed][1])

A statistically significant **overall-survival benefit** has been demonstrated for:

* Neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy in CheckMate 816: 5-year survival 65.4% versus 55.0%; HR 0.72.
* Perioperative pembrolizumab in KEYNOTE-671: 36-month survival 71% versus 64%; HR 0.72.
* Perioperative nivolumab in the smaller phase 2 NADIM II trial: 24-month survival 85.0% versus 63.6%; HR 0.43.

Overall-survival data remain immature or not definitive for several other perioperative phase 3 trials. ([PubMed][2])

**Adjuvant-only therapy is less consistent.** IMpower010 showed a disease-free-survival benefit with atezolizumab, particularly in stage II–IIIA tumors expressing PD-L1 on at least 1% of tumor cells. KEYNOTE-091 showed a more modest DFS benefit with pembrolizumab in the overall population, but not a statistically significant benefit in the prespecified PD-L1 ≥50% group. In contrast, BR.31 found no DFS benefit from durvalumab, and ANVIL found no DFS benefit from nivolumab versus observation. ([PubMed][3])

### What can be said specifically about age?

The trials included substantial numbers of patients aged 65 or older, but they did **not** study a typical geriatric oncology population:

* Eligibility was usually ECOG 0–1, with adequate organ function and fitness for major thoracic surgery and platinum chemotherapy.
* Frailty, cognition, falls, functional dependence, polypharmacy, and formal geriatric-assessment measures were generally not reported.
* Adults aged 75 or older were rarely reported separately.
* TD-FOREKNOW excluded patients older than 70.
* In KEYNOTE-671, EFS benefit appeared generally consistent by age, but the OS point estimate was less clearly favorable among patients aged 65 or older; the subgroup was not powered for an age interaction.
* ANVIL is particularly age-relevant because its median ages were 66 and 67 years, but the adjuvant nivolumab strategy was negative overall. ([PMC][4])

Thus, **chronologic age alone does not appear to eliminate the recurrence/EFS benefit**, but the evidence is insufficient to quantify net benefit in patients aged 75–85, those with frailty, ECOG 2, limited reserve, or major competing mortality risks.

## Practical interpretation

For a fit older surgical candidate without an actionable driver alteration, the evidence favoring **preoperative chemoimmunotherapy—with or without postoperative continuation—is considerably stronger and more consistent than the evidence for starting an ICI only after upfront surgery**.

The additional harms most relevant to an older adult include:

* Increased immune-mediated pneumonitis, thyroid dysfunction, hepatitis, colitis, adrenal or pituitary dysfunction, nephritis, and uncommon but potentially fatal myocarditis.
* More treatment discontinuation and additional clinic/infusion burden, particularly with a full year of postoperative treatment.
* Interaction with reduced pulmonary, cardiac, renal, marrow, and functional reserve.
* The possibility that competing mortality or postoperative decline prevents completion of adjuvant treatment.

The trials generally did not show that adding an ICI made surgery less feasible, but their participants were selected before enrollment as operable and systemically fit. The absolute benefit for a frail 80-year-old therefore cannot be inferred directly from the relative hazard ratios.
