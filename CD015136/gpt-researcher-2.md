## Primary comparative studies located

I treated **“outside hospital”** as care delivered while the patient was at home, in the community, or attending ambulatory care. Studies that recruited participants during admission or included substantial face-to-face care are flagged separately.

This is a high-confidence candidate set through **July 18, 2026**, rather than a PRISMA-complete database search. Every citation below is an individual primary study; no review articles are used as evidence.

## Overall interpretation

The most persuasive evidence of better clinical outcomes comes from **hypertension**, where two sizeable randomized trials found clinically important improvements in BP control when home monitoring was connected to active pharmacist medication management.

Results are more variable elsewhere:

* **Anticoagulation:** remote pharmacist management improved INR control, with the large 2026 valve-replacement trial also reporting fewer bleeding and thromboembolic events.
* **Diabetes:** some intensive or hybrid programs reduced HbA1c, but the large, fully telephone-based ENGAGE-DM trial was null. Adherence and self-care outcomes appear more consistently responsive than HbA1c.
* **Asthma and depression:** improvements were mainly in activation, knowledge, adherence or collaboration; evidence for symptom improvement was limited or based on very small/hybrid trials.
* **Heart failure:** adherence and medication optimization improved, but the strongest hospitalization result currently comes from a nonrandomized study.
* **Cancer pain:** one relatively small, short-duration digital-platform RCT reported better pain scores and adherence.

Thus, telepharmacy does not appear to be a single uniformly effective intervention. Benefits are most apparent when pharmacists can **review objective home data and actively modify or recommend modifications to treatment**, rather than providing education or motivational calls alone.
