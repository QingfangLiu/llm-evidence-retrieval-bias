## Scope used

I treated telepharmacy as pharmacist-delivered care through telephone, video, web portals, apps, SMS, or remotely transmitted home-monitoring data. Patients had to be living in the community or receiving outpatient care, and the study needed a concurrent usual-care/control group.

The tables contain **individual primary research reports only**. Reviews, meta-analyses, protocols, guidelines, editorials, and comments are excluded. This is a targeted, high-confidence inventory rather than a fully reproducible systematic search.

## Study-level interpretation

Across these primary studies, the most consistent benefits are for **medication adherence and blood-pressure control**. Large, well-described hypertension trials by Green and Margolis produced substantial improvements over usual care, while the smaller hypertension studies generally point in the same direction. ([PubMed][2])

The evidence for **HbA1c** is mixed. Poonprapai, Gerber and several smaller recent trials found improvements, whereas MASE, Sarayani, ENGAGE-DM and Odegard found no clear glycemic advantage over usual care. Differences in intervention intensity, uptake, co-interventions and baseline education may explain some of this inconsistency. ([PubMed][11])

For **asthma**, adherence or patient activation can improve, but disease-control effects are inconsistent. For **gout, anticoagulation and transplant medication safety**, the condition-specific trials showed promising improvements, although each evidence base is currently dominated by one trial. ([PubMed][14])

Most reports were designed around surrogate or process outcomes rather than mortality or major complications. The transplant trial is a notable exception because it demonstrated reductions in medication errors, serious adverse events and hospitalization, but it remains a single-center study.
