## Bottom line

Remote pharmacy care **can outperform usual care**, but it depends heavily on the condition and how intensive the service is. The strongest and most consistent primary-study evidence is for **uncontrolled hypertension**, particularly when pharmacists repeatedly review home blood-pressure readings and can adjust treatment. Results for diabetes, asthma, chronic kidney disease, anticoagulation, and heart failure are mixed.

I searched for comparative primary studies in ambulatory, community, primary-care, and home settings. The studies below are individual trials or comparative cohort studies—not reviews, guidelines, opinions, or editorials.

## Interpretation

The most persuasive evidence comes from the two larger hypertension trials by **Green** and **Margolis**. Both used repeated contact, patient-generated home measurements, treatment protocols and active pharmacist medication adjustment—not simply education or reminder calls. ([JAMA Network][1])

For **diabetes**, a substantial multicomponent video-based program improved HbA1c, while pharmacist telephone counseling improved adherence and self-care without clearly improving HbA1c. One isolated, very brief outreach call produced no benefit. ([PMC][13])

For **asthma, CKD and anticoagulant management**, patient engagement or care-process measures sometimes improved, but superiority in disease control or patient outcomes was inconsistent. The heart-failure results are encouraging but require confirmation in a randomized trial. ([PMC][9])

Overall, the primary studies suggest that telepharmacy is most likely to work better than usual care when it is **ongoing, individualized, linked to objective home data, and gives pharmacists authority to actively optimize medication treatment**.
