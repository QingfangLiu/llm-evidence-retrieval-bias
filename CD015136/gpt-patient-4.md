## Bottom line

**Remote pharmacist care is not consistently better than usual care for every chronic condition or outcome.** The strongest and most consistent benefits were seen in **hypertension**, particularly when pharmacists reviewed home blood-pressure readings and could recommend or make medication changes. Telephone or digital programs often improved **medication adherence**, but improvements in laboratory measures such as HbA1c were mixed. Education-only telepharmacy interventions were more likely to produce neutral results. This comparison is an inference from the individual primary studies below—not from review articles. ([JAMA Network][1])

I treated telepharmacy broadly as pharmacist care delivered by telephone, video, secure web messaging, text messaging, or remote monitoring. I included ambulatory/community patients and post-discharge patients receiving ongoing care at home. All studies below are original comparative primary studies.

## Hypertension

1. **Green et al., 2008 — randomized controlled trial, 778 adults**

   *Effectiveness of Home Blood Pressure Monitoring, Web Communication, and Pharmacist Care on Hypertension Control.*

   Patients received usual care, home monitoring plus web tools, or home monitoring plus secure web-based pharmacist management. At 12 months, blood pressure was controlled in **56% with pharmacist care versus 31% with usual care**; adjusted relative risk 1.84.
   **Conclusion:** Better than usual care.
   *JAMA. 2008;299:2857–2867. DOI: 10.1001/jama.299.24.2857; PMID: 18577730.* ([JAMA Network][1])

2. **Margolis et al., 2013 — cluster randomized trial, 450 adults**

   *Effect of Home Blood Pressure Telemonitoring and Pharmacist Management on Blood Pressure Control.*

   Home readings were transmitted to pharmacists who adjusted treatment under a protocol. Blood pressure was controlled at both six and 12 months in **57.2% versus 30.0% with usual care**. Systolic pressure decreased approximately **9.7 mm Hg more** at 12 months. Benefits remained partly evident six months after the intervention ended.
   **Conclusion:** Clearly better than usual care.
   *JAMA. 2013;310:46–56. DOI: 10.1001/jama.2013.6549; PMID: 23821088.* ([PubMed][2])

3. **Lau et al., 2022 — TECHNOMED randomized trial, 120 community-dwelling older adults**

   *Telemonitoring and Protocolized Case Management for Hypertensive Community-Dwelling Older Adults.*

   Pharmacist case management plus home telemonitoring was compared with home monitoring alone. The primary ambulatory-pressure target was **not significantly more likely** to be achieved: adjusted OR 1.48, 95% CI 0.87–2.52. Home measurements were modestly lower, but low systolic readings occurred more frequently with pharmacist management. Recruitment stopped early because of COVID-19.
   **Conclusion:** Neutral for the primary outcome; possible modest benefit with an overtreatment concern.
   *Journal of Hypertension. 2022;40:1702–1712. DOI: 10.1097/HJH.0000000000003202; PMID: 35943099.* ([PubMed][3])

4. **Ibrahim et al., 2022 — randomized trial in 16 UAE community pharmacies, 239 adults**

   *The Impact of Telepharmacy on Hypertension Management in the United Arab Emirates.*

   Telepharmacy was compared with traditional community-pharmacy services. Systolic pressure was significantly lower with telepharmacy at three, six and nine months. At 12 months it was **124.9 versus 132.4 mm Hg**, although that final systolic comparison did not reach statistical significance, *p*=.06. Diastolic pressure, adherence, hypertension knowledge and identification of medication problems generally favored telepharmacy.
   **Conclusion:** Mostly favorable, although the 12-month systolic result was statistically uncertain.
   *Pharmacy Practice. 2022;20:2734. DOI: 10.18549/PharmPract.2022.4.2734; PMID: 36793920.* ([PubMed][4])

5. **Mozu et al., 2023 — randomized trial, 118 adults in Ghana**

   *Utilization of Telepharmacy in the Management of Hypertension.*

   Phone-based pharmacist care plus standard clinical care was compared with standard care. In the intervention group, mean pressure fell from approximately **148/86 to 135/80 mm Hg**, and the proportion controlled increased from 39.0% to 66.1%; adherence and lifestyle measures also improved. However, the published abstract does not provide a clear between-group effect estimate for the principal blood-pressure outcome.
   **Conclusion:** Supportive but less conclusive for “better than usual care” because the comparative estimate is inadequately reported in the abstract.
   *Exploratory Research in Clinical and Social Pharmacy. 2023;12:100381. DOI: 10.1016/j.rcsop.2023.100381; PMID: 38145235.* ([PubMed][5])

## Diabetes and mixed cardiometabolic conditions

6. **Jahangard-Rafsanjani et al., 2018 — randomized trial, 100 people with type 2 diabetes**

   *Efficacy of a Telephone-Based Intervention Among Patients With Type-2 Diabetes.*

   Participants received initial diabetes education; the intervention group then received repeated pharmacist telephone counseling. HbA1c improved in both groups and was not clearly different between them, but medication adherence and self-care improved more with telephone care and remained better at nine months.
   **Conclusion:** Better for adherence and self-care, but not clearly better for HbA1c.
   *International Journal of Clinical Pharmacy. 2018. PMID: 29435911.* ([PubMed][6])

7. **Lyons et al., 2016 — MASE randomized trial, 677 patients**

   *The Medicines Advice Service Evaluation.*

   People using oral diabetes or lipid-lowering medicines received two tailored pharmacist telephone consultations, written advice and a reminder chart, or usual care. At six months, **10.6% versus 19.6%** were classified as nonadherent. Refill adherence also favored the intervention, but glycemic and lipid measurements did not significantly improve in the assessed subset.
   **Conclusion:** Better for adherence; no demonstrated metabolic benefit.
   *BMJ Quality & Safety. 2016;25:759–769. DOI: 10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004670; PMID: 26755665.* ([PubMed][7])

8. **Lauffenburger et al., 2019 — ENGAGE-DM pragmatic randomized trial, 1,400 patients**

   *Impact of a Novel Pharmacist-Delivered Behavioral Intervention for Patients With Poorly Controlled Diabetes.*

   Clinical pharmacists used telephone interviewing and shared decision-making. At 12 months, HbA1c changes were almost identical: **−0.75 percentage point with the intervention versus −0.79 with usual care**; between-group difference +0.04, 95% CI −0.22 to 0.30. Adherence was also not significantly different.
   **Conclusion:** Not better than usual care in the intention-to-treat analysis.
   *PLOS ONE. 2019;14:e0214754. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0214754; PMID: 30939143.* ([PubMed][8])

9. **Choudhry et al., 2018 — STIC2IT cluster randomized trial, 4,078 patients**

   *Effect of a Remotely Delivered Tailored Multicomponent Approach to Enhance Medication Taking.*

   Patients with poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes or hyperlipidemia received pharmacist telephone interviewing combined with texts, pillboxes and mailed reports, or usual care. Overall adherence improved by **4.7 percentage points**, but the odds of achieving disease control did not significantly improve. Emergency-department visits were less frequent, OR 0.62. In the diabetes subgroup, adherence to oral glucose-lowering medicines did not improve.
   **Conclusion:** Modest adherence benefit overall, without better disease control.
   *JAMA Internal Medicine. 2018;178:1182–1189. DOI: 10.1001/jamainternmed.2018.3189; PMID: 30083727.* ([PMC][9])

10. **Ihekoronye et al., 2024 — randomized trial, 309 people with type 2 diabetes**

*Pharmacist-Led Intervention to Improve Treatment Outcomes in Type 2 Diabetes.*

The intervention consisted of weekly pharmacist-led health education and follow-up delivered through phone-based SMS for 25 weeks, in addition to standard outpatient care. The investigators reported significantly greater HbA1c improvement, medication knowledge and adherence; 76% of the intervention group achieved refill adherence above 80%.
**Conclusion:** Better than standard care, although the intervention was studied at one tertiary-hospital outpatient service and requires replication.
*Journal of Pharmaceutical Health Services Research. 2024. DOI: 10.1093/jphsr/rmae005.* ([OUP Academic][10])

## Asthma

11. **Shdaifat et al., 2022 — randomized trial, 90 children with uncontrolled asthma**

*Clinical and Economic Impact of Telemedicine in the Management of Pediatric Asthma in Jordan.*

Children received standard care or pharmacist-led synchronous telemedicine counseling every four weeks. At 12 weeks, childhood asthma-control scores improved more with telepharmacy, and parental wage loss and overall economic burden were lower. Quality of life and satisfaction also improved.
**Conclusion:** Better than standard care, but based on a small, short pediatric trial.
*Journal of Asthma. 2022;59:1452–1462. DOI: 10.1080/02770903.2021.1924774; PMID: 33941032.* ([PubMed][11])

## Long-term anticoagulation

12. **Na Ayutthaya et al., 2018 — randomized trial, 50 patients taking warfarin after hospital discharge**

*Clinical Outcomes of Telemonitoring for Patients on Warfarin After Discharge From Hospital.*

Pharmacist telephone follow-up was compared with usual care. INR results were in the target range **45.6% versus 24.1%** of the time, and average time in therapeutic range was **49.8% versus 28.0%**. Bleeding, thrombosis and other adverse events were not significantly different.
**Conclusion:** Better laboratory anticoagulation control, but the trial was too small to establish effects on complications.
*International Journal of Telemedicine and Applications. 2018:7503421. DOI: 10.1155/2018/7503421; PMID: 30158972.* ([PubMed][12])

13. **Pham et al., 2023 — randomized trial, 561 ambulatory patients starting or restarting a direct oral anticoagulant**

*Impact of a Pharmacist Intervention on DOAC Knowledge and Satisfaction in Ambulatory Patients.*

The intervention included three pharmacist tele-visits, hotline access and coordination with other clinicians. Among 436 completing follow-up, medication-knowledge scores were **63.7% versus 62.2%**, and satisfaction was almost identical.
**Conclusion:** Not better than usual care for sustained knowledge or satisfaction. Clinical events were not the principal outcomes.
*Journal of Thrombosis and Thrombolysis. 2023;55:346–354. DOI: 10.1007/s11239-022-02743-0; PMID: 36510110.* ([PubMed][13])

## Additional nonrandomized primary study

14. **Wolverton et al., 2026 — retrospective comparative cohort, 1,776 adults with uncontrolled hypertension**

Pharmacist telehealth visits using home readings were compared with usual care. Participants receiving pharmacist care had greater systolic reductions at three months (**−12.69 versus −5.69 mm Hg**) and six months (**−13.87 versus −9.05 mm Hg**). Because patients were not randomized—people who accepted pharmacist visits were compared with those not reached or declining—the results may partly reflect selection differences.
*American Journal of Managed Care. 2026;32:23–29. DOI: 10.37765/ajmc.2026.89868; PMID: 41592209.* ([PubMed][14])

## Overall interpretation

For someone receiving care outside hospital:

* **Hypertension:** Remote pharmacist management frequently improves control, especially when combined with home readings and authority to optimize treatment.
* **Diabetes:** Telephone or digital pharmacist support can improve adherence, but large trials have not consistently improved HbA1c.
* **Asthma and warfarin:** Small trials are encouraging, but evidence is limited by sample size and short follow-up.
* **Education alone:** Tele-visits that primarily provide information may not outperform usual care.
* **Safety and patient-important outcomes:** Most studies measured blood pressure, laboratory values or adherence rather than hospitalization, complications, quality of life or survival.

Thus, the evidence supports telepharmacy as a useful **addition** to usual ambulatory care, particularly for monitoring-intensive conditions, but not as a universally superior replacement.
