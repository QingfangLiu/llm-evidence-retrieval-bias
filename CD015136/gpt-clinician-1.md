## Bottom line

Remote pharmacist care **can outperform usual care**, but the effect depends on the condition and intervention. Repeated randomized trials show the most consistent clinical benefit for **hypertension**. Diabetes results are mixed: adherence or self-care often improves, but HbA1c does not always improve. Individual trials also suggest benefits for anticoagulation control, transplant medication safety, asthma adherence/control, and ambulatory cancer pain. ([PubMed][1])

I treated telepharmacy as pharmacist-delivered care through telephone, secure messaging, video, apps, or text messages. I excluded reviews, protocols, guidelines, editorials, uncontrolled service descriptions, and interventions without substantive pharmacist participation.

## Additional controlled observational evidence

**Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction — Fox et al., 2025, JACC: Advances.** In a nonrandomized controlled study, 190 patients received pharmacist telehealth medication reconciliation, initiation, dose adjustment and monitoring, while 277 comparison patients received usual care. After weighting and difference-in-differences adjustment, pharmacist care was associated with greater use of three or four guideline-directed medication classes and a lower risk of cardiac hospitalization, RR 0.26. Because treatment assignment was nonrandom, residual confounding remains possible. ([PubMed][15])

## Clinical interpretation

For **uncontrolled hypertension**, remotely transmitted home readings coupled with pharmacist authority or a defined pathway to intensify medications appears substantially more effective than education or monitoring alone. ([PubMed][1])

For **diabetes**, the intervention design matters. Simple calls or adherence messaging may improve behavior without producing additional HbA1c reduction, whereas the more intensive Gerber intervention included pharmacist-led medication intensification and produced a clinically meaningful HbA1c difference. ([PubMed][5])

Across other conditions, promising results come from relatively small, frequently single-center studies. The transplant trial provides unusually strong medication-safety findings, but the asthma, warfarin and cancer-pain findings need replication in larger and more diverse ambulatory populations. ([PubMed][13])

This is a targeted, curated primary-study search through **July 18, 2026**, rather than a claim that every eligible study has been captured.
