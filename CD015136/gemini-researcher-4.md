Here is a breakdown of what the primary literature says about telepharmacy versus usual care for outpatient chronic disease management, bypassing all reviews and focusing directly on individual randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Overall, the primary evidence shows that telepharmacy—often involving remote medication therapy management via phone, text, or telemonitoring—consistently improves medication adherence compared to usual care. However, its impact on actual clinical biomarkers (like blood pressure or blood sugar) depends heavily on the intensity of the pharmacist's intervention.

### Improved Clinical Biomarkers (Blood Pressure and Glycemic Control)

When telepharmacy is combined with active remote monitoring and allows the pharmacist to actively adjust medication, the clinical improvements over usual care are substantial.

* **Hypertension Control:** A major cluster RCT evaluated 450 outpatients with uncontrolled hypertension. The intervention group received home blood pressure telemonitors and had regular phone consultations with pharmacists who actively adjusted their antihypertensive medications based on the data. After 12 months, 57.2% of the telepharmacy group achieved blood pressure control compared to only 30.0% of the usual care group. Systolic blood pressure (the top number) decreased by an average of 10.7 mm Hg more in the intervention group than in usual care (Margolis et al., 2013).
* **Type 2 Diabetes:** An RCT in Jordan evaluated outpatients with type 2 diabetes. Following an initial face-to-face education session, the intervention group received 8 weekly telephone follow-ups from a clinical pharmacist to review treatment plans and resolve medication issues. At 6 months, the telepharmacy group saw a significant 0.8% mean reduction in HbA1c (a measure of average blood sugar over three months), whereas the usual care group experienced a 0.1% *increase*. The telepharmacy group also saw significant improvements in secondary markers like LDL cholesterol and blood pressure (Jarab et al., 2012).

### The Adherence vs. Clinical Control Nuance

While telepharmacy reliably boosts the likelihood that a patient will take their medication correctly, increased adherence doesn't automatically guarantee that their underlying disease will be better controlled.

The STIC2IT pragmatic trial looked at a multicomponent telepharmacy intervention (including phone-based behavioral interviewing by pharmacists, text messaging, and mailed progress reports) tailored to outpatients who were nonadherent to medications for hyperlipidemia, hypertension, or diabetes.

* **Adherence wins:** The intervention resulted in a statistically significant improvement in medication adherence (an increase of 4.7% to 10.4% depending on the analysis) compared to usual care (Choudhry et al., 2018).
* **Clinical outcomes lag:** Despite taking their medications more reliably, the telepharmacy group did not see a statistically significant difference in their odds of achieving good clinical disease control (e.g., reaching target blood pressure or cholesterol levels) compared to the usual care group. However, the telepharmacy participants were significantly less likely to require an emergency department visit (Choudhry et al., 2018).

This suggests that while telepharmacy is highly effective at getting outpatients to take their chronic disease medications, to actually move the needle on clinical markers, the remote pharmacist often needs the clinical authority to adjust dosages and switch therapies, rather than simply encouraging compliance with an existing prescription.
