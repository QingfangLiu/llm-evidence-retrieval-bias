## Bottom line

Across the individual studies, **telepharmacy appears most effective when pharmacists receive home-monitoring data and can actively adjust medication treatment**, particularly for hypertension. Telephone education or adherence counseling can improve medication-taking, but improvements in HbA1c, cholesterol, quality of life, and other clinical outcomes are less consistent.

The citations below are all **individual primary studies**, principally randomized controlled trials. No reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, editorials, or opinion pieces are included.

## Informative primary study with a less exact match

**Fischer et al., 2014** randomized **2,378 adults with diabetes** to a single scripted telephone call from a diabetes educator or clinical pharmacist versus usual care. The brief call did **not** improve initial medication filling, six-month persistence, HbA1c, BP, or LDL cholesterol. Because calls were not delivered exclusively by pharmacists, this is not a pure telepharmacy trial, but it suggests that a single, very short contact is insufficient. ([Diabetes Journals][9])

## Interpretation of the individual studies

The most persuasive results concern **blood-pressure management**. The Green and Margolis trials were comparatively large and used repeated home BP information, protocol-guided pharmacist follow-up, and active medication adjustment—not simply education or reminders.

For **medication adherence**, individualized and repeated telephone care was beneficial in MASE, the Iranian diabetes study, the Chinese postdischarge study, and the 2026 stroke study. However, several adherence measures were self-reported, and the smaller trials may overestimate benefits.

For **diabetes control**, results were mixed. Gerber and Wang reported improved HbA1c, but both interventions contained additional components beyond remote pharmacist contact. MASE and Sarayani found better adherence without a clearly significant improvement in glycemic outcomes.

Thus, telepharmacy is **not uniformly better than usual care for every outcome**. It appears most likely to outperform usual care when it is sustained, individualized, linked to objective home measurements, and permits the pharmacist to recommend or implement medication changes. Brief advice-only telephone interventions appear less effective.
