## Scope

I included randomized comparative studies of **subcutaneous prandial insulin in adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus multiple daily injections**. I excluded pump/AID studies, pediatric-only studies, pharmacokinetic clamp studies, pregnancy studies, and noncomparative observational reports.

Several publications are extensions or substudies of the same randomized cohort; these are marked below and should not be counted as independent trials.

## Overall findings

### 1. Rapid-acting analogues versus regular human insulin

**Postprandial glucose is the clearest benefit.** Lispro and aspart consistently reduced the glucose rise during the first 1–2 hours after meals compared with regular human insulin. In large trials, reductions were commonly around **20–35 mg/dL**, although meal, monitoring method, and insulin-timing instructions differed. ([PubMed][1])

**HbA1c improvement is small and inconsistent.** Some trials reported an advantage of approximately **0.1–0.2 percentage points**, while others found no significant difference. Better postprandial control can be offset by higher premeal glucose or inadequate basal insulin coverage. ([PubMed][2])

**Severe hypoglycemia is generally not clearly reduced.** Total severe or major hypoglycemia was usually similar. Some studies found fewer nocturnal episodes with lispro or aspart, including a double-blind crossover trial in which major nocturnal hypoglycemia was substantially lower with aspart; this has not been uniformly reproduced across trials. ([PubMed][1])

**Practical convenience is important.** Rapid analogues were administered immediately or shortly before eating, whereas regular insulin was generally given 30–45 minutes before eating. Treatment satisfaction or acceptance favored the analogue in some studies, although not every crossover study found a clear patient preference. ([PubMed][1])

### 2. Ultra-rapid analogues versus standard rapid-acting analogues

#### Faster aspart versus insulin aspart

In the 26-week onset 1 trial, mealtime faster aspart reduced HbA1c by an additional **0.15 percentage points** and reduced the 1-hour post-meal glucose increment by approximately **21 mg/dL**. Postmeal faster aspart preserved HbA1c noninferiority but did not provide the same postprandial advantage. Overall severe or blood-glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was similar. ([Diabetes Journals][3])

At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference was approximately **0.10 percentage points**, with continued improvement in 1-hour postprandial glucose and no clear overall hypoglycemia or general adverse-event difference. This was an extension of the onset 1 cohort, not a separate randomized population. ([PMC][4])

In onset 8, where insulin degludec was the basal insulin, mealtime faster aspart produced essentially the same HbA1c as insulin aspart but lowered the 1-hour post-meal increment by approximately **16 mg/dL**. Postmeal faster aspart resulted in a small HbA1c disadvantage. Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was again similar; hypoglycemia 3–4 hours after meals was less frequent with mealtime faster aspart. ([PMC][5])

#### Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro

In PRONTO-T1D, mealtime ultra-rapid lispro produced similar HbA1c to lispro but lowered meal-test glucose by approximately **28 mg/dL at 1 hour** and **31 mg/dL at 2 hours**. Postmeal ultra-rapid lispro had a modest HbA1c disadvantage compared with mealtime lispro. Overall severe, documented, and total postprandial hypoglycemia were similar, while hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was less frequent. ([PubMed][6])

The CGM substudy found approximately **44 additional minutes per day in daytime time-in-range** with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro and less nighttime time below 70 mg/dL. This was a subset of PRONTO-T1D rather than an independent trial. ([PubMed][7])

At 52 weeks, HbA1c remained similar and the postprandial advantage persisted. Injection-site reactions were more frequent with ultra-rapid lispro—approximately **3.3% versus 0.9%**—although overall treatment-emergent adverse events were similar. This was an extension of the original PRONTO-T1D cohort. ([PubMed][8])

### 3. Head-to-head standard rapid analogues

A 26-week randomized comparison of insulin glulisine with lispro found equivalent HbA1c improvement and no clinically important differences in symptomatic, nocturnal, or severe hypoglycemia. This suggests that switching between standard rapid analogues is unlikely to produce the same postprandial difference seen when moving from regular insulin to a rapid analogue or from a standard rapid analogue to an ultra-rapid formulation. ([PubMed][9])

## Clinically relevant interpretation

For adults currently using **regular human insulin**, the strongest reasons to switch are:

* Lower early post-meal glucose excursions.
* Ability to inject immediately before eating rather than 30–45 minutes beforehand.
* A possible, but usually modest, HbA1c improvement.
* Possible reduction in nocturnal hypoglycemia in some patients, although the evidence is inconsistent.

For adults already using **aspart or lispro**, an ultra-rapid formulation mainly offers better control during the first 1–2 hours after meals. The average HbA1c advantage is small or absent. Benefits may therefore be most meaningful for people with reproducible early post-meal excursions despite appropriate carbohydrate estimation, injection technique, and premeal timing.

Routine postmeal administration provides flexibility when meal intake is uncertain, but the trials suggest that it generally gives up much of the postprandial advantage and may result in slightly higher HbA1c than mealtime dosing. ([PubMed][10])

Across these trials, there is **no demonstrated reduction in mortality, diabetic ketoacidosis, or long-term microvascular or cardiovascular outcomes**. Most studies lasted 12–52 weeks, and the longest aspart follow-up was approximately three years. Thus, the evidence primarily addresses glucose profiles, HbA1c, hypoglycemia, convenience, and short-term tolerability—not long-term complications. ([PubMed][11])
