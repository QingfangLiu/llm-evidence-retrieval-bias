As an evidence-synthesis researcher, your focus on individual primary studies is the most rigorous way to evaluate clinical effects without the confounding interpretations of secondary literature.

Based on primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating faster-acting insulin analogues—both the "first-generation" rapid-acting analogues (e.g., insulin lispro, aspart) and the newer "ultra-rapid" formulations (e.g., fast-acting insulin aspart [faster aspart], ultra-rapid lispro [URLi])—against regular human insulin (RHI) or older rapid analogues in adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) using multiple daily injections (MDI), the evidence demonstrates distinct profiles of benefits and harms.

Here is a synthesis of the findings from foundational and recent primary studies.

### Benefits of Faster-Acting Analogues

**1. Superior Postprandial Glucose (PPG) Control**
Faster-acting insulin analogues were explicitly designed to better mimic physiological insulin secretion, producing an earlier and higher peak insulin concentration.

* **Rapid Analogues vs. RHI:** First-generation rapid analogues achieve a faster onset of action and shorter duration than RHI, effectively blunting postprandial glycemic excursions that RHI often fails to control due to delayed absorption.
* **Ultra-Rapid vs. Rapid Analogues:** The newer ultra-rapid formulations achieve even greater early exposure. In euglycemic clamp and pharmacokinetic trials, URLi showed a five times faster appearance and up to a seven-fold greater early insulin exposure than standard insulin lispro, resulting in a left-shifted action curve (Linnebjerg et al., 2020). Clinically, the PRONTO-T1D trial showed that URLi administered at mealtime significantly reduced 1-hour and 2-hour PPG excursions compared to standard lispro (Klaff et al., 2020). Similarly, the *onset 1* trial demonstrated that faster aspart significantly reduced 1-hour PPG increments compared to conventional insulin aspart (Russell-Jones et al., 2017).

**2. Reductions in Severe and Nocturnal Hypoglycemia (Versus RHI)**
One of the most clinically significant benefits of moving from RHI to faster-acting analogues is the reduction of delayed hypoglycemia.

* Because RHI has a prolonged duration of action (often continuing to lower glucose 4–6 hours post-injection), it increases the risk of late postprandial and nocturnal hypoglycemia. A classic crossover RCT evaluating patients with T1D and impaired hypoglycemia awareness demonstrated a strong trend toward lower severe hypoglycemia with insulin lispro compared to RHI, driven predominantly by a 47% reduction in severe nocturnal hypoglycemic events (Ferguson et al., 2001).
* Ultra-rapid insulins also leave the bloodstream faster than their standard rapid-acting counterparts, which further reduces late glucose-lowering effects (Linnebjerg et al., 2020).

**3. Improved or Non-Inferior HbA1c**
Primary trials show that faster-acting analogues generally provide either modest improvements or non-inferiority in overall glycemic control.

* In the *onset 1* trial, mealtime faster aspart was associated with a statistically significant, though modest, improvement in HbA1c compared to conventional mealtime insulin aspart (Russell-Jones et al., 2017).
* In the PRONTO-T1D trial, URLi demonstrated non-inferiority for HbA1c reduction compared to lispro (Klaff et al., 2020).

**4. Dosing Flexibility**
A major quality-of-life benefit for MDI users is administration timing. While RHI must be injected 30 minutes prior to a meal, and rapid analogues (lispro, aspart) are recommended 0–15 minutes before eating, ultra-rapid formulations offer post-meal flexibility.

* The *onset 1* trial and a subgroup analysis of the *onset 8* trial confirmed that administering faster aspart up to 20 minutes *after* the start of a meal maintained non-inferior overall HbA1c control compared to pre-meal conventional insulin aspart, offering significant flexibility for patients uncertain of meal carbohydrate content (Russell-Jones et al., 2017; Komatsu et al., 2021).

### Harms and Risks

**1. Shift in Hypoglycemia Timing**
While overall severe hypoglycemia rates remain similar or lower compared to active comparators, the *timing* of hypoglycemic risk shifts significantly due to the altered pharmacokinetic profiles.

* **Early Postprandial Hypoglycemia:** Because ultra-rapid analogues peak so quickly, there is a higher risk of hypoglycemia occurring in the first 1 to 2 hours after a meal if the meal contains complex carbohydrates or fats that delay gastric emptying. For example, the *onset 1* trial noted that while overall hypoglycemia rates were similar, there were higher rates of hypoglycemia within the first hour of a meal with faster aspart compared to conventional insulin aspart (Russell-Jones et al., 2017).

**2. Injection Site Reactions**
The newer ultra-rapid formulations contain specific excipients to speed up absorption (e.g., niacinamide in faster aspart; citrate and treprostinil in URLi). These additives have been associated with slight increases in local tissue reactions. While most reports of infusion/injection site pain are derived from continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) pump studies, local site reactions and transient pain following injection have been noted as slightly more frequent with ultra-rapid formulations compared to their predecessors.
