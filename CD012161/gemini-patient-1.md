Deciding to switch insulin types is a big step when you manage type 1 diabetes on multiple daily injections (MDI). Moving from regular human insulin to a rapid-acting analogue—or upgrading from a standard rapid-acting analogue to an ultra-rapid one—involves weighing specific benefits and harms.

To help you understand the evidence, here is a breakdown based strictly on primary randomized controlled trials involving adults with type 1 diabetes on MDI.

## 1. Rapid-Acting Analogues vs. Regular Human Insulin (RHI)

First-generation rapid-acting analogues (like insulin aspart and insulin lispro) were developed to mimic the body's natural insulin response better than Regular Human Insulin (RHI), which requires a 30-minute wait before eating.

### Benefits

* **Better Post-Meal Control:** Because rapid-acting analogues absorb faster and peak sooner, they significantly reduce postprandial (post-meal) blood glucose spikes compared to RHI (Home et al., 2000).
* **Improved Long-Term Control:** Studies have shown that using rapid-acting insulin as a mealtime bolus results in small but statistically significant improvements in HbA1c compared to RHI (Home et al., 2000).
* **Flexibility and Satisfaction:** Patients report significantly higher treatment satisfaction with rapid-acting analogues because they can be taken immediately before eating, offering much more lifestyle flexibility (Home et al., 2000).

### Harms / Nuances

* **Pre-Meal Glucose Levels:** Because rapid-acting analogues have a shorter duration of action, they wear off sooner than RHI. This can sometimes lead to slightly higher blood glucose levels just before the *next* meal if basal insulin isn't properly adjusted (Home et al., 2000).
* **Hypoglycemia Risk:** While the overall risk of major severe hypoglycemia is similar between the two, rapid-acting analogues actually tend to *reduce* the risk of late post-meal and nocturnal (nighttime) severe hypoglycemia compared to RHI (Home et al., 2000).

## 2. Ultra-Rapid Analogues vs. Standard Rapid-Acting Analogues

Second-generation ultra-rapid analogues (like faster-acting aspart/Fiasp and ultra-rapid lispro/Lyumjev) add specific excipients to standard analogues to speed up absorption into the bloodstream even more.

### Benefits

* **Superior Post-Meal Control:** When taken at mealtime, ultra-rapid insulins reduce the 1-hour and 2-hour post-meal glucose spikes significantly better than standard rapid-acting insulins (Klaff et al., 2020; Russell-Jones et al., 2017).
* **Forgiveness with Late Dosing:** If you forget to pre-bolus, ultra-rapid insulins offer a safety net. Trials show that taking ultra-rapid insulin *after* you start eating (up to 20 minutes after the meal begins) controls HbA1c just as effectively as taking standard rapid-acting insulin *before* the meal (Russell-Jones et al., 2017).
* **Reduced Late Hypoglycemia:** Because ultra-rapid insulins peak fast and leave the system quickly, they can reduce the rate of hypoglycemia occurring more than 4 hours after a meal compared to standard analogues (Klaff et al., 2020).

### Harms / Nuances

* **Injection Site Reactions:** The additives that make these insulins absorb faster (such as citrate and treprostinil in ultra-rapid lispro) can cause mild, localized injection site reactions. Patients using ultra-rapid formulations report slightly higher rates of infusion or injection site pain compared to those using standard formulations (Klaff et al., 2020).
* **Similar Overall Severe Hypoglycemia:** Moving to an ultra-rapid insulin does not necessarily lower your overall risk of severe hypoglycemia; trials show the overall rates are comparable to standard rapid-acting insulins (Klaff et al., 2020; Russell-Jones et al., 2017).
