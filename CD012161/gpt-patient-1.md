## Evidence scope

I focused on randomized primary studies in nonpregnant adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus multiple daily injections. Pump-only, pediatric-only, single-dose pharmacokinetic, observational, review, guideline, and commentary publications were excluded from the effectiveness synthesis. Older studies commonly used NPH or ultralente as basal insulin, whereas newer trials used detemir, glargine, or degludec.

## Main findings

### 1. Ultra-rapid insulin versus a standard rapid-acting analogue

**Faster aspart versus insulin aspart**

In onset 1, 1,143 adults were randomized to mealtime faster aspart, mealtime insulin aspart, or postmeal faster aspart for 26 weeks with detemir. Mealtime faster aspart:

* Lowered HbA1c by an additional **0.15 percentage points** versus aspart.
* Lowered 1-hour meal-test glucose excursion by approximately **21 mg/dL** and 2-hour excursion by approximately **12 mg/dL**.
* Did not increase the overall rate of severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia.

Postmeal faster aspart maintained noninferior HbA1c, but did not provide the same postprandial advantage as premeal faster aspart. At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference remained small—about **0.10 percentage points**—with similar overall hypoglycemia and adverse-event rates. ([Diabetes Journals][1])

In onset 8, 1,025 adults received degludec plus mealtime faster aspart, mealtime aspart, or postmeal faster aspart. Mealtime faster aspart produced essentially the same HbA1c as aspart—difference **−0.02 percentage points**—but reduced 1-hour meal-test glucose excursion by approximately **16 mg/dL**. Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was similar, with less hypoglycemia 3–4 hours after meals in the mealtime faster-aspart group. Postmeal dosing was noninferior by the trial’s margin, but HbA1c was about **0.10 percentage points higher** than with premeal aspart. ([PMC][2])

**Ultra-rapid lispro, or lispro-aabc, versus lispro**

PRONTO-T1D randomized 1,222 adults to mealtime ultra-rapid lispro, mealtime lispro, or postmeal ultra-rapid lispro for 26 weeks. Mealtime ultra-rapid lispro:

* Produced a small HbA1c difference of **−0.08 percentage points** versus lispro.
* Reduced meal-test glucose excursion by approximately **28 mg/dL at 1 hour** and **31 mg/dL at 2 hours**.
* Had similar severe, documented, and overall postprandial hypoglycemia.
* Had 37% fewer hypoglycemic events occurring more than four hours after meals.
* Caused more injection-site reactions: **2.9% versus 0.2%** with lispro.

Postmeal ultra-rapid lispro met the noninferiority criterion, but its endpoint HbA1c was **0.13 percentage points higher** than with mealtime lispro. At 52 weeks, HbA1c remained similar between mealtime ultra-rapid lispro and lispro. ([PubMed][3])

**Glulisine versus lispro**

A 26-week trial involving 683 randomized adults found identical mean HbA1c reductions of **0.14 percentage points** with glulisine and lispro. Overall, nocturnal, and severe hypoglycemia were similar. The glulisine group used slightly less total insulin, but the clinical importance was uncertain. ([PubMed][4])

### 2. Standard rapid-acting analogues versus regular human insulin

**Aspart versus regular insulin**

Two large trials showed a consistent pattern: clearly lower postmeal glucose, but only a small HbA1c advantage.

In Home et al., 1,070 adults were treated for six months. Aspart lowered postmeal glucose by approximately **11–22 mg/dL**, depending on the meal, and improved HbA1c by **0.12 percentage points**. Major hypoglycemia was not significantly different. Treatment satisfaction favored aspart. Premeal glucose was sometimes higher with aspart, suggesting that basal insulin or the shorter bolus “tail” may require adjustment. ([PubMed][5])

In Raskin et al., 882 adults entered the six-month trial and 714 continued to 12 months. At six months, mean postmeal glucose was lower with aspart after breakfast, lunch, and dinner, while HbA1c was **7.78% versus 7.93%** with regular insulin. Adverse events and overall hypoglycemia were similar, and the findings persisted through 12 months. ([PubMed][6])

A Japanese phase 3 basal–bolus trial also compared aspart with soluble human insulin and reported improved postprandial control without a major new safety signal. ([J-STAGE][7])

**Lispro versus regular insulin**

A 12-month multicenter study reported 35% and 64% smaller glucose rises at one and two hours after meals with lispro; HbA1c in participants with type 1 diabetes was **8.1% versus 8.3%** with regular insulin. ([PubMed][8])

A large six-month crossover trial involving 1,008 participants found approximately **23 mg/dL less glucose rise at one hour** and **36 mg/dL less at two hours** with lispro. Reported hypoglycemic episodes were 12% less frequent, with the largest relative difference at night. ([PubMed][9])

Results were less uniform in smaller trials:

* In 135 randomized participants studied by Heller et al., HbA1c was similar, but total hypoglycemic episodes were **775 with lispro versus 1,156 with regular insulin**, largely because nocturnal episodes were **52 versus 181**. Because of a treatment-period interaction, only first-period data were used for the main comparison. ([PubMed][10])
* In 33 adults with impaired hypoglycemia awareness, severe events numerically favored lispro—55 versus 84—but the difference was not statistically conclusive. Nocturnal severe events were also numerically lower. ([PubMed][11])
* A 93-person double-blind crossover trial found better postbreakfast and postlunch glucose with lispro, but HbA1c and total symptomatic hypoglycemia were unchanged. Nocturnal hypoglycemia was less frequent, while morning hypoglycemia was more frequent. ([PubMed][12])
* In 45 newly diagnosed adults, lispro and regular insulin produced similar HbA1c, mild hypoglycemia, β-cell function, and antibody outcomes through 12 months. ([PubMed][13])
* A small 12-person crossover trial found lower postmeal glucose and fewer reported hypoglycemic events with lispro, but its size makes the estimates imprecise. ([ResearchGate][14])

## Benefits most consistently supported

**Better early postmeal control is the clearest benefit.** The improvement is generally larger and more consistent than the HbA1c improvement. With modern ultra-rapid products, the direct advantage over standard rapid analogues was commonly around **16–31 mg/dL during the first two hours after a standardized meal**. ([Diabetes Journals][1])

**HbA1c improvement is usually small.** Modern ultra-rapid versus standard rapid comparisons generally showed differences from approximately zero to **0.15 percentage points**. Older rapid-analogue versus regular-insulin trials commonly found an advantage around **0.1–0.2 percentage points**, although not every trial found one. ([Diabetes Journals][1])

**Less premeal waiting is a practical advantage.** In the trials, regular insulin was generally given about 30–45 minutes before eating, standard analogues immediately or shortly before eating, and ultra-rapid formulations at meal initiation. Postmeal dosing was feasible, but usually sacrificed some postprandial control and, in PRONTO-T1D, produced a slightly higher HbA1c. ([PubMed][6])

## Harms and uncertainties

**Overall severe or confirmed hypoglycemia was generally not increased**, but neither was a consistent major reduction demonstrated. Some lispro studies found less nocturnal hypoglycemia, and the modern ultra-rapid trials found fewer late postmeal episodes. Faster onset can shift risk toward the early postmeal period when insulin and carbohydrate absorption are mismatched, even though the major MDI trials did not show higher overall rates. ([PMC][2])

**Injection discomfort or reactions appear more relevant with ultra-rapid lispro.** PRONTO-T1D found reactions in approximately 2%–3% of ultra-rapid lispro users versus 0.2% with lispro. The faster-aspart injection trials did not identify a comparable overall safety difference. ([PubMed][3])

**Postmeal dosing is a fallback rather than the most effective strategy.** It offered flexibility and met liberal noninferiority margins, but premeal or meal-start ultra-rapid dosing generally controlled glucose better. ([Diabetes Journals][1])

**Long-term outcomes remain unknown.** These trials were designed around HbA1c, meal excursions, and hypoglycemia over months—not retinopathy, kidney disease, cardiovascular events, mortality, or decades-long safety.

For an individual using MDI, the evidence makes a faster formulation most attractive when early postmeal spikes remain problematic despite reasonably accurate carbohydrate dosing, or when consistently administering insulin well before eating is impractical. The likely benefit is smaller when the principal problem is missed boluses, inaccurate carbohydrate estimates, inadequate basal insulin, or delayed glucose absorption from gastroparesis or high-fat meals. A switch should be evaluated with CGM or structured glucose data because insulin-to-carbohydrate ratios, correction dosing, basal coverage, or timing may need adjustment.
