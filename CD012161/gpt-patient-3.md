## Bottom line

For adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus multiple daily injections, faster prandial insulins **most consistently improve glucose during the first 1–2 hours after eating** and permit dosing closer to mealtime. They generally produce only a **small improvement—or no meaningful difference—in HbA1c** compared with properly timed regular human insulin or a standard rapid-acting analogue.

Overall severe or confirmed hypoglycemia is usually similar between treatments. Some trials found fewer nocturnal or late post-meal episodes, but faster absorption can shift hypoglycemia earlier after eating. Ultra-rapid lispro has a clearer formulation-specific adverse effect: **more injection-site reactions**.

## Rapid-acting analogues versus regular human insulin

### Postprandial glucose

Aspart, lispro and glulisine generally reduced early post-meal excursions compared with regular insulin. For example:

* In a 1,070-adult trial, insulin aspart lowered post-meal glucose by approximately 0.6–1.2 mmol/L (11–22 mg/dL), depending on the meal and measurement, compared with regular human insulin. ([DOI][1])
* In a large aspart trial, post-meal glucose values were approximately 15–29 mg/dL lower after breakfast, lunch and dinner. ([PubMed][2])
* In the large lispro crossover trial, the rise in glucose was approximately 23 mg/dL lower at one hour and 36 mg/dL lower at two hours. ([PubMed][3])

Regular insulin in these studies was generally administered about 30–45 minutes before eating, while the analogues could be given immediately before the meal. Thus, part of the practical advantage is more convenient timing.

### HbA1c

Differences were small:

* Aspart produced an adjusted HbA1c advantage of approximately **0.12 percentage points** at six months in one large trial. ([DOI][1])
* Another large trial reported HbA1c of **7.78% with aspart versus 7.93% with regular insulin**. ([PubMed][2])
* Several smaller or tightly controlled trials found essentially no HbA1c difference. ([PubMed][4])

A three-year aspart follow-up found an HbA1c advantage of about **0.16 percentage points**, but this was an extension of an earlier randomized cohort rather than a separate new trial. ([PubMed][5])

### Hypoglycemia and other harms

The results depended on the timing and definition of hypoglycemia:

* A double-blind crossover study found **72% fewer major nocturnal episodes** with aspart when both aspart and regular insulin were given immediately before meals. Total major hypoglycemia was not significantly different. ([PubMed][4])
* A lispro trial found fewer total and nocturnal hypoglycemic events, while another found the same total symptomatic rate, with fewer nocturnal but more morning episodes. ([PubMed][6])
* In the three-year aspart extension, major hypoglycemia was similar, but minor hypoglycemia was more frequent with aspart, rate ratio **1.24**. ([PubMed][5])
* Some trials found somewhat higher fasting or pre-meal glucose with rapid analogues, plausibly reflecting their shorter duration and the need to optimize basal insulin or meal dosing. ([DOI][1])
* Glulisine given before meals modestly improved HbA1c compared with regular insulin; severe hypoglycemia occurred in 8.4% versus 10.1%, respectively, without a clear statistically significant safety advantage. ([PubMed][7])

## Ultra-rapid analogues versus standard rapid analogues

### Faster aspart versus insulin aspart

In ONSET 1, mealtime faster aspart produced:

* HbA1c approximately **0.15 percentage points lower** at 26 weeks.
* One-hour post-meal glucose approximately **1.18 mmol/L, or 21 mg/dL, lower**.
* Two-hour post-meal glucose approximately **0.67 mmol/L, or 12 mg/dL, lower**.
* No material difference in the overall rate of severe or blood-glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia. ([Diabetes Journals][8])

At 52 weeks, the HbA1c advantage was approximately **0.10 percentage points**, and one-hour post-meal glucose remained approximately 16 mg/dL lower. Overall hypoglycemia and adverse-event rates remained similar. ([PMC][9])

In ONSET 8, which used insulin degludec as the basal insulin:

* Mealtime faster aspart was noninferior for HbA1c, with an estimated difference of only **−0.02 percentage points**.
* One-hour post-meal glucose was approximately **16 mg/dL lower**.
* Overall safety was similar.
* Hypoglycemia three to four hours after meals was less frequent with faster aspart. ([PMC][10])

Post-meal faster aspart also met noninferiority criteria, but was generally less favorable than taking faster aspart before eating. In ONSET 8, its HbA1c was approximately 0.10 percentage points higher than with mealtime standard aspart. ([PMC][10])

### Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro

In PRONTO-T1D, mealtime ultra-rapid lispro produced:

* An HbA1c difference of approximately **−0.08 percentage points**, without a convincing clinically important HbA1c advantage.
* One-hour post-meal glucose approximately **1.55 mmol/L, or 28 mg/dL, lower**.
* Two-hour post-meal glucose approximately **1.73 mmol/L, or 31 mg/dL, lower**.
* Similar overall severe, documented and total post-meal hypoglycemia.
* Approximately **37% less hypoglycemia more than four hours after meals**.
* Injection-site reactions in **2.9% versus 0.2%** with standard lispro. ([PubMed][11])

At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference was not significant, while the post-meal advantage persisted. Late post-meal hypoglycemia was approximately 31% lower, but injection-site reactions remained more frequent, **3.3% versus 0.9%**. ([PubMed][12])

A continuous-glucose-monitoring substudy found that mealtime ultra-rapid lispro increased daytime time in range by approximately **44 minutes per day** and reduced nighttime time below 70 mg/dL by approximately **12 minutes per night** compared with lispro. This was a substudy of PRONTO-T1D, not an independent trial population. ([PubMed][13])

Post-meal ultra-rapid lispro was noninferior by the trial’s prespecified HbA1c margin, but endpoint HbA1c was approximately **0.13 percentage points higher** than with premeal standard lispro. Therefore, post-meal dosing is better viewed as an occasional flexibility option rather than the preferred routine strategy. ([PubMed][11])

### Standard rapid analogue versus another standard rapid analogue

A 26-week comparison of glulisine and lispro found essentially identical HbA1c reductions and no relevant difference in overall, nocturnal or severe hypoglycemia. Glulisine required a slightly smaller increase in total daily insulin. ([PubMed][14])

## Clinical interpretation for an adult using MDI

The evidence most strongly favors changing to a faster or ultra-rapid insulin when the main goals are:

* Reducing early post-meal spikes.
* Taking insulin immediately before eating rather than substantially in advance.
* Potentially reducing late post-meal or nocturnal hypoglycemia associated with longer prandial insulin action.

It is less compelling when the main expectation is a large HbA1c reduction: the average difference is usually **0–0.15 percentage points**, despite more visible improvements on post-meal glucose curves.

A switch can require reassessment of the insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio, correction timing and basal coverage. Because the action is shifted earlier, CGM or frequent glucose review during the first several hours after meals is particularly useful. Changes should be made with the prescribing diabetes clinician rather than by substituting unit-for-unit without follow-up.

## Important limitations

Most studies were open-label, treat-to-target trials lasting 12–52 weeks. They were not designed to evaluate diabetic complications, cardiovascular outcomes or mortality. Several older regular-insulin comparisons used NPH basal insulin and self-monitoring rather than current basal analogues and CGM. Some pivotal older studies included adolescents as well as adults; these are identified below as mixed-age evidence.
