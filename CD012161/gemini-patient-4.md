Deciding to switch your mealtime insulin is a significant step in managing multiple daily injections (MDI). When looking closely at primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the evolution of mealtime insulin breaks down into two distinct leaps: the shift from regular human insulin to rapid-acting analogues (like lispro and aspart), and the more recent shift to "ultra-rapid" formulations (like ultra-rapid lispro and faster-acting aspart).

Here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms demonstrated in key primary studies for adults with type 1 diabetes.

## 1. Rapid-Acting Analogues vs. Regular Human Insulin

Before the 1990s, regular human insulin was the standard mealtime therapy. However, regular insulin molecules cluster together under the skin, meaning it takes time to absorb, often acting too slowly to cover a meal and lingering too long afterward.

**Benefits**

* **Better Post-Meal Control:** Rapid-acting analogues (such as insulin lispro and insulin aspart) were structurally modified to break apart and enter the bloodstream much faster. Trials showed that this faster absorption mimics the body's natural physiological response more closely, resulting in significantly smaller blood glucose spikes 1 and 2 hours after eating (Garg et al., 1996; Home et al., 1998).
* **Overall Blood Glucose Stability:** Measuring over a full 24-hour period, patients using insulin aspart spent significantly more time in their target glucose range compared to those using regular human insulin (Home et al., 1998).

**Harms & Reductions in Risk**

* **Decreased Hypoglycemia:** Because regular human insulin lingers in the blood for hours after a meal, it can cause delayed low blood sugar, especially if another dose is "stacked" on top of it. Switching to a rapid-acting analogue significantly reduces the total number of hypoglycemic episodes, including severe events requiring the help of another person to treat (Garg et al., 1996; Home et al., 1998).

## 2. Ultra-Rapid Insulins vs. Standard Rapid-Acting Analogues

The newest generation of insulin adds specific compounds (excipients) to standard rapid-acting analogues to speed up absorption even further. Examples include ultra-rapid lispro (URLi) and faster-acting aspart.

**Benefits**

* **Faster Action and Superior Post-Meal Control:** These formulations enter the blood significantly faster. For instance, ultra-rapid lispro appears in the bloodstream five times faster than standard lispro, resulting in a much stronger early glucose-lowering effect (Linnebjerg et al., 2020). In head-to-head trials, both mealtime ultra-rapid lispro and faster-acting aspart provided superior control of postprandial glucose spikes at 1 and 2 hours compared to their standard rapid-acting counterparts (Klaff et al., 2020; Russell-Jones et al., 2017).
* **Non-Inferior A1c Reduction:** The newer formulations match or slightly improve overall HbA1c levels compared to standard rapid-acting insulins when used in an MDI regimen (Klaff et al., 2020; Russell-Jones et al., 2017).

**Harms & Considerations**

* **Lower Late-Hypoglycemia Risk:** Because ultra-rapid insulins leave the bloodstream faster, they carry a lower risk of late postprandial hypoglycemia. One trial found a 37% reduction in hypoglycemic episodes occurring more than 4 hours after a meal with ultra-rapid lispro compared to standard lispro (Klaff et al., 2020).
* **Injection Site Reactions:** The overall safety profiles are highly comparable, and the ultra-rapid versions do not increase the overall risk of severe hypoglycemia (Russell-Jones et al., 2017). However, the additives used to widen blood vessels and speed up absorption can cause a slight increase in minor injection site reactions. In one trial, 2.9% of patients using ultra-rapid lispro reported localized injection site reactions (like pain or swelling), compared to just 0.2% on standard lispro (Klaff et al., 2020).
