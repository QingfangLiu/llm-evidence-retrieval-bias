## Scope used

I treated this as a search for **comparative randomized trials in nonpregnant adults with type 1 diabetes using a basal–bolus multiple-daily-injection regimen**, prioritizing studies lasting **at least 24 weeks**. I excluded pump-only studies, pediatric-only studies, single-arm switching studies, short pharmacokinetic/clamp or single-meal experiments, and all secondary evidence.

I identified **15 underlying randomized trials reported in 14 principal journal publications**; one older lispro publication combined two underlying trials. Several trials also generated extension, CGM, quality-of-life, or subgroup publications.

## Main findings

### Faster analogues versus regular human insulin

**Postprandial glucose is the clearest benefit.** Insulin lispro and insulin aspart generally produced lower glucose excursions after meals than regular human insulin. In the large Anderson lispro program, postprandial increments among participants with type 1 diabetes were 35% lower at 1 hour and 64% lower at 2 hours, with an endpoint HbA1c of 8.1% versus 8.3%. ([PubMed][1])

Two large insulin-aspart trials found similar results:

* In the 1,070-participant European trial, aspart lowered postprandial glucose by approximately 0.6–1.2 mmol/L and produced a 0.12-percentage-point HbA1c advantage. Treatment satisfaction was also better. ([DOI][2])
* In the 882-participant North American trial, postmeal glucose was lower after breakfast, lunch, and dinner, and HbA1c was 7.78% with aspart versus 7.93% with regular insulin at six months. The differences persisted during the extension. ([PubMed][3])

The Japanese phase III trial found aspart noninferior for HbA1c, with lower 90-minute glucose after breakfast and a comparable safety profile. ([J-STAGE][4]) Smaller lispro studies were less consistent: Recasens found no material HbA1c difference, whereas the 12-person crossover study by Provenzano reported lower postprandial glucose and fewer hypoglycemic episodes with lispro. ([PubMed][5])

**HbA1c benefits were small.** Where statistically significant, between-group effects were generally around 0.1–0.2 percentage points. Some smaller studies found no meaningful HbA1c difference. This pattern is consistent with treat-to-target designs in which both groups receive active dose adjustment. ([PubMed][5])

**Overall hypoglycemia was usually similar**, but certain temporal categories favored the analogue:

* In the European aspart trial, total major hypoglycemia was not significantly different, but major nocturnal events requiring parenteral treatment occurred in 1.3% versus 3.4%, and late postmeal events in 1.8% versus 5.0%. ([DOI][2])
* Among 33 adults with impaired awareness, lispro produced nonsignificant trends toward fewer severe episodes—55 versus 84—and fewer nocturnal severe episodes—25 versus 47. The study was underpowered for these differences. ([PubMed][6])
* Raskin et al. found similar overall hypoglycemia and adverse-event rates despite better postprandial control with aspart. ([PubMed][3])

A longer extension of the European aspart trial found a maintained HbA1c advantage of approximately 0.16 percentage points and no difference in major hypoglycemia, but **minor hypoglycemia was more frequent with aspart**: relative risk 1.24, 95% CI 1.09–1.39. ([ScienceDirect][7])

### One rapid-acting analogue versus another

Two trials directly compared **insulin glulisine with insulin lispro**.

The multinational 26-week trial found identical mean HbA1c reductions of 0.14 percentage points and no relevant differences in symptomatic, nocturnal, or severe hypoglycemia. Total daily insulin dose decreased slightly with glulisine and increased slightly with lispro, although the clinical importance was uncertain. ([PubMed][8])

The 28-week Japanese trial also established noninferiority. HbA1c increased by 0.10 percentage points with glulisine and 0.04 with lispro; serious hypoglycemia-related events occurred in 6.8% and 4.4%, respectively, a nonsignificant difference. Treatment satisfaction and antibody outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][9])

Thus, **glulisine and lispro appear clinically similar** for HbA1c and hypoglycemia when used in MDI regimens.

### Ultra-rapid analogues versus established rapid analogues

#### Fast-acting insulin aspart versus insulin aspart

In **onset 1**, mealtime faster aspart reduced HbA1c by an additional 0.15 percentage points and lowered 1- and 2-hour postmeal glucose excursions by 1.18 and 0.67 mmol/L, respectively. Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia and general safety outcomes were similar. Faster aspart administered after meals remained noninferior for HbA1c, although it did not provide the same postprandial benefit as mealtime administration. ([Diabetes Journals][10])

At 52 weeks, the modest HbA1c advantage persisted—estimated difference −0.10 percentage points. ([PMC][11])

In **onset 8**, both mealtime and postmeal faster aspart were noninferior to insulin aspart for HbA1c. Mealtime faster aspart reduced the 1-hour meal-test excursion by 0.90 mmol/L, but produced no clinically important overall HbA1c advantage. ([Wiley Online Library][12])

#### Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro

In **PRONTO-T1D**, mealtime ultra-rapid lispro was noninferior for HbA1c, with an estimated difference of −0.08 percentage points. It reduced 1- and 2-hour postmeal excursions by 1.55 and 1.73 mmol/L. Severe, documented, nocturnal, and overall postprandial hypoglycemia were similar, but hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was 37% less frequent. Injection-site reactions occurred in 2.9% with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro versus 0.2% with lispro. ([PMC][13])

The CGM substudy found approximately **44 additional minutes per day in daytime target range** and about 12 fewer nighttime minutes below 70 mg/dL with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro. ([PubMed][14]) At 52 weeks, HbA1c remained similar, postmeal glucose remained lower, and injection-site reactions occurred in 3.3% versus 0.9%. ([PubMed][15])

Postmeal ultra-rapid lispro met the predefined noninferiority margin, but its endpoint HbA1c was 0.13 percentage points higher than with premeal lispro. This suggests that postmeal administration offers flexibility but may sacrifice some glycemic efficacy. ([PMC][13])

The predominantly Chinese phase III study also established HbA1c noninferiority: HbA1c changed by −0.21% with ultra-rapid lispro and −0.28% with lispro, for a between-group difference of +0.07%, while the study reported improved postprandial control with ultra-rapid lispro. ([Wiley Online Library][16])

## Overall interpretation

For adults using MDI:

* **The most reproducible advantage is lower early postprandial glucose**, especially with mealtime dosing.
* **HbA1c differences are generally small**, commonly between no difference and an approximately 0.1–0.2-percentage-point improvement.
* **Severe and overall hypoglycemia generally do not differ materially.** Some trials suggest less nocturnal or late postmeal hypoglycemia, but these findings are not uniform.
* **Ultra-rapid lispro causes somewhat more injection-site reactions** than conventional lispro.
* Postmeal administration is feasible and usually meets noninferiority margins, but **mealtime administration gives better postprandial control**, and postmeal ultra-rapid lispro sometimes produces slightly higher HbA1c.
* These studies primarily measured surrogate glycemic outcomes, hypoglycemia, treatment satisfaction, and short-term adverse events. They do not establish comparative effects on retinopathy, nephropathy, cardiovascular events, mortality, or other long-term complications.
