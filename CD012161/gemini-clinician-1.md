Transitioning patients from regular human insulin (RHI) to rapid-acting analogues, or upgrading them from standard rapid-acting insulins to newer "ultra-rapid" formulations, involves weighing incremental benefits in postprandial control against specific shifts in hypoglycemia timing.

Based entirely on primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adults with type 1 diabetes on multiple daily injections (MDI) or basal-bolus regimens, here is how the clinical profiles compare.

## Rapid-Acting Analogues vs. Regular Human Insulin

When comparing standard rapid-acting analogues (like insulin aspart) directly to RHI, the primary advantage is a tighter synchronization with carbohydrate absorption, which reduces both post-meal spikes and delayed hypoglycemia.

**Benefits:**

* **Glycemic Control:** Insulin aspart provides a modest but statistically significant improvement in overall HbA1c (a baseline-adjusted difference of ~0.12%) compared to RHI (Home et al., 2000).
* **Postprandial Excursions:** Rapid-acting analogues significantly lower postprandial glucose levels after all main meals.
* **Patient Satisfaction:** Trial data demonstrates that treatment satisfaction scores are significantly higher for patients using rapid analogues compared to RHI, likely due to the flexibility of injecting closer to mealtime rather than 30 minutes prior (Home et al., 2000).

**Harms:**

* **Hypoglycemia Shifts:** While the overall relative risk of major hypoglycemia is similar between the two, RHI's prolonged tail of action carries a distinct hazard. Patients on aspart experience significantly fewer major nocturnal hypoglycemic events requiring parenteral treatment, as well as fewer late post-prandial events 4 to 6 hours after eating (Home et al., 2000).
* **Pre-meal Baseline:** The trade-off for a faster offset of action is that pre-prandial glucose levels before breakfast and dinner can sometimes run slightly higher with rapid analogues if basal insulin is not properly optimized (Home et al., 2000).

## Ultra-Rapid Analogues vs. Standard Rapid-Acting Analogues

The newest generation of mealtime insulins—such as faster aspart (Fiasp) and ultra-rapid lispro (Lyumjev)—add excipients to standard analogues to accelerate subcutaneous absorption. These are primarily compared against standard rapid-acting analogues in recent phase 3 trials (e.g., the *onset* program and *PRONTO-T1D*).

**Benefits:**

* **Enhanced Postprandial Control:** The defining benefit of ultra-rapid formulations is superior control of early post-meal spikes. Across multiple trials, mealtime dosing of ultra-rapid lispro and faster aspart resulted in significantly lower 1-hour and 2-hour postprandial glucose excursions compared to standard lispro and aspart (Buse et al., 2018; Klaff et al., 2020; Russell-Jones et al., 2017).
* **Overall HbA1c:** Ultra-rapid analogues are definitively non-inferior to standard analogues for HbA1c reduction. In a few large trials (like *onset 1*), they even achieved slight, statistically significant superiority in HbA1c lowering at 26 and 52 weeks (Mathieu et al., 2018; Russell-Jones et al., 2017).

**Harms:**

* **Hypoglycemia Timing:** The overall rates of severe or blood-glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia remain comparable between ultra-rapid and standard analogues (Mathieu et al., 2018; Russell-Jones et al., 2017). However, because ultra-rapid insulins leave the bloodstream faster, trials have noted a significantly *lower* rate of late postprandial hypoglycemia (occurring >3 to 4 hours after a meal) compared to standard rapid-acting analogues (Buse et al., 2018; Klaff et al., 2020).
* **Injection Site Reactions:** A mild, unique harm associated with ultra-rapid formulations is a localized reaction driven by the added excipients (such as citrate and treprostinil used in ultra-rapid lispro). For example, in the PRONTO-T1D trial, localized injection site reactions were reported by 2.9% of patients using mealtime ultra-rapid lispro compared to only 0.2% of patients using standard lispro (Klaff et al., 2020).
