## Overview

There's a substantial body of head-to-head randomized trials comparing rapid-acting insulin analogues (lispro, aspart, glulisine) with regular human insulin, and newer trials comparing "ultra-fast" second-generation analogues (faster aspart, ultra-rapid lispro) with their standard analogue counterparts, in adults with type 1 diabetes using MDI. Below is a summary of the main findings by comparison type, followed by the full list of primary studies.

**Rapid-acting analogue vs. regular human insulin** (older analogues: lispro, aspart, glulisine): These trials, mostly from the 1990s–2000s, consistently show modestly lower postprandial glucose excursions and somewhat fewer hypoglycemic episodes with the analogue, with HbA1c differences that are statistically significant in some trials but small in absolute terms (roughly 0.1–0.2%).

**Second-generation ("ultra-fast") analogue vs. first-generation analogue** (faster aspart vs. aspart; ultra-rapid lispro vs. lispro): These more recent trials (the "ONSET" and "PRONTO" programs) generally show non-inferiority for HbA1c, with the ultra-fast formulations showing better 1–2 hour postprandial glucose control but sometimes more injection-site reactions or early post-injection hypoglycemia.

## Rapid-acting analogue vs. regular human insulin

These are the classic trials establishing rapid analogues as an alternative to regular human insulin in MDI regimens.

- **Insulin lispro vs. regular human insulin (adults with T1D, "IDDM"):** In a 6-month, 1,008-patient, 17-country multicenter crossover trial, insulin lispro was injected immediately before the meal and regular human insulin 30–45 minutes before the meal, and throughout the study the postprandial rise in serum glucose was significantly lower during lispro therapy. At endpoint, the postprandial glucose rise was reduced by 1.3 mmol/L at 1 hour and 2.0 mmol/L at 2 hours with lispro, and the hypoglycemia rate was 12% lower with lispro (6.4 vs. 7.2 episodes/30 days), with the largest relative improvement seen at night.

- **Insulin lispro (long-term safety, larger pooled/severe-hypoglycemia data):** A pooled analysis of 2,576 patients with type 1 diabetes found that 3.1% of patients on lispro had severe hypoglycemic attacks versus 4.4% on regular human insulin (Brunelle et al., 1998).

- **Insulin aspart vs. regular human insulin (adults with T1D):** A 6-month multicenter open-label RCT with a 6-month extension (882 subjects, extended to 714) found that subjects administered insulin aspart immediately before meals or regular human insulin 30 minutes before meals, with basal NPH taken as a single bedtime dose in most subjects. Glycemic control was assessed via HbA1c and 8-point glucose profiles (Raskin et al., *Diabetes Care* 2000).

- **Insulin aspart vs. regular human insulin (3-year extension):** A 30-month extension of a multinational, multicenter, open-label, parallel-group study of 753 people with type 1 diabetes originally randomized to insulin aspart or unmodified human insulin before meals (with NPH as basal), evaluating hypoglycemia, adverse events, and HbA1c.

- **Insulin glulisine vs. regular human insulin + glargine (adults with T1D):** Garg, Rosenstock & Ways (2005) compared optimized basal-bolus regimens using glulisine versus regular human insulin, both with basal glargine, in adults with T1D (*Endocr Pract*).

- **Insulin glulisine vs. insulin lispro (adults with T1D):** A multinational, multicenter, open-label RCT of 672 treated adults found a similar reduction in mean HbA1c in both groups (adjusted mean change from baseline −0.14% in both), though basal insulin dose stayed essentially flat with glulisine while it increased with lispro (0.12 vs. 1.82 IU).

## Second-generation "ultra-fast" analogue vs. first-generation analogue

- **Faster aspart vs. insulin aspart — ONSET 1 (26-week, then 52-week):** A 26-week multicenter, treat-to-target, double-blind RCT in adults with T1D (n=381 faster aspart, n=380 aspart, n=382 open-label postmeal faster aspart, all with insulin detemir) found HbA1c was reduced in both groups, with mealtime faster aspart statistically significantly reducing HbA1c versus aspart (P=0.0003), and superior postprandial glucose control for mealtime faster aspart. The 52-week extension confirmed durability: estimated mean HbA1c changes were −0.08% for faster aspart and +0.01% for aspart, significantly favoring faster aspart.

- **Faster aspart vs. insulin aspart — ONSET 8 (with insulin degludec):** In this trial, individuals with T1D were randomized to faster aspart or insulin aspart, both combined with insulin degludec, and noninferiority to mealtime insulin aspart was confirmed for both mealtime and post-meal faster aspart in HbA1c change from baseline.

- **Ultra-rapid lispro (URLi) vs. insulin lispro — PRONTO-T1D (26-week, phase 3):** After an 8-week lead-in, 451 patients were randomized to double-blind mealtime URLi, 442 to lispro, or 329 to open-label postmeal URLi. Both mealtime and postmeal URLi showed non-inferiority to lispro for HbA1c, with mealtime URLi showing a threefold increase in early glucose-lowering activity and onset of action 11 minutes earlier than lispro.

- **PRONTO-T1D continuous glucose monitoring substudy:** Mealtime URLi was superior to mealtime lispro in controlling 1- and 2-hour postprandial glucose excursions during standardized test meals; postmeal URLi provided similar but slightly less optimal control than mealtime URLi.

- **Faster aspart vs. insulin aspart in a closed-loop system (crossover):** A 17-week randomized, open-label crossover trial in adults with T1D using the MiniMed Advanced Hybrid Closed-Loop system compared faster aspart with insulin aspart to test whether the closed-loop system's enhancements would leverage faster aspart's pharmacokinetics to provide superior overall and postprandial glucose control (Klaff et al., published in an ADA journal, 2021). Note: this trial used an insulin pump, not MDI.

- **Faster aspart vs. insulin aspart on non-automated insulin pump with CGM (double-blind crossover):** Forty adults with T1D were randomized to 16 weeks of faster aspart or aspart, with a 3-week washout, then crossed over. Time-in-range was 60.6% for aspart vs. 62.5% for faster aspart at study end (p=0.24, primary endpoint not significant), though faster aspart improved baseline-adjusted secondary metrics but had a higher rate of injection-site reactions. Also a pump-based trial, not MDI, but relevant for comparison.
