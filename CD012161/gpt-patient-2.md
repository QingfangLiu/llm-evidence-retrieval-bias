## Evidence summary

I focused on randomized comparative primary studies in adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus multiple daily injections. Pump, closed-loop, pediatric, pregnancy, pharmacokinetic-clamp-only, and uncontrolled switch studies were excluded.

“Faster-acting insulin” covers two comparisons:

1. **Rapid-acting analogues**—lispro, aspart, or glulisine—versus regular human insulin.
2. **Ultra-rapid formulations**—fast-acting aspart or ultra-rapid lispro—versus conventional rapid analogues.

## What the individual trials showed

### Compared with regular human insulin

**Insulin aspart**

In Home et al., 1,070 adults received insulin aspart or regular human insulin with NPH for six months. Aspart improved HbA1c by 0.12 percentage point and reduced post-meal glucose by 0.6–1.2 mmol/L, or approximately 11–22 mg/dL. Premeal glucose was 0.7–0.8 mmol/L higher before some meals. Major hypoglycemia was not significantly different, while treatment-satisfaction scores favored aspart. ([PubMed][3])

In Raskin et al., 882 participants were followed for six months, with 714 continuing to 12 months. Aspart produced lower post-meal values after breakfast, lunch, and dinner and an HbA1c approximately 0.15 percentage point lower at six months. Overall hypoglycemia and adverse events were similar. ([PubMed][1])

**Insulin lispro**

The results were somewhat heterogeneous:

* Heller et al. reported similar HbA1c but fewer total and nocturnal hypoglycemic episodes with lispro. Because a treatment-period interaction occurred, only the first four-month period was analyzed as a parallel-group comparison, weakening the crossover inference. ([PubMed][7])
* The double-blind UK trial by Gale found better post-breakfast and post-lunch glucose but no HbA1c improvement, no difference in total symptomatic hypoglycemia, and no clear patient preference. Overnight hypoglycemia was less frequent, but morning hypoglycemia was more frequent. ([PubMed][5])
* Annuzzi et al. found HbA1c values of 8.1% with lispro versus 8.3% with regular insulin, better post-meal glucose, similar hypoglycemia, and greater treatment acceptance. This regimen frequently combined NPH with mealtime injections, so it differs from many contemporary basal–bolus regimens. ([PubMed][8])
* In 33 adults with impaired hypoglycemia awareness, severe events were numerically lower with lispro—55 versus 84—but the difference was not statistically significant. ([PubMed][9])

**Insulin glulisine**

A 12-week trial of 860 people compared premeal or postmeal glulisine with regular insulin, all with insulin glargine. Premeal glulisine produced the largest HbA1c decrease, but the study was relatively short for assessing sustained safety or glycemic benefit. ([PubMed][10])

### Compared with another rapid analogue

**Glulisine versus lispro**

In a 26-week trial involving 683 randomized adults, both groups had the same mean HbA1c reduction of 0.14 percentage point. Symptomatic, nocturnal, and severe hypoglycemia were similar. A small difference in total daily insulin dose favored glulisine, but the investigators considered its clinical significance uncertain. ([PubMed][11])

**Fast-acting aspart versus conventional aspart**

In onset 1, mealtime fast-acting aspart lowered HbA1c by an additional 0.15 percentage point and reduced 1-hour post-meal glucose by approximately 21 mg/dL. Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was similar. At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference had narrowed to approximately 0.10 percentage point. ([Diabetes Journals][2])

In onset 8, which used insulin degludec as the basal insulin, mealtime fast-acting aspart and conventional aspart produced nearly identical HbA1c changes. Fast-acting aspart reduced the 1-hour post-meal increment by approximately 16 mg/dL. Overall hypoglycemia was similar, while hypoglycemia during the third to fourth hour after meals was less frequent. Post-meal fast-acting aspart had worse early post-meal control than mealtime dosing. ([PMC][4])

**Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro**

PRONTO-T1D randomized 1,232 adults. Mealtime ultra-rapid lispro reduced 1- and 2-hour meal-test excursions by 1.55 and 1.73 mmol/L, approximately 28 and 31 mg/dL. HbA1c was only 0.08 percentage point lower. Overall severe, documented, and post-meal hypoglycemia were similar; hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was 37% less frequent. Injection-site reactions were more common. ([PubMed][6])

The CGM substudy found approximately 44 additional daytime minutes per day in range and about 12 fewer nighttime minutes below 70 mg/dL with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro, but this involved only 196 participants in the direct mealtime comparison. ([PubMed][12])

At 52 weeks, the small HbA1c difference was no longer statistically significant, but lower 1- and 2-hour post-meal glucose persisted. Overall hypoglycemia remained similar, later hypoglycemia remained less frequent, and injection-site reactions continued to be more common. ([PubMed][13])

## Practical interpretation

A faster or ultra-rapid analogue is most likely to provide a meaningful benefit when the main problem is **early post-meal hyperglycemia**, especially when reliably injecting regular insulin 30 minutes before eating—or a conventional rapid analogue sufficiently before eating—is difficult.

The studies do **not** show a large, dependable HbA1c advantage. The average incremental improvement over the comparator is typically around zero to 0.15 percentage point. They also do not establish a major reduction in severe hypoglycemia, although nocturnal or late post-meal events may decline in some settings.

Potential drawbacks include:

* Earlier glucose lowering when food absorption is delayed or the meal changes after injection.
* Higher fasting or next-premeal glucose if basal insulin or meal dosing is not adjusted.
* More local injection-site reactions with ultra-rapid lispro.
* Slightly poorer HbA1c or early post-meal control when an ultra-rapid insulin is routinely given after rather than at the beginning of meals.

The comparative trials lasted from 12 weeks to approximately one year. They therefore do not determine effects on retinopathy, nephropathy, cardiovascular events, mortality, or uncommon long-term adverse outcomes. ([PubMed][1])
