## Bottom line

For adults with type 1 diabetes using multiple daily injections, the **most reproducible benefit of faster-acting prandial insulin is lower early postprandial glucose**, not a large improvement in HbA1c.

* Compared with **regular human insulin**, conventional rapid-acting analogues—lispro, aspart and glulisine—generally reduce 1–2-hour postmeal excursions and permit dosing immediately before eating rather than 30–45 minutes beforehand. HbA1c improvements were usually small, commonly about **0–0.2 percentage points**. ([PubMed][1])
* Compared with standard rapid analogues, **faster aspart** and **ultra-rapid lispro, or URLi/lispro-aabc**, further improve postmeal glucose. Their HbA1c advantage is generally only **0–0.15 percentage points**. ([Diabetes Journals][2])
* Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was usually similar between treatments. Some studies found less nocturnal, severe or late-postmeal hypoglycemia, but the result was not consistent across trials, and faster insulin can shift hypoglycemia to an earlier postmeal period. ([Amsterdam UMC][3])
* URLi caused more injection-site reactions than lispro, although absolute rates were low. ([PubMed][4])
* No included study demonstrated a reduction in microvascular outcomes, cardiovascular events, mortality or other long-term clinical complications.

## Evidence by comparison

### 1. Rapid-acting analogue versus regular human insulin

#### Insulin lispro

The largest early crossover trial included 1,008 people and found substantially smaller 1- and 2-hour postmeal glucose rises with lispro. Reported hypoglycemia was about 12% lower overall, with the largest relative reduction overnight. ([PubMed][1])

Several smaller studies in well-controlled adults also found fewer nocturnal or severe events:

* Holleman and colleagues reported 36 severe events during lispro treatment versus 58 during regular-insulin treatment, including 3 versus 16 episodes involving coma, without an HbA1c difference. ([Amsterdam UMC][3])
* Heller and colleagues reported fewer nocturnal events with lispro, but because of a treatment-period interaction the primary comparison effectively used only the first crossover period, weakening confidence in the estimate. ([PubMed][5])
* Pfützner and colleagues found lower postmeal glucose, fewer hypoglycemic episodes and better treatment satisfaction, but no HbA1c difference. ([Thieme Connect][6])

Not all lispro studies showed a hypoglycemia advantage. In a double-blind crossover study, total symptomatic hypoglycemia and HbA1c were similar. Lispro reduced overnight events but increased events between 6 a.m. and noon; fasting and premeal glucose also tended to be higher, illustrating that its shorter action can expose inadequate basal coverage. ([PubMed][7])

Other year-long trials found HbA1c differences of approximately 0.2–0.4 percentage points, but some incorporated different NPH schedules or mealtime NPH mixing in the lispro arm. Those results cannot be attributed solely to the faster prandial insulin. ([PubMed][8])

#### Insulin aspart

In a 1,070-participant, 6-month trial, insulin aspart lowered HbA1c by an adjusted additional 0.12 percentage points and lowered postmeal glucose by approximately 0.6–1.2 mmol/L compared with regular insulin. Prebreakfast and predinner glucose were somewhat higher. Major hypoglycemia was not significantly different, although some late-postmeal and nocturnal severe-event categories favored aspart. ([DOI][9])

A separate 882-participant trial found lower glucose after all three meals and an HbA1c difference of approximately 0.15 percentage points at six months. Overall hypoglycemia and adverse events were similar, and the glycemic differences persisted during the extension. ([PubMed][10])

A Japanese phase III study likewise found aspart noninferior for HbA1c, with better 90-minute postbreakfast glucose and comparable safety. ([J-STAGE][11])

A 30-month extension continued to show a small HbA1c advantage, approximately 0.16 percentage points, with no difference in major hypoglycemia. Minor hypoglycemia was more frequent with aspart in that extension, although attrition and the extension design reduce certainty. ([PubMed][12])

#### Insulin glulisine

In 860 adults treated with basal glargine, premeal glulisine produced a modestly greater HbA1c reduction than regular insulin and lower postbreakfast and postdinner glucose. Severe hypoglycemia occurred in 8.4% with premeal glulisine, 8.4% with postmeal glulisine and 10.1% with regular insulin. ([PubMed][13])

### 2. One conventional rapid analogue versus another

A 26-week randomized comparison of glulisine with lispro found essentially identical HbA1c changes, with no clinically relevant differences in overall, nocturnal or severe hypoglycemia. Thus, this trial did not establish a meaningful efficacy or safety advantage for glulisine over lispro. ([PubMed][14])

### 3. Faster aspart versus standard insulin aspart

In the 26-week onset 1 trial:

* Mealtime faster aspart lowered HbA1c by an additional **0.15 percentage points**.
* The 1-hour postmeal glucose increment was **1.18 mmol/L lower**.
* Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia and general adverse-event rates were similar.
* Faster aspart given after the meal remained noninferior for HbA1c but did not perform as well as premeal faster aspart. ([Diabetes Journals][2])

At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference was approximately **0.10 percentage points**, and the 1-hour postmeal advantage was approximately **0.91 mmol/L**, without a difference in overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia. ([PMC][15])

In onset 8, in which participants used insulin degludec as basal insulin, mealtime faster aspart and standard aspart had nearly identical HbA1c results. Faster aspart lowered the 1-hour meal-test excursion by approximately **0.90 mmol/L** and produced fewer hypoglycemic episodes three to four hours after meals, while overall severe or confirmed hypoglycemia remained similar. Postmeal faster aspart had a small HbA1c disadvantage of approximately 0.10 percentage points. ([PMC][16])

### 4. Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro

In the 26-week PRONTO-T1D trial:

* Mealtime URLi was noninferior to lispro for HbA1c, with a small treatment difference of **−0.08 percentage points**.
* Postmeal URLi was also within the prespecified noninferiority margin but had an endpoint HbA1c approximately **0.13 percentage points higher** than lispro.
* Mealtime URLi lowered the 1- and 2-hour postmeal excursions by approximately **1.55 and 1.73 mmol/L**, respectively.
* Severe, documented and total postmeal hypoglycemia were similar.
* Hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was approximately 37% less frequent with mealtime URLi.
* Injection-site reactions occurred in 2.9% with mealtime URLi, 2.4% with postmeal URLi and 0.2% with lispro. ([PubMed][4])

In the continuous-glucose-monitoring substudy, mealtime URLi increased daytime time in range by approximately **44 minutes per day** and reduced nighttime time at or below 70 mg/dL by approximately **12 minutes per day** compared with lispro. ([PubMed][17])

At 52 weeks, HbA1c remained similar, postmeal glucose remained lower, and overall severe or documented hypoglycemia remained similar. Hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was approximately 31% lower, while injection-site reactions remained more common with URLi. ([PubMed][18])

## Practical interpretation

For an adult already using a conventional rapid analogue, switching to an ultra-rapid formulation is most likely to be worthwhile when the principal problem is:

* Persistent early postmeal hyperglycemia despite an appropriate carbohydrate ratio and injection technique.
* Difficulty administering insulin sufficiently before eating.
* Late postmeal hypoglycemia attributable to insulin action persisting after carbohydrate absorption.

A large HbA1c improvement should not be expected from formulation choice alone. Postmeal dosing offers flexibility, but in the randomized trials it was generally slightly less effective than dosing the ultra-rapid insulin at the beginning of the meal. ([Diabetes Journals][2])

After changing from regular insulin or a slower prandial analogue, early postmeal glucose and hypoglycemia should be reviewed because the risk may move earlier even when total hypoglycemia does not increase. Basal adequacy also deserves reassessment: several older lispro and aspart trials found higher fasting or premeal glucose when the shorter prandial action was not fully matched by the basal regimen. ([PubMed][7])

## Important limitations

Most studies lasted 12–52 weeks, were open-label and used HbA1c, self-monitored glucose or meal-test excursions rather than patient-important long-term outcomes. Many older trials used NPH basal insulin and pre-CGM treatment strategies, so their absolute hypoglycemia rates may not transfer directly to contemporary basal-analogue and CGM care. Definitions of severe, confirmed and nocturnal hypoglycemia also varied substantially among trials. ([PubMed][1])
