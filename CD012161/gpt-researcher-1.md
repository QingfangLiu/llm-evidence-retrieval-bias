## Scope

I identified the **long-term randomized evidence set** using these criteria:

* Adults with type 1 diabetes receiving basal–bolus **multiple daily injections**
* A rapid- or ultra-rapid-acting insulin analogue compared with regular human insulin or another rapid/ultra-rapid analogue
* Randomized treatment lasting at least 24 weeks
* Nonpregnant populations
* Pump-only, single-dose clamp, single-meal, pediatric-only, and shorter crossover studies excluded

This produced **15 distinct randomized trials**. Publications reporting extensions, continuous-glucose-monitoring substudies, or pooled trial cohorts are linked to their parent trial rather than counted as new studies.

## Overall findings from the primary studies

### Compared with regular human insulin

**Postprandial glucose is the clearest benefit.** Lispro and aspart generally reduced glucose excursions during the first one to two hours after meals. In the larger studies, the improvement in HbA1c was considerably smaller—typically no difference to approximately 0.1–0.2 percentage points in favor of the analogue. ([PubMed][1])

For example:

* In the pooled lispro trials reported by Anderson et al., the postprandial glucose increment was 35% lower at one hour and 64% lower at two hours; HbA1c was 8.1% versus 8.3%. ([PubMed][1])
* Home et al. found an adjusted HbA1c advantage of 0.12 percentage points and postprandial reductions of approximately 0.6–1.2 mmol/L with aspart. ([DOI][2])
* Raskin et al. found HbA1c values of 7.78% versus 7.93% at six months, with consistently lower post-meal glucose after breakfast, lunch, and dinner. ([PubMed][3])
* In the Japanese aspart trial, the between-group HbA1c change was −0.18 percentage points, with a confidence interval crossing no difference, although 90-minute post-breakfast glucose favored aspart. ([J-STAGE][4])

**Overall hypoglycemia generally did not clearly differ.** Some studies suggested less nocturnal or late post-meal hypoglycemia, but these findings were not consistent. In the high-risk Ferguson trial, lispro produced numerically fewer severe episodes—55 versus 84—and fewer nocturnal severe episodes—25 versus 47—but neither result reached conventional statistical significance. ([PubMed][5])

Home et al. reported no significant reduction in the overall risk of major hypoglycemia, although major nocturnal episodes requiring parenteral treatment and late post-meal episodes were less frequent with aspart. During the longer extension, major hypoglycemia remained similar, while minor hypoglycemia was more frequent with aspart. ([DOI][2])

### Compared with another rapid-acting analogue

**Glulisine and lispro were essentially equivalent.** Both glulisine trials demonstrated noninferior HbA1c control without a convincing hypoglycemia advantage. Dreyer et al. reported an HbA1c reduction of 0.14% in both groups and no relevant difference in symptomatic, nocturnal, or severe hypoglycemia. ([PubMed][6])

In the Japanese trial, hypoglycemia-related serious adverse events occurred in 6.8% with glulisine and 4.4% with lispro, a nonsignificant difference. ([PubMed][7])

**Ultra-rapid formulations improve early postprandial control relative to conventional aspart or lispro.** The HbA1c differences were generally small:

* In onset 1, mealtime faster aspart reduced HbA1c by an additional 0.15 percentage points and reduced one-hour postprandial excursion by 1.18 mmol/L compared with aspart. ([Diabetes Journals][8])
* In onset 8, the HbA1c difference was −0.02 percentage points, while the one-hour postprandial excursion was 0.90 mmol/L lower. ([PMC][9])
* In PRONTO-T1D, mealtime ultra-rapid lispro reduced one- and two-hour excursions by 1.55 and 1.73 mmol/L, respectively, but its HbA1c advantage was only 0.08 percentage points. ([PubMed][10])
* In the predominantly Chinese trial, ultra-rapid lispro was noninferior for HbA1c and reduced one- and two-hour excursions by approximately 1.0 and 1.4 mmol/L. ([Wiley Online Library][11])

**Overall severe or confirmed hypoglycemia was generally similar.** Faster aspart sometimes produced less hypoglycemia three to four hours after meals, and ultra-rapid lispro produced less hypoglycemia more than four hours after meals. These findings may reflect the shorter late glucose-lowering tail rather than a reduction in total risk. ([Sage Journals][12])

**Injection-site reactions were a specific harm with ultra-rapid lispro.** In PRONTO-T1D, reactions occurred in 2.9% with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro versus 0.2% with lispro; at 52 weeks the corresponding figures were 3.3% and 0.9%. ([PubMed][10])

**Dosing time matters.** Post-meal faster aspart generally retained noninferior HbA1c control, but post-meal ultra-rapid lispro had a statistically higher HbA1c than premeal lispro in PRONTO-T1D. Thus, the evidence for flexible post-meal administration should not be interpreted as equivalence under every formulation and dosing circumstance. ([Diabetes Journals][8])

## Evidence limitations

Most trials were open-label and treat-to-target. Differences in basal insulin, meal timing, titration algorithms, hypoglycemia definitions, and glucose-monitoring technology limit direct comparisons across trials. Follow-up was usually 24–52 weeks; therefore, these studies do not establish effects on mortality, microvascular or macrovascular complications, or uncommon long-term adverse events. The older lispro datasets also have a publication-unit problem: separate underlying trials were sometimes pooled into a single article, so trial-level data are not always independently extractable.

## Primary studies

1. **Ferguson SC, Strachan MWJ, Janes JM, Frier BM.** Severe hypoglycaemia in patients with type 1 diabetes and impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia: a comparative study of insulin lispro and regular human insulin. *Diabetes Metab Res Rev.* 2001;17(4):285–291. doi:10.1002/dmrr.202. Randomized 12-month crossover study; 33 participants. ([PubMed][5])

2. **Provenzano C, Vero R, Oliva A, et al.** Lispro insulin in type 1 diabetic patients on a Mediterranean or normal diet: a randomized, cross-over comparative study with regular insulin. *Diabetes Nutr Metab.* 2001;14(3):133–139. Twelve-person, four-period, one-year crossover study. ([ResearchGate][13])

3. **Recasens M, Aguilera E, Morínigo R, et al.** Insulin lispro is as effective as regular insulin in optimising metabolic control and preserving beta-cell function at onset of type 1 diabetes mellitus. *Diabetes Res Clin Pract.* 2003;60(3):153–159. doi:10.1016/S0168-8227(03)00036-6. Randomized 12-month study; 45 newly diagnosed participants. ([PubMed][14])

4. **Trial Z011.** Insulin lispro versus regular human insulin; 12 months. This was one of the underlying sponsor trials reported in pooled form by Anderson et al. Trial-level results are not fully separable in the journal publication. ([PubMed][1])

5. **Trial Z013.** Insulin lispro versus regular human insulin; 12 months. Reported together with Z011 in: **Anderson JH Jr, Brunelle RL, Koivisto VA, et al.** Improved mealtime treatment of diabetes mellitus using an insulin analogue. *Clin Ther.* 1997;19(1):62–72. doi:10.1016/S0149-2918(97)80073-2. The pooled type 1 diabetes cohort contained 336 participants. ([PubMed][1])

6. **Trial Z015.** Insulin lispro versus regular human insulin; 12 months. Principal publication: **Garg SK, Carmain JA, Braddy KC, et al.** Pre-meal insulin analogue insulin lispro vs Humulin R insulin treatment in young subjects with type 1 diabetes. *Diabet Med.* 1996;13(1):47–52. doi:10.1002/(SICI)1096-9136(199601)13:1<47::AID-DIA999>3.0.CO;2-M. 7. **Home PD, Lindholm A, Riis A; European Insulin Aspart Study Group.** Insulin aspart vs human insulin in the management of long-term blood glucose control in type 1 diabetes mellitus: a randomized controlled trial. *Diabet Med.* 2000;17(11):762–770. doi:10.1046/j.1464-5491.2000.00380.x. Six-month RCT; 1,070 adults. ([PubMed][16])

Twenty-four-week RCT; 205 participants. ([J-STAGE][4])

9. **Raskin P, Guthrie RA, Leiter L, Riis A, Jovanovic L.** Use of insulin aspart, a fast-acting insulin analog, as the mealtime insulin in the management of patients with type 1 diabetes. *Diabetes Care.* 2000;23(5):583–588. doi:10.2337/diacare.23.5.583.
