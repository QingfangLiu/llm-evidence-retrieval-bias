## Bottom line

These studies concern **blood-pressure management during roughly the first 24–72 hours after reperfusion treatment**, not long-term blood-pressure control for preventing another stroke.

After **successful mechanical thrombectomy**, routinely forcing systolic blood pressure to a fixed low target—particularly **below 120–140 mm Hg**—has generally **not reduced symptomatic brain bleeding** and has sometimes caused **worse neurological and functional recovery**. The clearest harms occurred in ENCHANTED2/MT and OPTIMAL-BP. ([PubMed][1])

A newer study, HOPE, found better outcomes with a **personalized strategy based on how completely blood flow was restored**, but this was not simply aggressive lowering: patients with incomplete reperfusion were kept at a higher range, and vasopressors could be used to prevent pressure from falling too low. The study also stopped before reaching its revised sample size, so its authors considered the findings hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. ([JAMA Network][2])

For patients treated with **intravenous alteplase**, ENCHANTED found that targeting 130–140 mm Hg reduced “any intracranial hemorrhage” but did **not improve disability or functional recovery**. ([PubMed][3])

## Potential benefits and risks

### Possible benefits of more intensive lowering

The proposed benefit is less reperfusion-related vascular injury and hemorrhagic transformation.

* In **ENCHANTED after alteplase**, any intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 14.8% with intensive treatment versus 18.7% with standard treatment—an absolute reduction of 3.9 percentage points. However, 90-day functional outcomes were identical. ([PubMed][3])
* In **HOPE after thrombectomy**, the personalized group had less hemorrhagic transformation, 22.3% versus 31.6%, and more patients reached functional independence, 60.0% versus 47.1%. Symptomatic hemorrhage and mortality were unchanged. ([JAMA Network][2])
* The other thrombectomy trials generally did **not** demonstrate a meaningful reduction in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage with intensive fixed targets. ([PubMed][4])

### Important risks

The concern is that damaged brain tissue may remain dependent on systemic blood pressure even after the main artery appears reopened. Excessive or rapid reduction can reduce microvascular or collateral perfusion.

* **ENCHANTED2/MT:** targeting below 120 mm Hg produced worse 90-day disability, more early neurological deterioration, and more major disability, without reducing symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage. ([PubMed][1])
* **OPTIMAL-BP:** targeting below 140 mm Hg resulted in functional independence in 39.4%, versus 54.4% with a 140–180 target—approximately 15 fewer functionally independent patients per 100 treated intensively. Malignant cerebral edema was also more frequent, 7.7% versus 1.3%. Symptomatic hemorrhage was essentially unchanged. ([JAMA Network][5])
* **BP-TARGET:** the 100–129 mm Hg target did not reduce radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Hypotensive events occurred in 8% versus 3%, although this difference was not statistically conclusive. ([PubMed][4])
* Smaller studies did not establish definite harm, but they also found little evidence of functional benefit. ([JAMA Network][6])

## How this applies clinically

For a patient whose artery was reopened by thrombectomy, the primary-trial evidence argues against **automatically driving SBP below 120 or below 140 mm Hg for everyone**. The appropriate range may depend on:

* Whether reperfusion was complete, near complete, or only partial.
* Residual intracranial or extracranial stenosis.
* Chronic hypertension and impaired autoregulation.
* Infarct size, cerebral edema and hemorrhagic transformation.
* Whether pressure is falling spontaneously versus requiring medication.
* Neurological worsening temporally associated with BP reduction.

The HOPE findings support studying individualized ranges, but they do not yet establish that its protocol should replace standard management in every patient. Acute targets should be determined by the treating stroke/neurocritical-care team rather than by changing home medications independently.
