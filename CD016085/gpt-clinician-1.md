# Intensive versus standard BP control after reperfusion therapy for ischemic stroke

I searched for comparative **primary interventional studies**, prioritizing randomized trials. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, committee statements, and editorials. Evidence is current through **July 25, 2026**.

## Bottom line

**After IV thrombolysis:** Targeting systolic BP (SBP) around **130–140 mm Hg** reduced radiographic intracranial hemorrhage, but it did **not improve 90-day functional outcomes**. A prespecified severe-stroke analysis raised a possible mortality concern.

**After successful endovascular thrombectomy:** A fixed intensive target—particularly **<120, <130, or <140 mm Hg**—has generally provided **no hemorrhage benefit** and has sometimes worsened neurological or functional recovery. Hypotension, early neurological deterioration, and cerebral edema are potential harms.

**Important new exception:** The 2026 HOPE trial found benefit from a **reperfusion-grade–tailored strategy**, not uniform aggressive lowering. Patients with incomplete reperfusion were maintained at a higher SBP range, while those with near-complete/complete reperfusion received a lower target.

## Individual trial findings

### 1. IV thrombolysis: ENCHANTED

ENCHANTED randomized 2,227 alteplase-eligible patients with SBP ≥150 mm Hg to an SBP target of **130–140 mm Hg within one hour and for 72 hours** or standard management targeting **<180 mm Hg**.

There was no difference in the 90-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) distribution: odds ratio (OR) 1.01, 95% CI 0.87–1.17. Intensive treatment reduced any intracranial hemorrhage from **18.7% to 14.8%**, an absolute reduction of 3.9 percentage points, without significantly reducing serious adverse events. The achieved mean 24-hour SBP difference was relatively modest: approximately 144 versus 150 mm Hg. ([PubMed][1])

**Interpretation:** Lowering SBP toward 130–140 mm Hg after alteplase can reduce radiographic bleeding, but this did not translate into better disability outcomes.

A prespecified analysis of 1,311 ENCHANTED participants classified as having severe stroke found no improvement in death or disability. Intensive treatment reduced clinician-reported intracranial hemorrhage but was associated with higher mortality: OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.09–2.13. Because this was a subgroup analysis using a heterogeneous severe-stroke definition, it should be considered a safety signal rather than definitive proof of mortality harm. ([PubMed][2])

### 2. Successful thrombectomy: BP-TARGET

BP-TARGET randomized 324 patients after successful endovascular treatment to an SBP target of **100–129 mm Hg** or **130–185 mm Hg** for 24 hours.

Radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage occurred in **42% versus 43%**, adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.60–1.51. Hypotensive events occurred in 8% versus 3%, although the difference was not statistically significant. Mean SBP was approximately 128 versus 138 mm Hg. ([PubMed][3])

**Interpretation:** Targeting 100–129 mm Hg did not prevent post-reperfusion hemorrhage and produced a numerical increase in hypotension.

### 3. Successful thrombectomy: ENCHANTED2/MT

ENCHANTED2/MT randomized 821 patients with successful recanalization and persistent SBP ≥140 mm Hg to **<120 mm Hg** or **140–180 mm Hg** for 72 hours. The trial was stopped early because of efficacy and safety concerns.

Intensive treatment worsened the 90-day mRS distribution: common OR for a worse outcome 1.37, 95% CI 1.07–1.76. It also increased:

* Early neurological deterioration: OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.18–1.97.
* Major disability at 90 days: OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.47–2.93.

There was no significant reduction in symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, serious adverse events, or mortality. ([ScienceDirect][4])

**Interpretation:** An SBP target below 120 mm Hg after thrombectomy was harmful to neurological and functional recovery.

### 4. Successful thrombectomy: OPTIMAL-BP

OPTIMAL-BP randomized 306 patients after successful reperfusion to **<140 mm Hg** or **140–180 mm Hg** for 24 hours. The trial was stopped early following safety concerns.

Functional independence at three months occurred in **39.4% versus 54.4%**, an absolute difference of −15.1 percentage points; adjusted OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.96. Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage was similar—9.0% versus 8.1%—as was stroke-related mortality. Malignant cerebral edema was more frequent with intensive treatment, **7.7% versus 1.3%**. ([PubMed][5])

At one year, functional independence remained numerically lower with intensive management: **40.5% versus 52.7%**, adjusted OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.34–1.00, P=0.051. The per-protocol analysis was statistically significant. Mortality and changes occurring between three months and one year did not differ. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** Even an SBP target below 140 mm Hg can impair recovery after successful thrombectomy, and the functional effect may persist.

### 5. Successful thrombectomy: BEST-II

BEST-II was a 120-patient phase 2 futility trial comparing SBP targets of **<140, <160, and ≤180 mm Hg** for 24 hours.

It did not meet its prespecified statistical boundaries for futility or harm based on infarct volume and utility-weighted 90-day mRS. Nevertheless, the modeled probabilities that a future phase 3 trial would demonstrate benefit were only **25% for <140 mm Hg** and **14% for <160 mm Hg**. Symptomatic hemorrhage was similar across groups. ([PubMed][7])

**Interpretation:** BEST-II did not conclusively demonstrate harm, but provided little support for a clinically meaningful benefit from routine targets below 140 or 160 mm Hg.

### 6. Successful thrombectomy: DETECT feasibility trial

DETECT randomized 30 patients with persistently elevated BP after successful thrombectomy to **<140 mm Hg** or **<180 mm Hg** for 48 hours.

Mean SBP was approximately 131 versus 139 mm Hg, and no differences were identified in the prespecified exploratory neurological, functional, hemorrhagic, or flow-dynamic outcomes. Target separation was difficult because BP frequently declined spontaneously; only 48% of measurements in the standard group were between 140 and 180 mm Hg. ([PubMed][8])

**Interpretation:** This small trial primarily demonstrated feasibility limitations and cannot establish clinical benefit or safety. The publication also contains a meta-analysis, which I did not use here.

### 7. Successful thrombectomy: IDENTIFY

IDENTIFY randomized 383 patients after successful anterior-circulation reperfusion to **<130 mm Hg** or **<180 mm Hg** until 24 hours after thrombectomy.

Unfavorable outcome, mRS 3–6, occurred in **71.0% versus 67.5%**, risk ratio 1.05, 95% CI 0.92–1.20. Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, malignant edema, and mortality were not significantly reduced. Hypotension within 24 hours was substantially more frequent with intensive control, **57.5% versus 32.7%**. Adjusted secondary analyses also suggested more severe disability, although the primary outcome was neutral. ([ScienceDirect][9])

**Interpretation:** Targeting below 130 mm Hg offered no primary functional benefit and markedly increased hypotensive episodes.

### 8. Reperfusion-guided management: HOPE

HOPE randomized 440 evaluable patients after successful anterior-circulation thrombectomy to a target determined by final reperfusion grade:

* **mTICI 2b:** SBP 140–160 mm Hg.
* **mTICI 2c/3:** SBP 100–140 mm Hg.
* Control: SBP <180 mm Hg.

Targets were maintained for 72 hours using antihypertensives or vasopressors when necessary.

Functional independence at 90 days occurred in **60.0% versus 47.1%**, an absolute difference of 13.3 percentage points, 95% CI 4.1–22.6. Hemorrhagic transformation was reduced from **31.6% to 22.3%**, OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41–0.95. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage—3.5% versus 3.9%—and mortality—15.4% versus 15.6%—were similar. ([PubMed][10])

**Interpretation:** HOPE supports avoiding a one-size-fits-all low target. Its benefit came from **matching the BP range to reperfusion completeness**, including actively avoiding low pressures after incomplete reperfusion. Because it is a single recent open-label trial conducted in Spain, external replication would strengthen confidence in the strategy.

## Clinical synthesis

The clearest pattern is:

* **Radiographic bleeding and functional recovery are not interchangeable outcomes.** ENCHANTED reduced hemorrhage after alteplase without improving disability.
* **After thrombectomy, lowering BP more is not necessarily neuroprotective.** Fixed targets below 120–140 mm Hg did not consistently reduce symptomatic hemorrhage and sometimes worsened recovery.
* **Potential harms include hypotension, reduced residual/collateral or microvascular perfusion, early neurological deterioration, malignant edema, and greater disability.** The perfusion mechanism is biologically plausible but was not directly established by most trials.
* **Reperfusion grade matters.** HOPE suggests that patients with incomplete reperfusion may require a higher BP range than patients with near-complete or complete reperfusion.
* These studies generally enrolled patients whose SBP was elevated after treatment. They do not establish that BP should be pharmacologically raised in every patient with spontaneous SBP below the trial targets.
