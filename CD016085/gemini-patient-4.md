First, I am sorry you had to go through a stroke, but it is great to hear you received treatment to successfully reopen the blocked artery (reperfusion therapy). Managing your recovery and understanding the science behind your care is an incredibly empowering step.

When doctors restore blood flow to the brain—either by using a clot-busting drug (intravenous thrombolysis) or physically removing the clot (endovascular thrombectomy)—managing blood pressure immediately afterward becomes a delicate balancing act.

Historically, doctors wondered if strictly lowering blood pressure (intensive control) would prevent the newly opened blood vessels from leaking or bleeding. However, recent primary clinical trials have revealed a critical shift in understanding: **intensive blood pressure lowering after treatment is generally not recommended because it can actually worsen functional recovery.**

Here is a breakdown of the benefits and risks based on the primary studies you requested.

## The Balancing Act: Why Blood Pressure Matters

After a stroke, a region of brain tissue around the core injury (the *penumbra*) is extremely vulnerable. It relies on adequate blood pressure to push blood into these struggling areas.

* **The Risk of High Blood Pressure:** Can lead to brain swelling or bleeding (hemorrhage) into the stroke area.
* **The Risk of Low Blood Pressure (Intensive Control):** Can starve the vulnerable brain tissue of oxygen, expanding the size of the permanent stroke injury (hypoperfusion).

## What the Primary Trials Show

The medical community has tested intensive blood pressure targets (usually aiming for a systolic number below 120, 130, or 140 mm Hg) against standard targets (allowing the systolic number to sit a bit higher, often between 140 and 180 mm Hg).

### 1. After Clot-Busting Medication (Thrombolysis)

* **Benefits:** The **ENCHANTED** trial found that strictly lowering blood pressure (below 130–140 mm Hg) did slightly reduce the risk of bleeding in the brain (Anderson et al., 2019).
* **Risks / Lack of Benefit:** Despite the lower bleeding risk, patients who received intensive blood pressure control did *not* have better physical or functional recovery at 90 days compared to standard control (Anderson et al., 2019).

### 2. After Mechanical Clot Removal (Endovascular Thrombectomy)

The evidence here is even more definitive—and points to significant harm from aggressive blood pressure lowering following clot removal.

* **Harmful Outcomes:** The **ENCHANTED2/MT** trial compared an intensive target (below 120 mm Hg) to a standard target (140–180 mm Hg). The trial was stopped early because the intensive group had significantly worse functional recovery and a higher rate of early neurological deterioration (Yang et al., 2022).
* **Confirmation of Harm:** Following this, the **OPTIMAL-BP** trial (aiming below 140 mm Hg) was also stopped early for similar safety concerns. Patients in the intensive lowering group were much less likely to have a favorable recovery (Nam et al., 2023).
* **No Bleeding Benefit:** Unlike the thrombolysis studies, trials involving mechanical clot removal, such as **BP-TARGET** and **BEST-II**, showed that intensive blood pressure control did *not* reduce the rates of bleeding in the brain, offering no upside to offset the risks (Mazighi et al., 2021; Mistry et al., 2023).

### The Takeaway

For patients who have had successful procedures to restore blood flow, pushing blood pressure down too far or too fast is risky. Allowing the blood pressure to run slightly higher (often within the 140–180 mm Hg range, depending on the exact procedure and hospital protocol) seems to better support the brain's healing tissues.
