## Summary of the evidence

Multiple RCTs have tested intensive vs. standard blood pressure (BP) targets in patients who achieved successful reperfusion (mainly via endovascular thrombectomy) for acute ischemic stroke. The overall picture:

- **No benefit shown**: BP‑TARGET (SBP 100–129 vs. 130–185 mmHg) found intensive lowering did not reduce radiographic intraparenchymal haemorrhage rates at 24–36 h compared with standard care.
- **Harm shown**: Two larger trials found intensive lowering worsened outcomes. OPTIMAL-BP found a lower proportion achieving functional independence with intensive (39.4%) vs. conventional (54.4%) management. ENCHANTED2/MT, the largest trial, was stopped early because intensive lowering to a systolic target of less than 120 mm Hg led to worse functional outcomes than a less intensive target of 140–180 mm Hg, and was stopped early due to safety concerns.
- **Neutral/no clear signal**: BEST-II was a phase 2 futility trial with three arms (<140, <160, ≤180 mmHg) that found futility criteria for lower SBP targets were not met. The more recent IDENTIFY trial (intensive <130 vs. standard <180 mmHg, EVT within 6h) found intensive BP management did not improve outcomes, with no significant difference in unfavourable functional outcome, symptomatic ICH, malignant brain oedema, or all-cause death at 90 days.
- A pooled meta-analysis of these trials concluded intensive control was associated with lower rates of good functional outcome and increased all-cause mortality — but note this is a meta-analysis, not a primary study, and is included here only for context, not in your requested list.

Trial results have varied somewhat by timing (early vs. late reperfusion), target thresholds, and population, so the "optimal" target remains actively debated. Since you've had a stroke yourself, I want to flag that these trials involve carefully monitored in-hospital BP management protocols decided by your treating team — this isn't something to interpret as guidance for managing your own blood pressure at home; that should come directly from your stroke team, who know your specific case (occlusion location, time to reperfusion, comorbidities).
