As an evidence-synthesis researcher, you need the raw outcomes from primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) rather than pre-digested reviews. The current evidence regarding blood pressure (BP) control after successful reperfusion for acute ischemic stroke hinges heavily on the modality of the reperfusion—intravenous thrombolysis versus endovascular thrombectomy (EVT).

Here is a synthesis of the primary RCTs evaluating intensive versus standard BP targets, followed by the reference list.

## Intravenous Thrombolysis

Following thrombolysis, intensive BP lowering has demonstrated a specific safety benefit regarding hemorrhage, though it lacks functional efficacy.

* **The ENCHANTED Trial:** This trial evaluated intensive BP lowering (targeting 130–140 mm Hg) versus standard guideline-recommended targets (<180 mm Hg) in patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis (Anderson et al., 2019).
* **Findings (Benefits/Risks):** Intensive BP control safely reduced the risk of any intracranial hemorrhage. However, it did not translate to an improvement in functional outcomes or reduced disability at 90 days (Anderson et al., 2019).

## Endovascular Thrombectomy (EVT)

For patients undergoing EVT, a consensus of recent primary trials demonstrates significant harm associated with intensive BP lowering. In fact, multiple trials have been halted early due to safety concerns.

* **The OPTIMAL-BP Trial:** This trial compared early intensive BP management (systolic target <140 mm Hg) to conventional management (140–180 mm Hg) during the first 24 hours after successful EVT reperfusion (Nam et al., 2023).
* **Findings (Benefits/Risks):** The trial was stopped early by its safety monitoring board because the intensive management group had significantly lower rates of functional independence at 3 months (39.4% vs. 54.4% in the conventional group), establishing that aggressive lowering is harmful post-EVT (Nam et al., 2023).
* **The ENCHANTED2/MT Trial:** This study tested a highly intensive target (<120 mm Hg) against a less intensive target (140–180 mm Hg) after successful EVT (Yang et al., 2022).
* **Findings (Benefits/Risks):** Also stopped early due to persistent safety and efficacy concerns, this trial found that patients in the intensive group suffered more early neurological deterioration and had a twofold increase in major disability at 90 days compared to the standard group (Yang et al., 2022).
* **The BEST-II Trial:** This phase 2 futility trial investigated lowering systolic targets to <140 mm Hg or <160 mm Hg compared to the standard <180 mm Hg post-EVT (Mistry et al., 2023).
* **Findings (Benefits/Risks):** While the lower targets did not definitively meet the prespecified criteria for futility, they showed a remarkably low predicted probability of benefit for any future superiority trial (14% to 25%), and trended toward worse functional outcomes and larger follow-up infarct volumes at lower BP targets (Mistry et al., 2023).

## The Physiological Rationale for Harm

The prevailing consensus from the EVT trials is that dropping blood pressure too aggressively exacerbates ischemic injury. Even after successful large-vessel recanalization, the surrounding ischemic penumbra may remain in a vulnerable, oligemic state with severely impaired autoregulation. When systemic blood pressure drops rapidly, cerebral blood flow to these specific vulnerable areas decreases, effectively starving the tissue and worsening long-term functional recovery (Nam et al., 2023).
