Recent primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have fundamentally shifted the clinical understanding of blood pressure (BP) management following successful reperfusion in acute ischemic stroke (AIS).

While observational data historically suggested that lowering blood pressure might reduce reperfusion injury and hemorrhage, high-quality primary studies indicate that the risks of intensive BP lowering significantly outweigh the theoretical benefits.

## Risks of Intensive BP Lowering

* **Reduced Functional Independence:** Multiple trials have demonstrated that intensive BP targets actually *worsen* functional recovery. In the OPTIMAL-BP trial, lowering systolic BP to an intensive target of <140 mm Hg resulted in a significantly lower proportion of patients achieving functional independence (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0–2) at 3 months (39.4%) compared to conventional management targeting 140–180 mm Hg (54.4%) (Nam et al., 2023). Follow-up data showed this functional disadvantage persisted at 1 year.
* **Early Neurological Deterioration and Mortality:** The ENCHANTED2/MT trial evaluated an even stricter intensive target (<120 mm Hg) against a standard 140–180 mm Hg target. The trial was stopped early because the intensive target severely compromised functional recovery and significantly increased the odds of early neurological deterioration and death at 90 days (Yang et al., 2022).

## Lack of Established Benefits

* **Intracranial Hemorrhage (ICH):** The primary theoretical benefit of intensive BP lowering is the prevention of symptomatic ICH and parenchymal hematomas. However, the data show no clinical advantage. In the OPTIMAL-BP trial, rates of symptomatic ICH were statistically indistinguishable between the intensive group (9.0%) and the conventional group (8.1%) (Nam et al., 2023).
* **Infarct Volume Reduction:** The BEST-II trial assessed whether intensive targets (<140 mm Hg or <160 mm Hg) could reduce follow-up infarct volumes compared to standard guideline-recommended targets (≤180 mm Hg). The intensive targets failed to show meaningful reductions in infarct volume. Furthermore, predictive modeling within the trial concluded there was a low probability (futility) that these lower targets would yield any clinical benefit in larger superiority trials (Mistry et al., 2023).

## Summary

The consensus across recent primary evidence is that intensive blood pressure reduction (e.g., systolic targets <120, <140, or <160 mm Hg) following successful endovascular thrombectomy should be avoided. The data strongly suggest that inducing relative hypotension in brain tissue that recently suffered ischemia exacerbates hypoperfusion and impairs collateral flow, ultimately resulting in durable harm to the patient's functional recovery.
