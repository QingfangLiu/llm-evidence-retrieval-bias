Recovering from an ischemic stroke is a major physiological event, and it is completely understandable that you want to grasp the clinical reasoning behind your treatment protocol.

When a blocked artery in the brain is reopened—a process called **reperfusion**, achieved via clot-busting drugs (thrombolysis) or mechanical removal (thrombectomy)—managing blood pressure in the immediate aftermath is critical. Intuitively, it might seem safer to lower blood pressure as much as possible to prevent bleeding into the newly opened vessels. However, recent primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) overwhelmingly show that **intensive blood pressure lowering is harmful** compared to standard, more permissive control.

Here is the breakdown of why this happens, along with the primary clinical trials that established this standard.

## Why Intensive Lowering Can Be Harmful

After a stroke, the brain tissue surrounding the core injury—known as the **ischemic penumbra**—remains highly vulnerable.

Normally, the brain regulates its own blood flow regardless of your body's overall blood pressure (a process called *cerebral autoregulation*). However, an ischemic stroke damages this regulatory mechanism. In the critical 24 hours following reperfusion, the recovering brain tissue relies directly on higher systemic blood pressure to push blood through the newly opened vessels and into the delicate microcirculation.

If blood pressure is lowered too aggressively (intensive control), the blood pressure drops below the threshold needed to perfuse the brain. This starves the recovering tissue of oxygen and expands the volume of the stroke, leading to significantly worse physical and neurological recovery.

## The Evidence: Intensive vs. Standard Control

Recent phase 2 and phase 3 randomized clinical trials have tested whether driving systolic blood pressure (SBP) down to intensive targets (<120 or <140 mm Hg) is better than the standard guideline-recommended target (maintaining SBP ≤180 mm Hg).

The results have consistently favored the standard approach:

| Trial | Intensive Target | Standard Target | Key Finding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ENCHANTED2/MT** | < 120 mm Hg | 140–180 mm Hg | The trial was stopped early for safety reasons. Patients in the intensive group suffered worse functional recovery and quality of life at 3 months (Yang et al., 2022). |
| **OPTIMAL-BP** | < 140 mm Hg | 140–180 mm Hg | Intensive lowering led to a significantly lower likelihood of functional independence (39.4% vs. 54.4% in the standard group) at 3 months (Nam et al., 2023). |
| **BEST-II** | < 140 or < 160 mm Hg | ≤ 180 mm Hg | Aggressive lowering did not decrease stroke volume and met criteria for futility, meaning there is virtually no statistical probability that future trials would find a benefit to intensive targets (Mistry et al., 2023). |

In short, standard blood pressure control—which allows blood pressure to remain relatively elevated (up to 180 mm Hg) while avoiding extreme spikes—is currently the safest and most effective standard of care to protect the brain as it heals.
