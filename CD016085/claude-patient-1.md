Let me pull each individual RCT directly.Here's what the primary trial evidence shows on blood pressure targets after successful reperfusion therapy (endovascular thrombectomy) for acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion. All are randomized controlled trials — no reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines.

## What the trials tested

All five trials asked the same basic question: after your artery is successfully reopened, is pushing blood pressure lower than the traditional target (roughly <180 mmHg systolic) better or worse than keeping it in that traditional range?

**BP-TARGET (2021)** — the first of these trials — randomized 318 patients and found intraparenchymal hemorrhage rates on imaging were the outcome studied, comparing a target of 100–129 mmHg against 130–185 mmHg. It found no effect of early intensive BP lowering on outcomes.

**ENCHANTED2/MT (2022)**, conducted at 44 tertiary hospitals in China, randomized patients with persistently elevated systolic blood pressure after successful thrombectomy to a more intensive target below 120 mmHg or a less intensive target of 140–180 mmHg, to be reached within an hour and maintained for 72 hours. This trial was stopped early because the intensive-lowering group was doing worse.

**OPTIMAL-BP (2023)**, run at 19 stroke centers in South Korea, enrolled 306 patients with large vessel occlusion stroke who had achieved partial or complete reperfusion, randomizing them to an intensive systolic target below 140 mmHg or a conventional target of 140–180 mmHg. Functional independence at 3 months was achieved by 39.4% of the intensive group versus 54.4% of the conventional group — a significant difference favoring conventional control, meaning intensive lowering appeared harmful.

**BEST-II (2023)**, run at three US comprehensive stroke centers, was a smaller phase 2 futility trial that randomized 120 patients to systolic BP targets across a range of intensities. It was designed to test whether lower targets could be ruled out as futile — and it did not clearly rule that out, again pointing away from benefit from more intensive lowering.

**IDENTIFY (2025)**, run at 63 stroke centers in China in patients who had thrombectomy within 6 hours, randomized patients to an intensive systolic target below 130 mmHg or a standard target below 180 mmHg. Existing evidence did not support intensive BP management after successful reperfusion regardless of how long the artery had been blocked before treatment; where recanalization was late, intensive management was actively harmful in ENCHANTED2/MT and OPTIMAL-BP, while where recanalization was earlier, BP-TARGET and IDENTIFY found no significant effect either way.

## Bottom line across these trials

The consistent pattern is: **intensive blood pressure lowering after successful reperfusion does not improve outcomes, and in trials with longer delays before reperfusion, it appears to worsen functional recovery** (fewer patients regaining independence), without a clear reduction in bleeding risk. None of the trials showed a benefit large enough to justify aggressive lowering; several showed harm signals. This is why standard, more permissive BP targets (generally up to 180 mmHg systolic) have remained the conventional approach rather than being replaced by tighter targets.

Your own target BP, medications, and timeline should be individualized by your treating stroke team based on how quickly your artery was reopened, your imaging findings, and your overall cardiovascular risk — the trials above found population-level results, not guidance that applies uniformly to everyone.
