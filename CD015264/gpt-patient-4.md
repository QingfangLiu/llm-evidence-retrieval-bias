I found **11 primary-study reports from two randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials** that directly gave vitamin B12 to children under 12. I grouped reports from the same trial so they are not mistaken for independent participant samples.

I included oral supplements or fortified pastes in which the comparison was placebo or an otherwise identical supplement without B12. I excluded maternal supplementation, observational studies, protocols, reviews, and studies comparing oral with injected B12 rather than with placebo/no B12.

## 1. Nepal infant trial — NCT02272842

**Population/intervention:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months in Bhaktapur, Nepal. Children received **2 µg vitamin B12 daily for 12 months** in a micronutrient paste or the same paste without B12.

| Primary publication                                                                                                                                                                                                  | Outcomes and findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Strand TA, et al. Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese infants. PLoS Medicine. 2020;17:e1003430. PMID 33259482.**                                                     | Main trial report. B12 improved biochemical status: methylmalonic acid and homocysteine were substantially lower than with placebo. It did **not improve Bayley developmental scores, length or weight growth, or hemoglobin**. Adverse events and hospitalizations were not attributed to supplementation. ([PubMed][1]) |

Mean WPPSI full-scale IQ was 84.4 with B12 and 85.0 with placebo, difference −0.5 points, 95% CI −1.97 to 0.94. ([PubMed][2])                                                     |

### Interpretation of the Nepal trial

In at-risk infants, daily B12 clearly **improved laboratory indicators of B12 nutrition**, but this did not translate into measurable improvements in growth, hemoglobin, neurodevelopment, later IQ, infection burden, or telomere length.

## 2. North India factorial trial — NCT00717730

**Population/intervention:** 1,000 children aged 6–30 months in New Delhi. Children were assigned for six months to:

* 1.8 µg/day vitamin B12;
* 150 µg/day folic acid;
* both;
* or placebo.

Because this was a 2×2 factorial design, researchers could estimate a B12 effect by comparing children assigned to B12 with those not assigned to B12. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][5])

| Primary publication                                                                                                                                                                                         | Outcomes and findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Taneja S, et al. Folic acid and vitamin B-12 supplementation and common infections in 6–30-mo-old children in India. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. 2013;98:731–737. PMID 23902779.**            | B12 improved B12 status but **did not reduce diarrhea or lower respiratory infection incidence**. Increased persistent diarrhea occurred in the folic-acid-containing groups, so that signal should not be attributed to B12 alone. ([PubMed][6])                                                                                               |
| **Strand TA, et al. Vitamin B-12, folic acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children. Pediatrics. 2015;135:e918–e926. PMID 25802345.**                                                                   | The overall B12 effect was a small improvement in weight-for-age z score, **+0.07**, 95% CI 0.01–0.13. Larger growth differences were reported in initially wasted, underweight, or stunted subgroups; the trial did not show a substantial general-population linear-growth benefit. ([PubMed][7])                                             |
| **Kvestad I, et al. Vitamin B12 and Folic Acid Improve Gross Motor and Problem-Solving Skills in Young North Indian Children. PLOS ONE. 2015;10:e0129915. PMID 26098427.**                                  | Development was evaluated in a 422-child trial subset. **B12 alone improved the gross-motor domain**, but generally did not improve the other assessed developmental domains. Effects were more evident in some prespecified or exploratory higher-risk subgroups. ([PMC][8])                                                                   |
| **Kumar T, et al. Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age. Clinical Nutrition. 2017;36:986–991. PMID 27486122.**                            | In the 262 children with detailed hematologic measurements, **B12 did not improve hemoglobin or anemia** after six months. Children with anemia had also received iron during the first two months, complicating interpretation of anemia outcomes but not producing evidence of a B12 benefit. ([PubMed][9])                                   |
| **Kvestad I, et al. Vitamin B12, Folate, and Cognition in 6- to 9-Year-Olds. Pediatrics. 2020;145:e20192316. PMID 32019814.**                                                                               | Six-year follow-up of 791 participants. Early-childhood B12 supplementation produced **no detectable improvement in IQ, verbal ability, executive/neuropsychological tests, or other cognitive outcomes** at ages 6–9 years. ([PubMed][10])                                                                                                     |
| **Taneja S, et al. Vitamin B12 and/or folic acid supplementation on linear growth: a 6-year follow-up study. British Journal of Nutrition. 2023;129:1172–1179. DOI 10.1017/S0007114522002343.**             | There was **no statistically significant overall long-term linear-growth benefit**. Among children whose baseline cobalamin was below 200 pmol/L, the estimated B12 effect was +0.34 height-for-age z score, 95% CI 0.11–0.58; this is a subgroup finding rather than the overall trial result. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][11]) |
| **Upadhyay RP, et al. Daily Folic Acid and/or Vitamin B12 Supplementation Between 6 and 30 Months of Age and Cardiometabolic Risk Markers After 6–7 Years. Journal of Nutrition. 2023;153. PMID 36889645.** | Combined B12 plus folic acid was associated with **1.19 µmol/L lower homocysteine** six years later than placebo. Other cardiometabolic findings were largely subgroup-dependent, and the study did not establish a clear clinical cardiovascular benefit from B12 alone. ([PMC][12])                                                           |

### Interpretation of the India trial

This trial produced a few positive signals:

* a **very small average weight-for-age improvement**;
* improvement in one early developmental domain, gross motor skills;
* potentially larger growth effects among children who were malnourished or B12-deficient initially;
* persistent improvement in a metabolic marker in the combined B12–folic acid group.

However, it found **no convincing improvement in anemia, infections, general developmental performance, later cognition/IQ, or overall long-term height**.

## Overall conclusion from these primary studies

For generally healthy or mildly malnourished children under 12:

* **B12 supplements reliably improve B12 laboratory status** when status is low or marginal.
* Evidence that routine supplementation improves **growth is inconsistent and small**, with possible benefit concentrated among deficient or malnourished children.
* Evidence does **not show a consistent benefit for hemoglobin/anemia, infections, broad neurodevelopment, or later cognitive performance**.
* These prevention trials do not address treatment of a child with **confirmed clinical B12 deficiency**, for whom supplementation is standard medical treatment and placebo-controlled withholding would usually be inappropriate.

The 11 publications above represent **two underlying randomized participant cohorts**, not 11 independent trials.
