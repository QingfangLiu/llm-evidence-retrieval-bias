## Primary comparative studies identified

I grouped publications from the **same randomized cohort** so that outcome papers are not mistakenly counted as independent trials. The main set below includes studies in which vitamin B12 was given directly to children and compared with placebo, no vitamin B12, or an otherwise similar intervention without vitamin B12.

### 1. North India factorial RCT — one trial, six outcome publications

**Population/intervention:** 1,000 children aged 6–30 months were randomized, double-blind, to vitamin B12 1.8 µg/day, folic acid 150 µg/day, both vitamins, or placebo for six months. This design permits comparisons of B12 versus no B12 and B12-plus-folate versus folate alone. Trial registration: NCT00717730 and CTRI/2010/091/001090. ([PubMed][1])

Primary publications:

* **Taneja S, et al.** *Folic acid and vitamin B-12 supplementation and common infections in 6–30-mo-old children in India: a randomized placebo-controlled trial.* **Am J Clin Nutr.** 2013;98:731–737. DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.113.059592. B12 improved biochemical status but did not reduce diarrhea or lower-respiratory-tract infections. Persistent diarrhea was increased in folic-acid-containing groups, rather than clearly by B12 alone. ([PubMed][2])
* **Strand TA, et al.** *Vitamin B-12, folic acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children: a randomized controlled trial.* **Pediatrics.** 2015;135:e918–e926. PMID: 25802345; DOI: 10.1542/peds.2014-1848. Overall, B12 produced a small improvement in weight-for-age z score, mean difference 0.07 SD (95% CI 0.01–0.13), without a general effect on linear growth. Larger weight or height signals appeared in children who were initially wasted, underweight, or stunted. ([PubMed][1])
* **Kvestad I, et al.** *Vitamin B12 and Folic Acid Improve Gross Motor and Problem-Solving Skills in Young North Indian Children: A Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.* **PLoS One.** 2015;10:e0129915. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0129915. In a 422-child substudy, combined B12 plus folate improved gross-motor scores by 0.45 SD (95% CI 0.19–0.73) and problem-solving scores by 0.28 SD (95% CI 0.02–0.54) versus placebo. B12 alone improved gross-motor scores but not the other developmental domains. ([PMC][3])
* **Kumar T, et al.** *Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: A randomized placebo controlled trial.* **Clin Nutr.** 2017;36:986–991. PMID: 27486122; DOI: 10.1016/j.clnu.2016.07.002. B12, folate, or their combination did not improve hemoglobin; nearly 90% of the assessed children remained anemic after six months. ([PubMed][4])
* **Kvestad I, et al.** *Vitamin B12, Folate, and Cognition in 6- to 9-Year-Olds: A Randomized Controlled Trial.* **Pediatrics.** 2020;145:e20192316. PMID: 32019814; DOI: 10.1542/peds.2019-2316. At six-year follow-up, 791 children showed no intervention-group differences on intelligence, verbal, executive, or neuropsychological assessments. ([PubMed][5])
* **Taneja S, et al.** *Vitamin B12 and/or folic acid supplementation on linear growth: a 6-year follow-up study of a randomised controlled trial in early childhood in North India.* **Br J Nutr.** 2023;129:1172–1179. PMID: 35876047; DOI: 10.1017/S0007114522002343. There was no statistically significant overall long-term height benefit. In a prespecified/secondary subgroup with baseline cobalamin below 200 pmol/L, B12 was associated with 0.34 higher HAZ (95% CI 0.11–0.58); this subgroup finding should not be interpreted as the overall trial result. ([PMC][6])

**Trial-level interpretation:** B12 corrected biochemical status and produced small short-term growth and developmental signals, particularly in nutritionally vulnerable subgroups. It did not improve anemia, common infections, overall long-term height, or school-age cognition.

### 2. Nepal infant RCT — one trial, three principal publications

**Population/intervention:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months in Bhaktapur were randomized, double-blind, to 2 µg/day oral vitamin B12 or placebo for 12 months. Both groups received an otherwise identical preparation containing 15 other vitamins and minerals, making this a relatively clean test of adding B12. Trial registration: NCT02272842. ([PMC][7])

Primary publications:

* **Strand TA, et al.** *Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese Infants: A randomized controlled trial.* **PLoS Med.** 2020;17:e1003430. PMID: 33259482; DOI: 10.1371/journal.pmed.1003430. B12 substantially lowered methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine, but did not improve Bayley-III development, length, weight, or hemoglobin. The estimated length difference was 0.20 cm (95% CI −0.23 to 0.63), and the cognitive scaled-score difference was 0.16 points (95% CI −0.54 to 0.87). Adverse events and hospitalizations were not associated with treatment assignment. ([PubMed][8])
* **Ulak M, et al.** *The effect of infant vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment: a follow-up of a randomised placebo-controlled trial in Nepal.* **Br J Nutr.** 2023;129:41–48. PMID: 35152918; DOI: 10.1017/S0007114522000071. Follow-up at approximately 30–35 and 42–47 months found no benefit for preschool cognitive performance; full-scale IQ differed by −0.5 points (95% CI −1.97 to 0.94). ([PubMed][9])
* **Strand TA, et al.** *The Effect of Daily Vitamin B-12 Supplementation on the Incidence of Common Childhood Infections in Nepal: Secondary Outcomes from a Double-Blind Randomized Controlled Trial.* **J Nutr.** 2026;156:101534. PMID: 41997492; DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101534. None of the 20 evaluated infection outcomes differed materially; the clinic-visit incidence-rate ratio was 0.99 (95% CI 0.85–1.14). ([PubMed][10])

**Trial-level interpretation:** Clear correction of metabolic B12 markers, but no demonstrated improvement in growth, hemoglobin, infections, infant development, or preschool cognition.

### 3. Guatemalan infant fortified-food RCT

**Allen LH, et al.** *Vitamin B12 Status and Development of Young Guatemalan Children: Effects of Beef and B12 Supplements.* **FASEB J.** 2007;21:A681. DOI: 10.1096/fasebj.21.5.A681.

**Population/intervention:** 304 infants were randomized at 12 months to beef providing approximately 0.56 µg B12/day, an isocaloric fruit-and-vegetable food fortified with 0.86 µg B12/day, or the same unfortified fruit-and-vegetable food. Foods were supervised five days per week through 21 months.

**Findings:** Neither the B12-fortified food nor beef significantly improved growth, developmental scores, ferritin, or hematologic outcomes relative to the unfortified comparator. Plasma B12 increased numerically in the fortified-food group, but the reported treatment-group difference was not statistically significant. ([ResearchGate][11])

**Limitation:** Published as a conference abstract, so details needed for full risk-of-bias assessment are limited.

### 4. El Salvador and Guatemala schoolchildren controlled supplementation study

**The Effect of Supplements of Animal and Vegetable Protein, Vitamin B12, and Aureomycin on Hematological Values in Central American School Children.** **Am J Clin Nutr.** 1955;3:225–229. DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/3.3.225.

Schoolchildren received 20 µg/day oral B12 either alone or added to a vegetable-protein lunch; control groups received the lunch without B12 or no corresponding supplementation. Follow-up was five months in El Salvador and two years in Guatemala. B12 did not significantly affect hemoglobin or red-cell counts. ([ScienceDirect][12])

**Limitation:** The accessible report does not adequately describe random-sequence generation, concealment, or whether school-level differences were controlled analytically.

### 5. United States prepubertal-schoolchild RCT

**Larcomb JW, Perry CS, Peterman RA.** *Dietary supplementation of vitamin B12 in prepuberty school-age children: I. Growth studies.* **J Pediatr.** 1954;45:70–74. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(54)80064-3.

A total of 132 prepubertal schoolchildren were randomly divided between B12 and placebo for a school year. B12 increased weight gain among children classified as underweight, but did not affect height or weight gain in normally nourished children. A possible height-gain signal was reported among children classified as overweight. The dose is not stated in the accessible summary. ([ScienceDirect][13])

### 6. United States newborn controlled study

**Chinnock RF, et al.** *Results of administration of vitamin B12 to newborn infants.* **J Pediatr.** 1952;40:182–185. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(52)80180-5.

Twelve infants received 5 µg/day for eight weeks, 11 received it for four weeks, and outcomes were compared with a control group. B12 did not produce greater weight gain or length gain. The accessible summary does not specify the method of allocation. ([ScienceDirect][14])

### 7. Protein-deficient children, Caribbean setting

**Mackay IFS, Patrick SJ, Stafford D, Cleveland FS Jr.** *The Influence of Vitamin B12 and Aureomycin upon the Growth of Protein-Deficient Children.* **J Nutr.** 1956;59:155–170. PMID: 13320203; DOI: 10.1093/jn/59.1.155.

This comparative study evaluated B12 and chlortetracycline, separately and/or factorially, in protein-deficient children. B12 did not improve weight or height increments. Chlortetracycline produced only a small weight-gain signal and no height benefit. ([ScienceDirect][15])

## Bottom line from the individual studies

**Biochemical nutrition:** The modern India and Nepal trials show that oral B12 reliably improves circulating or functional B12-status markers. ([PubMed][2])

**Growth:** Most trials found no meaningful effect on linear growth. Signals were limited to a small short-term weight-for-age improvement in India, selected nutritionally vulnerable subgroups, and an older US finding of increased weight gain among underweight—not normally nourished—schoolchildren. ([PubMed][1])

**Development and cognition:** Combined B12 plus folate improved two short-term screening domains in the Indian substudy, but neither the Indian nor Nepalese trial found sustained cognitive or neurodevelopmental benefit at later follow-up. ([PMC][3])

**Anemia and infections:** The better-characterized trials did not demonstrate improvement in hemoglobin, anemia, diarrhea, respiratory infections, or overall childhood-infection incidence. ([PubMed][4])

This is a publication-level study list rather than a pooled synthesis. Several historical reports require retrieval of scans or library copies before their eligibility, participant overlap, and risk of bias can be resolved confidently.
