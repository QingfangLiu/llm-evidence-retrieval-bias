To support your evidence synthesis, I have bypassed all systematic reviews and aggregated data to isolate strictly primary, randomized, placebo-controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on children under 12.

The primary literature is largely anchored by two major, rigorously conducted population-based RCTs in South Asia (where B12 deficiency is highly prevalent), alongside their respective long-term follow-up studies, as well as targeted facility-based clinical trials.

Here is a synthesis of the findings from these primary studies, categorized by clinical outcome.

## 1. Neurodevelopment and Cognition

The evidence regarding cognitive benefits is decidedly mixed and appears heavily dependent on the presence of co-supplementation and the time horizon evaluated.

* **Short-Term Benefits with Co-Supplementation:** In a 2×2 factorial RCT of young North Indian children (6–30 months old), Kvestad et al. (2015) found that daily supplementation with a combination of vitamin B12 and folic acid for six months significantly improved gross motor functioning and problem-solving skills compared to placebo. However, providing vitamin B12 *alone* had no significant effect on any developmental domain except for a borderline improvement in gross motor scores.
* **Null Long-Term Findings (North Indian Cohort):** When researchers re-enrolled children from the North Indian trial 6 to 9 years later, they found no lasting differences in intelligence quotient (WISC-IV), verbal scale, or executive functioning between the B12/folate intervention groups and the placebo group (Kvestad et al., 2020).
* **Null Findings in Infancy (Nepalese Cohort):** A rigorously powered RCT in Nepal investigated the effect of daily 2 μg vitamin B12 supplementation over 12 months in 600 infants at risk of deficiency. Despite the intervention generating a robust metabolic response (significantly lowering homocysteine and methylmalonic acid), it produced no measurable improvement in neurodevelopmental scores compared to placebo (Strand et al., 2020).
* **Null Long-Term Findings (Nepalese Cohort):** A follow-up of the Nepalese cohort at 30 to 47 months of age similarly found no long-term cognitive improvements—specifically noting no difference in the WPPSI-IV Full Scale IQ between the group that had received B12 during infancy and the placebo group (Ulak et al., 2022).

## 2. Physical Growth and Anemia

Despite the well-known biological role of vitamin B12 in erythropoiesis and cellular metabolism, population-level supplementation in mildly deficient but otherwise healthy pediatric groups has not consistently yielded physical improvements.

* In the Nepalese RCT, 12 months of daily vitamin B12 supplementation in infants had absolutely no effect on physical growth trajectories (length/height) or hemoglobin concentrations (anemia) when compared to the placebo control (Strand et al., 2020).

## 3. Cellular, Metabolic, and Systemic Health

While overt physical and cognitive metrics often show null results, researchers tracking longer-term biochemical and cellular markers have observed select physiological effects.

* **Cardiometabolic Risk Markers:** A follow-up of the North Indian RCT evaluated children at 6–7 years of age to determine if early childhood supplementation influenced future cardiometabolic risk. The children who had received both vitamin B12 and folic acid in infancy maintained significantly lower plasma total homocysteine concentrations six years later compared to the placebo group, suggesting a persistent beneficial metabolic footprint (Manapurath et al., 2023).
* **Cellular Aging / Telomere Length:** To test whether B12's role in DNA synthesis protects against cellular aging, researchers evaluated leukocyte telomere length (LTL) at the end of the 12-month Nepalese RCT. They found that B12 supplementation did not significantly affect LTL in mildly stunted infants compared to placebo (Ulak et al., 2024).

## 4. Autism Spectrum Disorder (Clinical Populations)

Targeted studies have evaluated B12 for specific pediatric clinical conditions characterized by distinct metabolic profiles.

* Hendren et al. (2016) conducted an 8-week RCT evaluating subcutaneous methyl B12 versus saline placebo in 57 children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). The researchers reported that the methyl B12 group exhibited significantly greater improvement in the clinician-rated Clinical Global Impressions-Improvement (CGI-I) score compared to the placebo group. These clinical improvements correlated directly with biochemical improvements in cellular methylation capacity.
