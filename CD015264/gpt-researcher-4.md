I grouped multiple publications from the same randomized cohort so that follow-up and secondary-outcome papers are not counted as independent studies. The main set below is limited to **vitamin B12 given directly to children**, orally, with a placebo, no-supplement, or B12-free comparator.

## Clearly eligible, well-characterized oral trials

### 1. North India factorial randomized trial — one cohort, several primary reports

**Population/design:** 1,000 children aged approximately 6–30/35 months; individually randomized, double-blind, 2×2 factorial trial; supplementation for six months.

**Arms:** vitamin B12 1.8 µg/day, folic acid 150 µg/day, B12 plus folic acid, or placebo. Thus, relevant B12 contrasts include B12 versus placebo and B12-plus-folate versus folate alone.

**Trial registration:** NCT00717730; CTRI/2010/091/001090.

Primary publications from this cohort:

* **Taneja S, et al.** *Folic acid and vitamin B-12 supplementation and common infections in 6–30-mo-old children in India: a randomized placebo-controlled trial.* **Am J Clin Nutr.** 2013;98:731–737. DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.113.059592. PMID: 23902779.
  B12 improved biochemical B12 status but did not reduce diarrhea or lower respiratory infections. Persistent diarrhea was more frequent in the folic-acid-containing groups, rather than being an isolated B12 effect. ([PubMed][1])

* **Strand TA, et al.** *Vitamin B-12, folic acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children: a randomized controlled trial.* **Pediatrics.** 2015;135:e918–e926. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2014-1848. PMID: 25802345.
  The B12 main effect increased weight-for-age z score by 0.07, 95% CI 0.01–0.13. There was no clear overall linear-growth benefit, although larger effects were reported in children who were wasted, underweight, or stunted at baseline. ([PubMed][2])

* **Kvestad I, et al.** *Vitamin B12 and Folic Acid Improve Gross Motor and Problem-Solving Skills in Young North Indian Children: A Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.* **PLoS One.** 2015;10:e0129915. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0129915. PMID: 26098427.
  In the developmental substudy, combined B12 plus folate improved gross-motor and problem-solving scores relative to placebo. B12 alone produced a smaller gross-motor signal, while most other developmental domains were not improved. ([PMC][3])

* **Kumar T, et al.** *Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: a randomized placebo controlled trial.* **Clin Nutr.** 2017;36:986–991. DOI: 10.1016/j.clnu.2016.07.002. PMID: 27486122.
  Neither B12 nor folic acid improved hemoglobin concentration; nearly 90% of assessed children remained anemic at six months. ([PubMed][4])

* **Kvestad I, et al.** *Vitamin B12, Folate, and Cognition in 6- to 9-Year-Olds: A Randomized Controlled Trial.* **Pediatrics.** 2020;145:e20192316. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2019-2316. PMID: 32019814.
  At approximately six years after supplementation, 791 children were reassessed. Early B12 or folate supplementation did not improve the principal cognitive outcomes. ([PubMed][5])

* **Manapurath R, et al.** *Daily Folic Acid and/or Vitamin B12 Supplementation Between 6 and 30 Months of Age and Cardiometabolic Risk Markers After 6–7 Years: A Follow-Up of a Randomized Controlled Trial.* **J Nutr.** 2023;153:1493–1501. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2023.03.003. PMID: 36889645.
  Combined B12 plus folic acid was associated with a 1.19 µmol/L lower total homocysteine concentration than placebo six years later. This was a biomarker outcome, not evidence of reduced clinical cardiovascular disease. ([PubMed][6])

**Cohort-level interpretation:** biochemical status improved, with a small mean weight effect and some short-term developmental-domain signals, but no convincing benefit for infections, hemoglobin, overall growth, or long-term cognition.

### 2. Bhaktapur, Nepal infant trial — one cohort, several primary reports

**Population/design:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months; individually randomized, double-blind trial; supplementation for 12 months.

**Intervention/comparator:** 2 µg/day vitamin B12 in a lipid-based paste versus an otherwise comparable paste without B12. Both groups received approximately one recommended daily allowance of 15 other vitamins and minerals.

**Trial registration:** NCT02272842.

Primary publications:

* **Strand TA, et al.** *Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese Infants: A randomized controlled trial.* **PLoS Med.** 2020;17:e1003430. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pmed.1003430. PMID: 33259482.
  B12 improved metabolic markers: total homocysteine and methylmalonic acid were respectively 23% and 30% higher in the comparator group. It did not improve Bayley developmental scores, attained growth, or hemoglobin. Mean length difference was 0.20 cm, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.63. Adverse events were not associated with treatment allocation. ([PubMed][7])

* **Ulak M, et al.** *The effect of infant vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment: a follow-up of a randomised placebo-controlled trial in Nepal.* **Br J Nutr.** 2023;129:41–48. DOI: 10.1017/S0007114522000071. PMID: 35152918.
  At ages 42–47 months, mean full-scale IQ was 84.4 in the B12 group and 85.0 in the comparator group; mean difference −0.5, 95% CI −1.97 to 0.94. Other neurodevelopmental outcomes were also null. ([PubMed][8])

* **Ulak M, et al.** *The Effect of Vitamin B12 Supplementation on Leukocyte Telomere Length in Mildly Stunted Nepalese Children: A Secondary Outcome of a Randomized Controlled Trial.* **J Nutr.** 2024;154:2543–2550. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2023.10.015. PMID: 37918674.
  No effect on leukocyte telomere length was detected: standardized mean difference 0.04, 95% CI −0.14 to 0.21. ([PubMed][9])

* **Strand TA, et al.** *The Effect of Daily Vitamin B-12 Supplementation on the Incidence of Common Childhood Infections in Nepal: Secondary Outcomes from a Double-Blind Randomized Controlled Trial.* **J Nutr.** 2026;156:101534. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101534. PMID: 41997492.
  The clinic-visit incidence-rate ratio was 0.99, 95% CI 0.85–1.14. No significant benefit was found for any of 20 recorded illness outcomes. ([PubMed][10])

**Cohort-level interpretation:** clear biochemical repletion, but no detected benefit for growth, anemia, neurodevelopment, later IQ, telomere length, or infections.

### 3. Guatemalan infant B12-fortification trial

* **Allen LH, et al.** *Vitamin B12 Status and Development of Young Guatemalan Children: Effects of Beef and B12 Supplements.* **FASEB J.** 2007;21:A681. DOI: 10.1096/fasebj.21.5.A681.
  Infants were assigned at approximately 12 months to beef, an equicaloric fruit-and-vegetable preparation fortified with about 0.86 µg/day B12, or the same unfortified preparation, generally five days per week through approximately 21 months. The fortified-versus-unfortified comparison isolates added B12. No clear effects on growth, development, or behavior were reported. This is available only as a conference abstract, so allocation, attrition, and numerical outcome reporting require caution. ([ResearchGate][11])

## Historical oral controlled studies with usable indexed information

These studies are relevant but often have sparse methods, quasi-random allocation, school-level controls, or incomplete outcome reporting.

* **Wetzel NC, Fargo WC.** *Growth failure in school children as associated with vitamin B12 deficiency; response to oral therapy.* **Science.** 1949;110:651–653. DOI: 10.1126/science.110.2868.651.
  Early oral B12 study in schoolchildren with growth failure. The short indexed record does not provide enough information for a modern risk-of-bias assessment.

* **Downing DF.** *Failure of vitamin B12 to promote growth of premature infants.* **Science.** 1950;112:181. DOI: 10.1126/science.112.2902.181. PMID: 15442295.
  A short primary report with a negative growth finding; the abstract and detailed allocation method are unavailable. ([PubMed][12])

* **Rascoff H, Dunewitz A, Norton R.** *The weight progress of premature infants given supplementary feedings of vitamin B12; a comparative study.* **J Pediatr.** 1951;39:61–64. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(51)80281-6. PMID: 14851186.
  Comparative premature-infant study; full text is needed to confirm the allocation procedure, exact dose, and analyzable results. ([PubMed][13])

* **Chinnock RF, et al.** *Results of administration of vitamin B12 to newborn infants.* **J Pediatr.** 1952;40:182–185. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(52)80180-5.
  Small controlled study in newborns receiving approximately 5 µg/day for four or eight weeks. No greater weight or length gain was observed. ([ScienceDirect][14])

* **Finberg L, Chow BF.** *Lack of effect of supplementary vitamin B12 administered to premature infants.* **AMA Am J Dis Child.** 1952;84:165–167. DOI: 10.1001/archpedi.1952.02050020029002. PMID: 14943249.
  Approximately 117 premature infants were allocated alternately to oral B12, reported as 30 µg/day, or control. No beneficial growth or hematologic effect was found. Alternation is quasi-random rather than truly random. ([JAMA Network][15])

* **Spies TD, Dreizen S, Currie C, Buehl CC.** *The effect of supplements of vitamin B12 on the growth of children with nutritive failure.* **Int Z Vitaminforsch Beih.** 1952;23:414–429. PMID: 14937886.
  Primary study in children with nutritional growth failure; no abstract is available, so full-text review is necessary before extracting arms or outcomes. ([PubMed][16])

* **Wetzel NC, Hopwood HH, Kuechle ME, Grueninger RM.** *Growth failure in school children: further studies of vitamin B12 dietary supplements.* **J Clin Nutr.** 1952;1:17–31. DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/1.1.17. PMID: 12990680.
  Controlled schoolchild study, but the accessible indexed material does not adequately report allocation, sample analyzed, or arm-level results. ([ScienceDirect][17])

* **Wilde E.** *The treatment of growth failure in Aleut school children.* **J Pediatr.** 1952;40:565–569. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(52)80146-5. PMID: 14928163.
  Primary growth-failure study involving vitamin supplementation; full text is required to determine whether a clean B12-specific comparison can be extracted. ([PubMed][18])

* **Larcomb JW, Perry CS, Peterman RA.** *Dietary supplementation of vitamin B12 in prepuberty school-age children: I. Growth studies.* **J Pediatr.** 1954;45:70–74. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(54)80064-3. PMID: 13175080.
  A controlled school-year study in 132 prepubertal children randomized to B12 or placebo. B12 increased weight gain among underweight children, did not improve height or weight in normal-weight children, and was associated with greater height gain in the overweight subgroup. ([ScienceDirect][19])

* **Crump J, Tully R.** *The use of partial vitamin supplements in the treatment of growth failure in children.* **J Pediatr.** 1955;46:671–681. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(55)80172-2. PMID: 14368478.
  Primary controlled study, but the indexed record is insufficient to confirm whether the B12 contribution is independently estimable. ([PubMed][20])

* **Montoye HJ, Spata PJ, Pinckney V, Barron L.** *Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on physical fitness and growth of young boys.* **J Appl Physiol.** 1955;7:589–592. DOI: 10.1152/jappl.1955.7.6.589. PMID: 14381333.
  Primary controlled study of growth and physical fitness; no abstract is available, so arm sizes, allocation, and effect estimates need full-text extraction. ([PubMed][21])

* **Aguirre F, Scrimshaw NS.** *The Effect of Supplements of Animal and Vegetable Protein, Vitamin B12, and Aureomycin on Hematological Values in Central American School Children.* **Am J Clin Nutr.** 1955;3:225–229. DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/3.3.225. PMID: 14376358.
  School- or group-level comparisons in El Salvador and Guatemala included oral B12 at 20 µg/day, alone or with a vegetable-protein meal. No significant improvement in red-cell count or hemoglobin was found over five months to two years. This should not be treated as an individually randomized trial. ([ScienceDirect][22])

* **Mackay IF, Patrick SJ, Stafford D, Cleveland FS Jr.** *The Influence of Vitamin B12 and Aureomycin upon the Growth of Protein-Deficient Children.* **J Nutr.** 1956;59:155–170. DOI: 10.1093/jn/59.1.155. PMID: 13320203.
  Controlled comparisons in protein-deficient children found no positive B12 effect on weight or height. ([ScienceDirect][23])

## Studies requiring exclusion or separate handling

* **Shimazono N, et al.** *The Effect of Vitamin B12 Administration on Japanese Children.* **J Vitaminol.** 1963;9:79–88. DOI: 10.5925/jnsv1954.9.79. PMID: 14066706.
  The reported age range extended from approximately 3 to 14 years. It is not strictly an under-12 study unless outcomes for younger children can be separated. ([PubMed][24])

* Intramuscular studies such as **Bjørke-Monsen et al. 2008**, **Torsvik et al. 2013**, and **Worthington-White et al. 1994** provide relevant biological evidence but do not meet a strict oral-supplement eligibility criterion. The Torsvik trial, for example, used a 400 µg intramuscular injection and reported improved metabolic markers, motor scores, and regurgitation at one month. ([PubMed][25])

## Overall pattern from the primary studies

The two strongest modern oral trials show that supplementation **reliably improves biochemical B12 status**, including methylmalonic acid and homocysteine. However, they found little or no benefit for anemia, overall linear growth, infections, general neurodevelopment, or later cognitive performance. The principal positive clinical signals were a small weight-for-age effect and selected short-term developmental-domain effects in the North India trial; these were not reproduced as broad developmental or cognitive benefits at longer follow-up. ([PubMed][2])

The historical studies should be extracted cautiously: several used alternation, school-level controls, poorly described allocation, small samples, or selectively reported growth subgroups.
