## Bottom line

The best contemporary evidence comes from **two large randomized, placebo-controlled trials** in Nepal and India, with several publications reporting different outcomes or later follow-up from the same children. Vitamin B12 supplementation consistently improved laboratory measures of B12 status, but generally **did not improve height, anemia, infections, overall development, IQ, or later cognitive performance**. ([PubMed][1])

A few favorable findings were reported—particularly a very small improvement in weight-for-age and some short-term motor/problem-solving scores in the Indian trial—but these were not consistent across outcomes or confirmed by the later cognitive follow-up. ([AAP Publications][2])

These studies evaluated **preventive supplementation**, mostly in populations with nutritional vulnerability. They should not be interpreted as evidence against treating a child who has confirmed B12 deficiency.

## Practical interpretation

For children without diagnosed deficiency, the primary trials do **not** show that routinely adding B12 improves general growth, resistance to infection, anemia, intelligence, or long-term development. The most dependable benefit is improvement in **B12 biochemical status**.

There remains some uncertainty about whether nutritionally depleted children—especially those who begin with genuinely low B12—might receive a small growth or developmental benefit. The subgroup evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive.

I excluded maternal-only supplementation studies, multivitamin trials in which B12’s effect could not be separated from the other ingredients, injection studies, and treatment comparisons in children already diagnosed with deficiency.
