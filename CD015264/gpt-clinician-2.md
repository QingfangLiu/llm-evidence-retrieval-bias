## Bottom line

I identified **five distinct randomized controlled trials** in children under 12 in which vitamin B12 was the differentiating intervention. Two trials—the North India and Nepal studies—generated several publications and follow-up reports.

Across generally healthy or marginally nourished infants and young children, vitamin B12 supplementation **consistently improved biochemical B12 status**, particularly methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine. It did **not consistently improve growth, hemoglobin, infection rates, neurodevelopment, or later cognitive performance**. Clinical benefits were more apparent in selected infants with biochemical B12 impairment or prematurity, although those findings have narrower applicability.

## Individual primary trials

### 1. North India factorial RCT — oral vitamin B12

**Population/intervention:** 1,000 children aged 6–30 months were randomized for six months to:

* Vitamin B12, 1.8 µg/day
* Folic acid, 150 µg/day
* Both
* Placebo

The factorial design permitted estimation of the effect of B12 independently of folic acid. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][1])

**Infections**

Taneja S, et al. *Folic acid and vitamin B-12 supplementation and common infections in 6–30-mo-old children in India: a randomized placebo-controlled trial.* **Am J Clin Nutr. 2013.** PMID: 23902779.

B12 supplementation improved B12 status but did not reduce diarrhea or lower respiratory tract infections. The reported increase in persistent diarrhea occurred in the folic-acid groups and was not clearly attributable to B12. ([PubMed][2])

**Growth**

Strand TA, et al. *Vitamin B-12, folic acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children: a randomized controlled trial.* **Pediatrics. 2015.** PMID: 25802345. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2014-1848.

B12 produced a small improvement in weight-for-age z score—approximately 0.07 z-score units. Larger effects were reported in children who were initially wasted, underweight, or stunted, but there was no substantial overall improvement across growth measures. ([PubMed][3])

**Neurodevelopment**

Kvestad I, et al. *Vitamin B12 and Folic Acid Improve Gross Motor and Problem-Solving Skills in Young North Indian Children: A Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.* **PLoS One. 2015;10:e0129915.** PMID: 26098427. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0129915.

In a developmental substudy of 422 children, B12 alone improved the gross-motor score by about 4 points, corresponding to approximately 0.29 SD. B12 alone did not significantly improve the other measured developmental domains. Combined B12 plus folic acid improved gross-motor and problem-solving scores relative to placebo. ([PMC][4])

**Hemoglobin**

Mohan VR, et al. *Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: a randomized placebo-controlled trial.* **Clin Nutr.** PMID: 27486122. DOI: 10.1016/j.clnu.2016.07.002.

In the hemoglobin substudy, B12 did not improve hemoglobin concentration. Hemoglobin decreased over follow-up in all randomized groups. ([PubMed][5])

**School-age cognitive follow-up**

Kvestad I, et al. *Vitamin B12, Folate, and Cognition in 6- to 9-Year-Olds: A Randomized Controlled Trial.* **Pediatrics. 2020.** PMID: 32019814. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2019-2316.

Among 791 children reassessed approximately six years after the infant intervention, there were no meaningful differences in cognitive outcomes between the original randomized groups. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** Oral B12 corrected nutritional biomarkers and produced small, inconsistent signals in weight and gross-motor performance, but it did not prevent infections, anemia, or later cognitive impairment.

### 2. Nepal BeLive RCT — oral vitamin B12 added to an otherwise identical supplement

**Population/intervention:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months received either:

* Cyanocobalamin 2 µg/day for 12 months, or
* An identical micronutrient paste without B12

Both groups received approximately one recommended daily allowance of 15 other vitamins and minerals, making this the most direct comparison of “the same supplement with versus without B12.” ([ClinicalTrials.gov][7])

**Growth, neurodevelopment, hemoglobin and B12 status**

Strand TA, et al. *Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese infants: a randomized controlled trial.* **PLoS Med. 2020;17:e1003430.** PMID: 33259482. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pmed.1003430.

B12 substantially improved metabolic B12 status: methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine were lower in the supplemented group. However, supplementation did not improve neurodevelopment, linear growth, weight, or hemoglobin. No intervention-related serious safety signal was reported. ([PubMed][8])

**Later neurodevelopment**

Ulak M, et al. *The effect of infant vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment: a follow-up of a randomised placebo-controlled trial in Nepal.* **Br J Nutr. 2023.** PMID: 35152918. DOI: 10.1017/S0007114522000071.

At approximately 42–47 months of age, full-scale IQ was similar between groups: 84.4 versus 85.0, adjusted mean difference −0.5 points, 95% CI −1.97 to 0.94. No benefit was demonstrated in the other developmental outcomes. ([PubMed][9])

**Common infections**

Strand TA, et al. *The Effect of Daily Vitamin B-12 Supplementation on the Incidence of Common Childhood Infections in Nepal: Secondary Outcomes from a Double-Blind Randomized Controlled Trial.* **J Nutr. 2026;156:101534.** PMID: 41997492. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101534.

B12 did not reduce overall illness episodes or clinic visits. The incidence-rate ratio for the aggregate illness outcome was 0.99, 95% CI 0.85–1.14. ([PubMed][10])

**Telomere-length outcome**

Ulak M, et al. *The Effect of Vitamin B12 Supplementation on Leukocyte Telomere Length in Mildly Stunted Nepalese Children: A Secondary Outcome of a Randomized Controlled Trial.* **J Nutr. 2024.** PMID: 37918674. DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2023.10.015.

B12 supplementation did not affect leukocyte telomere length. ([PubMed][11])

**Interpretation:** This well-controlled trial shows clear biochemical correction without measurable improvements in growth, anemia, infection risk, or neurodevelopment.

### 3. Healthy breastfed infants in Norway — intramuscular B12 versus no intervention

Torsvik I, et al. *Common metabolic profile in infants indicating impaired cobalamin status responds to cobalamin supplementation.* **Pediatrics. 2008.** PMID: 18595990. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2007-2716.

**Population/intervention:** 107 healthy six-week-old infants were randomized to a single 400-µg intramuscular cobalamin injection or no intervention and followed to four months.

The injection increased serum B12 by approximately 75% and markedly reduced methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine. The study primarily assessed biochemical outcomes and was not designed to demonstrate improvements in growth, illness, or development. ([PubMed][12])

### 4. Symptomatic infants with impaired B12 function in Norway — intramuscular B12 versus sham injection

Torsvik IK, et al. *Cobalamin supplementation improves motor development and regurgitations in infants: results from a randomized intervention study.* **Am J Clin Nutr. 2013;98:1233–1240.** PMID: 24025626. DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.113.061549.

**Population/intervention:** 79 infants younger than eight months with feeding difficulties, developmental concerns or excessive regurgitation and biochemical evidence of impaired cobalamin function received 400 µg intramuscular hydroxycobalamin or a sham injection.

After one month:

* Total homocysteine fell by 54%.
* Methylmalonic acid fell by 84%.
* Median Alberta Infant Motor Scale improvement was 7.0 versus 4.5 points.
* Regurgitation improved in 69% versus 29%.

These findings support benefit in a **selected symptomatic, biochemically abnormal population**, rather than routine supplementation of all infants. ([PubMed][13])

### 5. Preterm infants in the United States — factorial folate/B12 trial

Worthington-White DA, et al. *Premature infants require additional folate and vitamin B-12 to reduce the severity of the anemia of prematurity.* **Am J Clin Nutr. 1994;60:930–935.** PMID: 7985636. DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/60.6.930.

**Population/intervention:** 184 preterm infants weighing under 1,800 g and born before 36 weeks were randomized to:

* Folic acid
* Intramuscular B12, 100 µg monthly for four months
* Both
* Neither

All infants received iron and vitamin E. At approximately 10–12 weeks, infants receiving B12 alone or B12 plus folate had higher hemoglobin concentrations than those receiving neither supplement or folate alone. Applicability is largely limited to premature infants and to parenteral supplementation. ([PubMed][14])

## Clinical interpretation

For **community-dwelling infants and young children without overt B12-deficiency disease**, the trials indicate:

| Outcome                            | Overall trial evidence                                                      |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Serum or functional B12 status     | Consistently improves                                                       |
| Growth                             | Little or no average benefit; one small weight-for-age effect               |
| Hemoglobin/anemia                  | No benefit in community children; possible benefit in preterm infants       |
| Acute infections                   | No reduction                                                                |
| Early neurodevelopment             | Mostly no benefit; isolated gross-motor signal                              |
| Later cognition/IQ                 | No demonstrated benefit                                                     |
| Symptomatic biochemical deficiency | Short-term motor and regurgitation improvement in one selected infant trial |

These prevention trials should not be interpreted as evidence against treating a child with **confirmed clinical B12 deficiency, megaloblastic anemia, neurologic manifestations, malabsorption, or a high-risk restrictive diet**. Such children differ substantially from the largely healthy or marginally nourished populations enrolled in the placebo-controlled trials.
