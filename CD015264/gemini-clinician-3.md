The primary literature presents a nuanced picture regarding vitamin B12 supplementation in pediatric populations. Overall, the data indicate that while B12 successfully corrects metabolic markers in at-risk children, prophylactic oral supplementation in community settings does not reliably translate to measurable improvements in physical growth, anemia, or overarching cognitive development when given alone. Clinical benefits are largely restricted to specific, targeted interventions for documented deficiencies, or when B12 is co-administered with folic acid.

Here is a breakdown of the primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating these outcomes:

## Community-Based Supplementation in At-Risk Infants

* **The Nepalese Infant Cohort (Strand et al., 2020; Ulak et al., 2022):** A large, population-based RCT involving 600 marginally stunted infants (6–11 months of age) evaluated the daily administration of a 2 µg oral dose of B12 for one year. While the intervention successfully improved metabolic biomarkers—significantly lowering total homocysteine (tHcy) and methylmalonic acid (MMA)—it failed to produce clinical improvements in neurodevelopment, physical growth, or hemoglobin concentrations (Strand et al., 2020). A follow-up of this cohort at 3 to 4 years of age confirmed no long-term benefits to preschool cognitive functioning or overall intelligence quotients (Ulak et al., 2022).
* **The North Indian Cohort (Kvestad et al., 2015):** In a factorial RCT evaluating children aged 6–30 months, subjects were randomized to receive a placebo, B12, folic acid, or a combination of B12 and folic acid for six months. While B12 alone produced an isolated improvement in gross motor scores, it had no effect on other developmental domains. However, the *combination* of B12 and folic acid yielded significant improvements in both gross motor functioning and problem-solving skills compared to placebo.

## Targeted Interventions in Biochemically Deficient Infants

* **The Norwegian Cohort (Torsvik et al., 2015):** Clinical benefits appear to be far more pronounced when treating documented deficiencies rather than providing blanket supplementation. In a double-blind trial evaluating infants born at subnormal weights (2,000–3,000 g) who developed biochemical signs of impaired B12 function by 6 months of age (tHcy > 6.5 µmol/L), an intramuscular injection of 400 µg of hydroxycobalamin significantly improved gross motor scores on the Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS) and the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) within one month compared to a sham injection (Torsvik et al., 2015).

## Maternal and Perinatal Supplementation

* **The Indian Maternal Cohort (Srinivasan et al., 2016):** An RCT exploring the prenatal window provided pregnant women with 50 µg of oral B12 daily from <14 weeks gestation through 6 weeks postpartum. At 9 months of age, there were no significant differences in the cognitive, language, or motor domains on the Bayley-III scales between infants in the supplemented and placebo groups, though the study did note that baseline elevated maternal homocysteine was independently associated with poorer infant cognitive outcomes.

## Clinical Takeaway

For young children, the primary literature suggests that prophylactic oral B12 supplementation reliably corrects metabolic markers of deficiency but does not translate to standalone improvements in growth, hemoglobin, or neurodevelopment. However, targeted interventions (especially intramuscular dosing) in children with documented biochemical deficiencies yield rapid developmental improvements. Furthermore, if oral supplementation is pursued for neurodevelopmental support, there is evidence that pairing B12 with folic acid is more efficacious than B12 alone due to their intertwined one-carbon metabolic pathways.
