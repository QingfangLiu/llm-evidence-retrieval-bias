## North Indian cohort (6–30 months at enrollment), New Delhi

This is a single large factorial RCT (~1,000 children randomized to placebo, B12 alone, folic acid alone, or both) with several published outcome papers:

- **Kvestad et al. 2015, PLOS ONE** — measured the effect of daily supplementation of vitamin B12 and/or folic acid on development in 422 children aged six to 30 months using a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, factorial design, finding improved gross motor and problem-solving scores with combined B12+folic acid versus placebo.
- **Strand et al. 2015, Pediatrics** — original trial report on growth outcomes (referenced in citation #4 above).
- **Hemoglobin outcomes (ScienceDirect)** — assessed whether folate or vitamin B12 given daily for 6 months improved hemoglobin concentration in 262 children, finding that supplementation of either or both vitamins did not improve hemoglobin, and anemia prevalence actually increased in all four groups during follow-up.
- **6-year growth follow-up (Br J Nutr, PMC10011595)** — re-measured height in 791 of the original children at a mean age of 7.4 years, finding a small, non-significant effect of vitamin B12 on linear growth and no effect of folic acid.
- **6–7 year cardiometabolic follow-up (PMC10196576)** — investigated effects of the same 6-month supplementation on cardiometabolic risk markers (homocysteine, adiponectin, leptin) measured 6–7 years later in 791 children.

## Nepal (Bhaktapur) infant cohort, 6–11 months at enrollment

- **Strand et al. 2020, PLOS Medicine** — a community-based, individually randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 600 marginally stunted 6–11-month-old infants, randomized 1:1 to 2 μg vitamin B12 or placebo daily for 12 months, assessing neurodevelopment (Bayley-III), growth, and hemoglobin. No effects were found on Bayley-III scores, growth, or hemoglobin, though metabolic markers improved.
- **Follow-up at 30–47 months, Br J Nutr (Cambridge)** — re-examined neurodevelopment later in childhood, since standardized tests are more reliable after age two; this follow-up contradicted the Norwegian trials that had found improved motor development.
- **Secondary outcomes on childhood infections** (J Nutrition, 2026) — referenced as a secondary-outcomes paper from this same double-blind RCT on daily B12 and incidence of common childhood infections.

## Norwegian infant cohorts (Torsvik et al., Bergen)

- **Torsvik et al. 2013, Am J Clin Nutr** — In Norway, 79 infants were treated with B12 or placebo; one month later those treated with B12 showed a marked fall in homocysteine and methylmalonic acid, and clinically a larger improvement than placebo in gross motor development and regurgitation.
- **Torsvik et al. 2015, BMC Pediatrics** — a randomized intervention trial examining motor development related to duration of exclusive breastfeeding, B-vitamin status, and B12 supplementation in infants with birth weight 2000–3000 g; found that B12 supplementation in this susceptible group led to improved motor performance versus placebo.

## Other relevant primary studies

- **Nigerian children with nephrotic syndrome (PMC12129721)** — a single-blind RCT of 48 children with nephrotic syndrome and elevated homocysteine, randomized to daily folic acid, B6, and B12 versus placebo for six months, showing significant homocysteine reduction with active treatment versus minimal change with placebo. Note: this is a clinical population (kidney disease), not healthy children, and doesn't isolate B12 alone.
- **Sublingual vs. intramuscular B12 in deficient children (PMC12873626)** — a prospective comparative study in children aged 1–15 years with confirmed nutritional B12 deficiency megaloblastic anemia, comparing sublingual versus IM B12 administration. Note: this compares two delivery routes of B12 to each other, not to placebo/no-B12, and its age range extends beyond 12.

**A few caveats worth flagging as a parent evaluating this evidence:**
- Several of these trials used B12 as part of a multi-vitamin or combined B12+folic acid arm rather than isolating B12 alone, which can make it hard to attribute effects specifically to B12.
- Results are mixed and population-dependent — trials in vitamin B12–deficient or at-risk populations (Norway, some Nepal subgroups) tended to show benefits on motor development, while trials in broader, marginally-stunted populations (main Nepal RCT, hemoglobin outcome in India) found no benefit on growth, hemoglobin, or overall neurodevelopment.
- Most of these trials were conducted in infants and toddlers (under age 3) rather than school-age children (6–12), so the evidence base is heavily weighted toward the youngest end of the "under 12" range.
