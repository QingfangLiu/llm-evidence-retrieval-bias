## Scope

I included randomized or controlled primary studies in which oral vitamin B12 was administered directly to children younger than 12 and compared with placebo, no supplementation, or an otherwise identical supplement without B12. I excluded maternal supplementation, intramuscular B12, uncontrolled treatment series, and broad multivitamin-versus-placebo trials that cannot separate B12’s contribution.

Several papers below are **different outcomes or follow-ups from the same randomized cohort**, so they should not be counted as independent trials.

## Most directly relevant primary trials

### 1. North India factorial trial — NCT00717730

**Population/intervention:** Approximately 1,000 children aged 6–30 months were randomized for six months to daily B12 1.8 µg, folic acid 150 µg, both, or placebo. The factorial design permits estimation of a B12 effect separately from folic acid.

### 2. Nepal infant trial — NCT02272842

**Population/intervention:** Six hundred marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months received either B12 2 µg/day or placebo for 12 months. Both groups received an identical lipid-based paste containing 15 other vitamins and minerals, making this the cleanest comparison of **the same supplement with versus without B12**.

### 3. Guatemalan infant fortified-food trial

**Primary report:** **Allen LH, et al. Vitamin B12 Status and Development of Young Guatemalan Children: Effects of Beef and B12 Supplements.** *FASEB Journal.* 2007;21:A681. DOI 10.1096/fasebj.21.5.A681.

**Population/intervention:** Three hundred four infants, approximately 12 months old, were randomized for nine months to:

* 72 g/day beef providing approximately 0.56 µg B12;
* an equicaloric fruit-and-vegetable product fortified with 0.86 µg B12; or
* the same unfortified fruit-and-vegetable product.

Plasma B12 increased by approximately 61 pmol/L in the fortified-B12 group, compared with a decline of 13 pmol/L in controls, but the reported between-group difference was not statistically significant. No intervention effect was found for growth, Bayley development, language, temperament, atypical behavior, or activity. ([ResearchGate][10])

**Important limitation:** This appears to have been published only as a conference abstract rather than a complete peer-reviewed article, so methods, attrition, numerical outcome data, and risk of bias cannot be evaluated adequately. The trial cohort and its substantial baseline B12 insufficiency were described in a separate primary baseline paper. ([PubMed][11])

## Clinical bottom line

Across the directly attributable oral trials:

* **Nutritional biomarkers:** B12 supplementation generally raises circulating B12 and lowers methylmalonic acid and homocysteine.
* **Growth:** No reproducible improvement in linear growth; one Indian trial found a very small average weight-for-age effect and larger subgroup effects in children already undernourished.
* **Anemia/hemoglobin:** No demonstrated benefit.
* **Development and cognition:** One short-term Indian assessment found a modest gross-motor signal, but the larger Nepal trial and longer-term follow-ups found no benefit.
* **Infections:** No reduction in diarrhea, respiratory infection, or overall childhood illness.
* **Safety:** The Nepal trial found no intervention-associated adverse-event signal, but uncommon harms were not well evaluated.

These studies concern **population supplementation with nutritional doses in children who were generally at risk of low status but did not have overt, severe symptomatic B12 deficiency**. They do not address the effectiveness of therapeutic-dose B12 for a child with confirmed deficiency, macrocytic anemia, neurologic manifestations, malabsorption, or an inborn metabolic disorder.
