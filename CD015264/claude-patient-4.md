Here are individual primary studies (RCTs) on vitamin B12 supplementation in children under 12, comparing it against placebo, no supplementation, or the same regimen minus B12. All are trials, not reviews/guidelines.

**Neurodevelopment, growth, and hemoglobin**
- Kvestad et al., a community-based, individually randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled trial conducted in Bhaktapur, Nepal, enrolled 600 marginally stunted 6- to 11-month-old infants between 2015 and 2017, randomized 1:1 to 2 μg of vitamin B12 or placebo daily for 12 months, both groups also receiving 15 other vitamins/minerals, published in PLOS Medicine (2020). Daily B12 supplementation improved metabolic B12 status but did not affect neurodevelopment, growth, or hemoglobin concentration.

**Growth (linear growth / anthropometry)**
- Strand TA, Taneja S, Kumar T, Manger MS, Refsum H, Yajnik CS, et al. "Vitamin B-12, Folic Acid, and Growth in 6- to 30-Month-Old Children: A Randomized Controlled Trial." *Pediatrics* 2015;135:e918–926. (New Delhi factorial RCT, 1000 children, B12/folic acid/both/placebo for 6 months.)
- A follow-up study of this factorial-designed, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 1000 young children, published in the British Journal of Nutrition, measured height in 791 children six years after 6 months of supplementation with folic acid, vitamin B12, both, or placebo, given starting at 6–30 months of age.

**Cognitive and motor development**
- A randomized, double-blind trial gave children aged 6 to 30 months placebo or vitamin B12 and/or folic acid for six months in a 1:1:1:1 factorial design, measuring development via the Ages and Stages Questionnaire in 422 children, published in PLOS One (Kvestad et al., 2015). Combined B12 and folic acid produced higher gross motor and problem-solving scores versus placebo, and vitamin B12 alone significantly improved gross motor scores.

**Hemoglobin/anemia**
- Kumar T, Taneja S, Sachdev HPS, Refsum H, Yajnik CS, Bhandari N, Strand TA. "Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: A randomized placebo controlled trial." *Clinical Nutrition* 2017;36(4):986-991 (same New Delhi cohort).

**Infectious morbidity**
- One thousand North Indian children (6-30 mo of age) were enrolled in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to receive 2 times the Recommended Dietary Allowance of folic acid and/or vitamin B-12, testing effects on diarrhea and respiratory infection incidence (Taneja et al., *AJCN* 2013). Treatment had no measurable beneficial effect on respiratory or diarrheal morbidity, and folic acid (with or without B12) was linked to a modest increase in diarrhea risk.

**Cardiometabolic follow-up**
- In the original trial, children were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1:1 ratio in blocks of 16 to receive vitamin B12 and folic acid, vitamin B12, folic acid, or placebo supplements daily for 6 months, with cardiometabolic risk markers reassessed 6–7 years later (*AJCN*-affiliated follow-up, PMC10196576).

**Clinical/deficiency population**
- A single-blind, randomized controlled trial at University College Hospital, Ibadan, Nigeria randomly assigned 48 children with nephrotic syndrome and elevated homocysteine to daily folic acid, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12, or placebo, for six months, finding a significant homocysteine reduction in the treatment group.

A few notes: the Cochrane review protocol/review itself (PMC9651173) and the completed 2026 Cochrane review (CD015264) are reviews, so I excluded them per your criteria, but they're useful indexes of trials if you want more. The MATCOBIND trial (maternal B12, vegetarian mothers) is a protocol without published results yet, and the sublingual-vs-intramuscular B12 trial compares delivery routes rather than B12 vs. placebo/no-B12, so I left those out as not matching your comparator criteria.
