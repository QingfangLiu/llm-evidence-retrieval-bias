## Bottom line

In children without a clearly diagnosed vitamin B12 deficiency, oral B12 supplementation **often improves blood or metabolic measures of B12 status**, but the better-designed trials have generally **not shown meaningful improvements in height, hemoglobin/anemia, infections, sleep, or long-term cognitive development**. A North Indian trial found a very small improvement in weight-for-age, and short-term developmental benefits occurred only with **B12 plus folic acid**, so they cannot confidently be attributed to B12 alone. ([PMC][1])

Below are individual primary reports, grouped by the randomized trial that produced them. Grouping matters because several publications are secondary analyses or follow-ups of the same children rather than independent replications.

## Trials that most directly isolate B12

### 1. North India factorial randomized trial — NCT00717730

**Population/intervention:** 1,000 children approximately 6–30 months old received daily B12, folic acid, both, or placebo for six months.

Individual primary reports:

* **Taneja et al., 2013 — infections.** B12 improved biochemical B12 status but did not reduce diarrhea or lower respiratory infections. The increased persistent-diarrhea signal occurred in the folic-acid-containing groups, not specifically with B12. *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*. PMID 23902779; DOI 10.3945/ajcn.113.059592. ([PubMed][2])
* **Strand et al., 2015 — growth.** B12 produced a small overall improvement in weight-for-age z score of **0.07** points, with no clear overall linear-growth benefit. Larger effects were reported in children who were already wasted, underweight, or stunted, but these were subgroup findings. *Pediatrics*. PMID 25802345; DOI 10.1542/peds.2014-1848. ([PubMed][3])
* **Kvestad et al., 2015 — early development.** In a 422-child subset, **combined B12 plus folic acid** improved gross-motor scores by 0.45 SD and problem-solving by 0.28 SD versus placebo. This result does not establish that B12 alone caused the benefit. *PLOS One*. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0129915. ([PMC][4])
* **Kumar et al., 2017 — hemoglobin.** Among 262 children with laboratory measurements, B12, folic acid, or their combination did not improve hemoglobin after six months. *Clinical Nutrition*. PMID 27486122; DOI 10.1016/j.clnu.2016.07.002. ([PubMed][5])
* **Kvestad et al., 2020 — long-term cognition.** Six years later, 791 children were reassessed at ages 6–9 years. There were no differences between the original B12, folic-acid, combination, and placebo groups on intelligence, language, attention, executive-function, or other cognitive measures. *Pediatrics*. PMID 32019814; DOI 10.1542/peds.2019-2316. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** This trial provides a small signal for weight gain and a short-term signal for B12 plus folate, but no reduction in infections or anemia and no lasting cognitive advantage.

### 2. Nepal infant randomized trial — NCT02272842

**Population/intervention:** 600 mildly or marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months received **2 µg B12 daily or an identical placebo for 12 months**. Both groups received the same approximately 15 other vitamins and minerals, making this one of the cleanest tests of adding B12 to an otherwise identical supplement.

Individual primary reports:

* **Strand et al., 2020 — growth, development and hemoglobin.** B12 substantially improved methylmalonic acid and homocysteine, indicating better functional B12 status. It did not improve Bayley developmental scores, length or other growth measures, or hemoglobin. The between-group difference in length was 0.20 cm and in cognitive scaled score was 0.16 points; neither was statistically significant. Adverse events were not associated with treatment. *PLOS Medicine*. PMID 33259482; DOI 10.1371/journal.pmed.1003430. ([PLOS][7])
* **Hysing et al., 2022 — sleep.** B12 did not improve nighttime sleep duration, total sleep, or waking after sleep onset. A small difference in sleep-onset latency was observed, but the overall findings did not support B12 for improving infant sleep. *Clinical Nutrition*. PMID 34999324; DOI 10.1016/j.clnu.2021.11.040. ([PubMed][8])
* **Ulak et al., 2023 — preschool neurodevelopment.** At ages 42–47 months, mean full-scale IQ was 84.4 after B12 and 85.0 after placebo, a difference of −0.5 points; no other neurodevelopmental outcome or prespecified subgroup benefited. *British Journal of Nutrition*. PMID 35152918. ([PubMed][9])
* **Ulak et al., 2024 — leukocyte telomere length.** B12 had no effect on telomere length overall or in subgroups defined by baseline B12 status, anemia, sex, or growth. *Journal of Nutrition*. PMID 37918674; DOI 10.1016/j.tjnut.2023.10.015. ([PubMed][10])
* **Strand et al., 2026 — infections.** B12 did not reduce health-clinic visits or any of 20 recorded infection or illness outcomes. The clinic-visit incidence-rate ratio was 0.99, meaning essentially identical rates. *Journal of Nutrition*. PMID 41997492; DOI 10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101534. ([PubMed][11])

**Interpretation:** B12 clearly changed B12-related laboratory markers, but that biochemical improvement did not translate into measurable growth, anemia, developmental, sleep, or infection benefits.

### 3. Guatemalan infant randomized trial

**Allen et al., 2007 — “Vitamin B12 Status and Development of Young Guatemalan Children: Effects of Beef and B12 Supplements.”**

**Population/intervention:** 304 infants beginning at 12 months were assigned for nine months to B12-fortified fruit and vegetables providing 0.86 µg/day, unfortified fruit and vegetables, or beef providing 0.56 µg B12, five days per week.

**Findings:** The investigators reported no treatment effects on growth or any measured developmental outcome. Plasma B12 increased numerically in the B12-fortified group but the between-group difference was not statistically significant.

**Important limitation:** This appears to have been published only as a conference abstract, so methods, missing data, adverse events, and full outcome tables are incompletely reported. *FASEB Journal* 2007;21:A681; DOI 10.1096/fasebj.21.5.A681. ([ResearchGate][12])

## B12-containing multinutrient studies that cannot isolate B12

### 4. Hinnouho et al., 2022 — Laos

A double-blind trial randomized 1,704 children aged 6–23 months to a powder containing **0.9 µg B12 plus 14 other micronutrients** or placebo for approximately 36 weeks. In the 260-child biomarker subgroup, the intervention did not improve plasma B12 or reduce B12 deficiency; it did improve folate status. Because all 15 nutrients differed between groups, the study cannot determine the independent effect of B12. *European Journal of Nutrition*. DOI 10.1007/s00394-022-02890-3. ([Springer][13])

### 5. Ndeezi et al., 2011 — Ugandan children with HIV

Children aged 1–5 years received either 14 micronutrients—including B12 and folate—or a standard six-vitamin supplement without B12 or folate. In the 214-child biomarker analysis, B12 and folate concentrations improved in the broader micronutrient group; hemoglobin increased in both groups, and CD4 counts did not change significantly. This supports an effect on nutrient status but cannot separate B12 from folate and the other added minerals, and the results apply to a specialized HIV population. *Nutrition Journal*. PMID 21600005; DOI 10.1186/1475-2891-10-56. ([PMC][14])

## Non-oral treatment evidence

These trials do not answer the routine oral-supplement question, but they illustrate why **treating an infant with biochemical B12 impairment is different from supplementing a generally healthy population**:

* In 79 infants with biochemical evidence of impaired cobalamin function, one **intramuscular 400-µg hydroxycobalamin injection** improved B12 biomarkers, motor-score gains and parental reports of regurgitation after one month compared with sham injection. ([PubMed][15])
* In 107 six-week-old infants, an intramuscular 400-µg injection markedly improved serum B12, methylmalonic acid and homocysteine compared with no intervention, although clinical-developmental outcomes were not evaluated in that report. ([PubMed][16])
* In 184 premature infants, monthly intramuscular B12, alone or with folate, produced higher hemoglobin at 10–12 weeks than no additional supplementation or folate alone. This is a preterm-infant anemia study, not evidence for routine supplementation of healthy children. ([PubMed][17])

## What this means for a parent

The primary trials do **not** show that routinely giving extra B12 to an otherwise adequately nourished child improves intelligence, immunity, sleep, anemia, or height. The clearest effect is on B12 laboratory status. The situation is different when a child has documented or strongly suspected deficiency, restricted animal-food intake, malabsorption, or neurologic or hematologic findings; the population-prevention trials above should not be used as evidence against evaluating and treating such a child.
