## Bottom line

Across the strongest randomized trials, **vitamin B12 supplementation consistently improved biochemical B12 status**, such as lowering methylmalonic acid or homocysteine. However, it generally **did not improve height, neurodevelopment, cognition, hemoglobin/anemia, or childhood infections**.

There were two possible clinical signals:

* A **very small improvement in weight-for-age** in one large Indian trial.
* A short-term improvement in one **gross-motor development measure**, also in the Indian trial.

These findings were not accompanied by broader developmental benefits and were not reproduced in the large Nepal trial.

All citations below are **individual primary trial reports**, not reviews or guidelines. Multiple papers arising from the same randomized trial are grouped together to avoid counting the same children more than once.

## 1. North India factorial randomized trial — NCT00717730

**Population and intervention:** 1,000 children aged 6–30 months from New Delhi were randomized, double-blind, to placebo, vitamin B12 1.8 µg/day, folic acid, or both for six months.

## 2. Nepal randomized trial — NCT02272842

**Population and intervention:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months were randomized, double-blind, to 2 µg/day of B12 or placebo for 12 months. Both groups received an otherwise identical preparation containing 15 other vitamins and minerals, making this a particularly direct comparison of adding B12.

| Primary publication                                                          | Outcome and result                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Strand et al., 2020 — growth, development, hemoglobin and B12 markers**    | B12 substantially improved metabolic B12 status: homocysteine and methylmalonic acid were respectively 23% and 30% higher in the placebo group. Nevertheless, there was **no benefit for Bayley developmental scores, length gain, other growth measurements or hemoglobin**. Length-gain difference was 0.20 cm, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.63. Adverse events were not associated with treatment. ([PLOS][7]) |
| **Kvestad and colleagues, follow-up reported 2022/2023 — later development** | At ages approximately 30–47 months, there remained **no developmental or intelligence advantage**. Mean full-scale IQ was 84.4 after B12 versus 85.0 after placebo; mean difference −0.5, 95% CI −1.97 to 0.94. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][8])                                                                                                                                         |
| **Ulak et al., 2024 — telomere length**                                      | Leukocyte telomere length did **not differ** between B12 and placebo groups: standardized mean difference 0.04, 95% CI −0.14 to 0.21. ([PubMed][9])                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Strand et al., 2026 — childhood infections**                               | B12 did **not reduce any of 20 measured clinical illness outcomes**. Clinic-visit incidence-rate ratio was 0.99, 95% CI 0.85–1.14. ([PubMed][10])                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |

**Interpretation:** Even in infants at risk for poor B12 status, correcting the biochemical abnormalities did not translate into measurable improvements in growth, anemia, development or infections.

## 3. Larcomb, Perry and Peterman, 1954 — US schoolchildren

**Study:** *Dietary supplementation of vitamin B12 in prepuberty school-age children: I. Growth studies.*

A total of 132 prepubertal schoolchildren were randomly assigned to B12 or placebo for a school year. The investigators reported:

* No effect on height or weight gain in children of normal weight.
* Greater weight gain among initially underweight children.
* Faster height gain among children classified as overweight.

The reporting is very limited by modern standards, and detailed numerical estimates are not provided in the indexed summary. ([PubMed][11])

## 4. Mackay et al., 1956 — protein-deficient children

**Study:** *The Influence of Vitamin B12 and Aureomycin upon the Growth of Protein-Deficient Children.*

This controlled study examined B12 and the antibiotic aureomycin in protein-deficient children. The investigators found **no positive effect of B12 on either height or weight gain** and concluded that it was not practically useful for correcting the malnutrition in that population. ([ScienceDirect][12])

Because the trial was conducted in the 1950s, the allocation procedures, masking and analyses do not meet the reporting standards expected of contemporary trials.

---

## 5. Aguirre and Scrimshaw, 1955 — Guatemala and El Salvador schoolchildren

**Study:** *The Effect of Supplements of Animal and Vegetable Protein, Vitamin B12, and Aureomycin on Hematological Values in Central American School Children.*

Children in several schools received different lunches or supplements, including oral B12 20 µg/day, and were compared with school control groups for periods ranging from five months to two years. B12, either alone or with a vegetable-protein lunch, produced **no significant improvement in hemoglobin or red-blood-cell counts**. ([PubMed][13])

This appears to have been a school-level controlled intervention rather than a well-documented individually randomized trial, so it is considerably less reliable than the modern India and Nepal studies.

## Overall interpretation for a parent

For children without a diagnosed deficiency:

* B12 supplements can **raise B12 concentrations and improve metabolic markers**.
* Current randomized evidence does **not show consistent improvements in height, cognition, broad development, anemia or resistance to infections**.
* The small weight effect and isolated gross-motor finding from India are not strong enough to establish a general health benefit.
* These preventive studies do not answer the different clinical question of treating a child with **confirmed B12 deficiency, malabsorption or deficiency-related neurologic or hematologic illness**.
