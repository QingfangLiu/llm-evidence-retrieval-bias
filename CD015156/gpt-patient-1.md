## Bottom line

There is **no single nonsurgical treatment proven best for all lower-limb apophysitis**. The evidence differs substantially by location:

* **Heel/Sever disease:** Heel cups, heel lifts, and foot orthoses can provide short-term pain relief. No device has shown consistent long-term superiority.
* **Knee/Osgood–Schlatter disease:** Pain-guided activity modification combined with progressive strengthening and gradual return to sport is the most defensible first-line approach. No particular exercise program has clearly beaten good usual care.
* **Outer foot/Iselin disease:** Rest, footwear modification, and temporary immobilization have favorable outcomes in one retrospective series, but the treatments were not compared.
* **Hip or pelvic apophysitis:** Evidence is limited to case reports and small series. Relative rest, physical therapy, and staged return to sport appear successful, but no study establishes which component works best.

I searched for primary treatment studies available through **July 25, 2026** and excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, expert opinions, and editorials from the evidence synthesis below.

## 1. Heel: calcaneal apophysitis, or Sever disease

This is where the best comparative evidence exists.

### Heel cups, lifts, and orthoses

A practical conclusion is that **an inexpensive heel cup or heel lift is a reasonable initial adjunct**, particularly when pain occurs during running and jumping.

* In 124 children, a heel raise produced a small short-term functional advantage over a prefabricated orthosis at one and two months. By six and twelve months, neither the type of insert nor replacement footwear was superior. The investigators specifically suggested considering cost and patient preference when selecting a treatment. ([PubMed][1])
* In a separate 101-child trial, a heel raise, supervised eccentric exercise/physical therapy, and a “wait-and-see” approach all reduced pain. There were some six-week differences in satisfaction and function, but **no clinically important difference between treatments at three months**. ([PubMed][2])
* A randomized crossover study of 51 boys found considerably less sport-related pain with a molded heel cup than with a simple heel wedge; more than 75% preferred the cup. This was a relatively small, all-male study, however. ([PubMed][3])
* A 208-child trial reported substantially greater 12-week pain improvement with custom polypropylene orthoses than with off-the-shelf heel lifts. That large advantage has not been reproduced consistently in the other orthosis trials, so it does not establish that every child needs an expensive custom device. ([PMC][4])

Taken together, the trials support **trying a comfortable heel cup, heel lift, or orthosis**, but they do not show a dependable long-term advantage of custom devices over simpler alternatives.

### Physical therapy and activity modification

Physical therapy may be useful when there is calf tightness, weakness, movement difficulty, or trouble progressing back to sport. However, the trial comparing supervised exercise with heel raises and wait-and-see management did not find one approach clearly superior by three months. ([PubMed][2])

“Wait and see” in this context should not necessarily be interpreted as ignoring the pain; it generally involved avoiding or adjusting activities that aggravated symptoms.

### Taping and braces

Kinesio tape does not appear to add meaningful pain relief. In a trial of 22 male youth football players, Kinesio tape did not improve pain compared with sham tape, although there was a very small difference in a foot-function score. ([PubMed][5])

For children participating in barefoot sports, a 2023 trial found no difference between two commercially available braces. Both groups improved over three months, so comfort, usability, and cost are reasonable deciding factors. ([PubMed][6])

**Best-supported heel approach:** pain-guided activity adjustment plus a comfortable heel cup or lift; add individualized exercise or physical therapy when needed. Replacing otherwise suitable shoes solely to treat Sever disease has not shown benefit.

## 2. Knee: Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Activity modification plus progressive strengthening

A 12-week prospective study followed 51 adolescents using:

* Education and pain monitoring
* An activity ladder to adjust patellar-tendon loading
* Progressive knee strengthening
* Gradual return to sport

At 12 weeks, 80% reported improvement, increasing to 90% at one year. Strength and jumping measures also improved. Only 16% had returned to sport at 12 weeks, however, compared with 69% at one year, suggesting that recovery and full sports participation can take considerably longer than pain reduction alone. Because there was no control group, this study cannot determine how much improvement resulted from treatment versus time. ([PubMed][7])

A more informative 2025 randomized trial included 130 adolescents. A standardized self-management program was **not superior to usual care** at five months. Both groups received varying combinations of exercise, education, and activity modification, and both improved by more than 20 points on the knee sport/function scale. Approximately 36%–44% still had not reached an acceptable symptom state. This has so far been published as a peer-reviewed conference abstract rather than a complete journal article. ([BMJ Open Seminars][8])

These results suggest that **consistent load management, strengthening, education, and follow-up are probably more important than one rigid, standardized exercise package**.

### Dextrose injections

The evidence is conflicting.

* A 2011 trial involving 54 adolescent athletes and 65 symptomatic knees found better three-month sport outcomes with dextrose plus lidocaine injections than with lidocaine injections or supervised usual care. However, the analysis counted knees rather than independent children, and participants could cross over to dextrose after three months, making the one-year comparison less definitive. ([PubMed][9])
* A later double-blind pediatric trial involving 49 knees found **no difference between dextrose plus lidocaine and saline plus lidocaine** at any measured time point. Both groups improved. ([PubMed][10])

Because one pediatric trial was positive and another was negative, dextrose injection cannot currently be considered the best treatment or a routine first-line treatment.

### Dexamethasone iontophoresis

A randomized trial compared physical therapy plus dexamethasone iontophoresis, physical therapy plus saline iontophoresis, and physical therapy alone. It enrolled only 45 of a planned 147 participants and was terminated because of slow enrollment. The treatment is described as investigational for pediatric knee apophysitis, and the under-enrollment prevents a firm effectiveness conclusion. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][11])

**Best-supported Osgood–Schlatter approach:** adjust painful running, jumping, squatting, and kicking loads rather than prescribing indefinite complete rest; then progressively rebuild quadriceps, hip, and sport-specific capacity. Injections and medication-delivery procedures have insufficient or conflicting evidence.

## 3. Other knee apophysitis: Sinding–Larsen–Johansson disease

I did not identify a comparative treatment trial in children.

One case report described a 13-year-old football player treated by stopping sports participation; symptoms and ultrasound abnormalities resolved after approximately five months. A single case cannot determine whether complete rest is better than graded activity or exercise. ([PubMed][12])

## 4. Outer-foot apophysitis: Iselin disease

The main primary treatment study was a retrospective series of 27 young athletes. Treatments varied according to severity and included rest, shoe modification, a walking boot, or a postoperative shoe. Treatment lasted an average of 24 days, and average return to play was 38 days. All cases healed, although three reinjuries occurred. Because patients were not randomized and the treatment groups were not directly compared, the study cannot tell us whether a boot is better than rest or shoe modification. ([PubMed][13])

**Reasonable interpretation:** start with load reduction and footwear modification; temporary immobilization may be used for children whose pain or walking difficulty is more substantial, but comparative evidence is absent.

## 5. Hip and pelvic apophysitis

I found **no randomized or controlled nonsurgical treatment trial** for iliac-crest, pelvic, or greater-trochanter apophysitis.

Available primary reports describe:

* Relative rest or cessation of the painful sport
* Sometimes temporary limited weight bearing
* Physical therapy emphasizing flexibility, strength, and movement recovery
* A progressive running and return-to-sport program

Four adolescent athletes with greater-trochanter apophysitis were treated with stopping sport and physical therapy; all returned to sport, generally within roughly two to three months, although the study had no comparison group. ([PubMed][14]) A separate 15-year-old basketball player returned successfully after relative rest and anti-inflammatory medication. ([PubMed][15])

A 2025 case report described a 16-year-old triathlete with an iliac-crest injury and contralateral apophysitis who completed a staged running progression over four months and remained pain-free at one year. ([PubMed][16]) A June 2026 case report described full return after eight weeks of activity modification, limited weight bearing, and physical therapy. ([PubMed][17])

Thus, the hip evidence supports only the broad strategy of **temporary unloading followed by rehabilitation and criteria-based return**, not a particular exercise, medication, or duration of rest.

## Overall treatment hierarchy supported by the primary evidence

1. **Pain-guided load modification** rather than automatically stopping all activity for a prolonged period.
2. **Progressive strengthening and gradual return to sport**, especially for Osgood–Schlatter disease.
3. **Site-specific offloading:** heel cup/lift for Sever disease; shoe modification or short-term boot use for symptomatic Iselin disease.
4. **Individualized physical therapy** when symptoms persist, strength or flexibility deficits are present, or return to sport is difficult.
5. **Do not routinely escalate to dextrose injections, steroid iontophoresis, or therapeutic taping**, because the pediatric evidence is conflicting, underpowered, or negative.
