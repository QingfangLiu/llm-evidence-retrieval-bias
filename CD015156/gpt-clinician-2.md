## Bottom line

No nonsurgical treatment has convincing, high-certainty evidence of being **best across lower-limb apophysitis**. The evidence is diagnosis-specific and mostly small, heterogeneous, and short-term.

* **Calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease):** Heel cushioning—particularly a heel cup—or a heel lift is the most defensible low-burden treatment. It may reduce pain sooner, but differences between devices, physiotherapy, and observation usually disappear by approximately 3 months.
* **Osgood–Schlatter disease:** Education, symptom-guided load modification, progressive strengthening, and gradual return to sport are reasonable first-line care, but a standardized program has not proved superior to good usual care. Injection evidence is inconsistent.
* **Iselin disease:** One recent nonrandomized study favors relative immobilization plus early rehabilitation over complete rest, but this requires replication.
* I did not identify useful comparative treatment studies for **Sinding-Larsen–Johansson syndrome, iliac-crest apophysitis, or ischial apophysitis**.

I searched the current primary literature through **July 25, 2026**, excluding reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee statements, and editorials from the evidence synthesis.

## Evidence by condition

### Calcaneal apophysitis

**Heel cups, heel cushioning, or heel lifts have the most consistent comparative support.** A randomized crossover study in 51 boys found less activity-related pain with a heel cup than with a heel wedge; more than three-quarters preferred the heel cup. A related biomechanical study found that the cup increased functional heel-pad thickness and reduced peak pressure. ([PubMed][1])

However, the size and duration of benefit appear modest:

* In a 101-child pragmatic RCT, a heel raise and supervised eccentric-exercise physiotherapy produced some early differences in satisfaction or foot-related function, but by 3 months there were no clinically important pain differences among heel raise, physiotherapy, and wait-and-see groups. ([PubMed][2])
* In a 124-child factorial RCT, heel raises had a small early advantage over prefabricated orthoses on one physical-function domain, but footwear and orthosis assignments did not produce meaningful differences at 6 or 12 months. ([PubMed][3])

**Custom orthoses are promising but not conclusively superior.** One relatively large RCT found substantially larger 12-week pain improvements with custom polypropylene orthoses than with off-the-shelf heel lifts. This result has not been independently replicated and differs from the less impressive results obtained with prefabricated orthoses. ([PMC][4])

Other modalities are less compelling:

* Kinesio taping did not improve pain compared with sham taping in a 22-athlete trial; a small functional-score difference is difficult to interpret because of the sample size and baseline differences. ([Springer][5])
* In barefoot athletes, a heel cup and a more extensive heel/ankle brace produced similar 3-month pain and functional improvement. ([PubMed][6])

**Clinical interpretation:** Start with an inexpensive heel cup or heel lift that fits the child’s sport and footwear. A custom orthosis is more reasonably reserved for persistent symptoms, significant biomechanical issues, or failure of simpler cushioning rather than used routinely for every child.

### Osgood–Schlatter disease

#### Load management and exercise

A prospective cohort of 51 adolescents used a pain-monitoring/activity ladder, progressive knee strengthening, and gradual return to sport. Eighty percent reported improvement at 12 weeks and 90% at 12 months, but only 16% had returned to sport at 12 weeks and 69% at 12 months. Because there was no control group, natural improvement and regression to the mean cannot be separated from the treatment effect. ([PMC][7])

The subsequent SOGOOD randomized trial compared a standardized self-management program with usual care in 130 adolescents. Both arms included variations of education, exercise, and activity modification. The standardized program was **not superior** for KOOS-Child sport/play at 5 months; both groups improved by more than 20 points. This is presently available as a conference abstract rather than a complete results article. ([BMJ Open Seminars][8])

**Clinical interpretation:** Active rehabilitation is preferable to prolonged blanket rest because it preserves strength and provides a graded path back to activity, but the evidence does not identify one particular exercise protocol as superior. Symptom-guided loading and individual follow-up probably matter more than a fixed program.

#### Dextrose injections

The two pediatric randomized trials conflict:

* Topol et al randomized 54 adolescent athletes/65 knees to usual care, lidocaine injection, or lidocaine plus 12.5% dextrose. At 3 months, asymptomatic sport was achieved in 14/21 dextrose-treated knees, 5/22 lidocaine-treated knees, and 3/22 usual-care knees. Interpretation beyond 3 months is complicated because participants could cross over to dextrose. ([PubMed][9])
* Nakase et al randomized 43 analyzed knees to ultrasound-guided 20% dextrose plus lidocaine or saline plus lidocaine. Both groups improved, with no significant between-group advantage for dextrose. ([PubMed][10])

A separate positive dextrose RCT by Wu et al involved military personnel with a mean age around 22 years, so it does not directly answer the pediatric question.

**Clinical interpretation:** Dextrose injection cannot currently be considered an established first-line pediatric treatment. It may be discussed for persistent, sports-limiting Osgood–Schlatter disease after an adequate rehabilitation trial, but the child and family should understand that pediatric placebo-controlled results are inconsistent.

#### Platelet-rich plasma

PRP findings are favorable but substantially less reliable than randomized evidence:

* An uncontrolled series of 152 children receiving leukocyte-rich PRP alongside conservative care reported 75% satisfaction and 72% return to full activity, with improvements from baseline in pain and knee scores. There was no untreated or active comparator. ([PMC][11])
* A related observational analysis found better PRP outcomes in acute than chronic Osgood–Schlatter disease, but comparison by symptom duration does not establish that PRP was responsible for recovery. ([MDPI][12])
* A 2025 retrospective study of 40 adolescents with refractory symptoms found greater 12-week pain and Lysholm-score improvement and faster return to sport with one PRP injection than with three 10% dextrose injections. Allocation depended on treatment availability and patient preference, follow-up was short, and each group contained only 20 patients. ([PMC][13])

**Clinical interpretation:** PRP has an emerging signal for refractory disease, but the evidence is not sufficient to conclude that it is superior to rehabilitation, placebo injection, or natural recovery. Its invasiveness, cost, preparation variability, and lack of long-term growth-plate safety data argue against routine pediatric use.

#### Dexamethasone iontophoresis

A randomized, triple-masked trial compared physical therapy plus dexamethasone iontophoresis, physical therapy plus saline iontophoresis, and physical therapy alone. Recruitment was terminated early for slow enrollment after 45 of a planned 147 participants, leaving the trial too small to provide a dependable treatment estimate. Results are registry-only; I did not locate a full journal report. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][14])

### Iselin disease

A 2026 prospective but nonrandomized cohort compared relative immobilization with an ankle brace or strapping plus early physiotherapy against strict rest in 44 adolescents. Mean pain-free return to sport was 5.8 versus 8.3 weeks, and 4-week pain was lower in the combined-treatment group. Because assignment was not randomized, selection, motivation, and baseline-severity differences could account for some of the result. ([ScienceDirect][15])

An earlier retrospective series of 27 adolescents treated variably with rest, footwear modification, a walking boot, or a postoperative shoe found that all cases healed; mean return to play was 38 days. It did not compare the treatments. ([PubMed][16])

**Clinical interpretation:** Relative immobilization sufficient to settle pain, followed promptly by graded mobility, peroneal flexibility work, proximal strengthening, and sport-specific progression appears more rational than extended complete inactivity. Evidence remains low certainty.

## Practical evidence-based hierarchy

1. **Use low-risk, diagnosis-specific mechanical and loading strategies first.** For Sever disease, trial heel cushioning or a lift. For knee apophysitis, use pain-guided activity modification and progressive strengthening rather than prolonged immobilization.
2. **Do not promise that orthoses or formal PT shorten the overall natural history.** Their clearest value is short-term symptom control and helping the child remain appropriately active.
3. **Escalate devices selectively.** Custom orthoses may help some children with persistent Sever disease but have not shown consistent superiority across trials.
4. **Reserve injections for unusually persistent, function-limiting Osgood–Schlatter disease.** Dextrose results conflict, and PRP evidence is mainly observational. Neither has established pediatric superiority or robust long-term safety evidence.
5. **Evidence for ice, oral/topical NSAIDs, stretching alone, patellar straps, massage, and complete rest is particularly thin.** These were frequently included as background co-interventions, making their independent effects impossible to determine.
