## Bottom line

No nonsurgical treatment has been shown to be clearly best for **all** lower-limb apophysitis. The evidence is strongest for calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease), weaker for Osgood–Schlatter disease, and limited mainly to case series for hip/pelvic, Sinding-Larsen–Johansson, and fifth-metatarsal apophysitis.

For most children, the most defensible starting approach is:

1. **Temporarily reduce the specific painful load**, rather than necessarily stopping all physical activity.
2. **Progressively restore strength, flexibility, and sport loading**, guided by symptoms.
3. For heel apophysitis, add an inexpensive **heel cup, cushioned heel insert, or heel raise** when it makes walking or sport more comfortable.
4. Consider short-term bracing or a removable boot when ordinary walking remains painful.
5. Reserve injections and other more invasive treatments for specialist review because the results are inconsistent and pediatric safety data are limited.

## What the individual studies show

### 1. Osgood–Schlatter disease: tibial-tubercle apophysitis

**Activity modification and progressive strengthening appear reasonable, but no particular program has proved superior.** In a prospective cohort of 51 adolescents, a 12-week program incorporating education, a pain-guided activity ladder, knee strengthening, and gradual return to sport produced self-reported improvement in 80% at 12 weeks and 90% at one year. Return to sport was slower: 16% at 12 weeks and 69% at one year. Because there was no control group, some improvement could represent natural recovery or regression to the mean. ([PubMed][1])

A subsequently completed randomized trial assigned 130 adolescents to a standardized self-management program or usual care. The standardized program was **not superior** for sport/play function at five months; both groups improved by more than 20 KOOS-Child points, but approximately 36%–44% had not reached an acceptable symptom state. This suggests that follow-up and individualized progression may matter more than one standardized program. The findings are currently available as a conference abstract rather than a full trial report. ([BMJ Open Seminars][2])

**Dextrose injections have conflicting results.** An older randomized study of 54 adolescent athletes found that lidocaine injections, particularly when combined with dextrose, produced faster return to unaltered or pain-free sport than supervised usual care. However, after the blinded phase, participants could cross over to dextrose, making the one-year comparison more difficult to interpret. ([PubMed][3])

A later double-blind trial involving 38 children found no significant difference between ultrasound-guided dextrose and saline injections at any measured time point; both groups improved. ([PubMed][4]) Consequently, dextrose injection cannot currently be considered a reliably established treatment for children.

**Dexamethasone iontophoresis has not shown convincing benefit.** A three-group randomized trial comparing dexamethasone iontophoresis plus physical therapy, saline iontophoresis plus physical therapy, and physical therapy alone was terminated after enrolling 45 participants because of slow recruitment. Posted results did not demonstrate a clear advantage in pain, function, or return-to-sport measures, and the small numbers make clinically meaningful differences difficult to exclude. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][5])

**Practical interpretation:** start with load management and progressive rehabilitation. Injections should not be routine first-line treatment.

### 2. Calcaneal apophysitis: Sever disease

This condition has the most comparative research.

A randomized trial of 101 children compared:

* Pain-guided “wait and see”
* A silicone heel raise
* Physiotherapist-supervised eccentric calf exercise

All three groups improved substantially. Heel raises produced greater early satisfaction and some early functional advantages, but there was no clinically relevant difference between treatments at three months. ([PubMed][6])

A 124-child factorial randomized trial compared heel raises with prefabricated orthoses, with or without replacement athletic shoes. Heel raises had a small short-term physical-function advantage at one to two months, but there was no meaningful advantage for either insert or footwear strategy at six or twelve months. ([PubMed][7])

Two smaller crossover studies in highly active boys found that inserts reduced pain during sport without requiring cessation of sport. A contoured heel cup performed better than a simple heel wedge during activity and was preferred by most participants. These studies were small, involved only boys, and had limited blinding. ([PubMed][8])

A larger randomized trial of 208 children found substantially greater 12-week pain improvement with custom-made polypropylene orthoses than with inexpensive heel lifts. There was no untreated group, however, and this finding is not fully consistent with the other orthosis trials. ([PMC][9])

Kinesiology tape was compared with sham tape in 22 young male footballers. Pain outcomes were not significantly different; the kinesiology-tape group had somewhat better foot-and-ankle function scores. The very small study does not support taping as a primary pain treatment. ([PubMed][10])

For barefoot athletes, a trial comparing two commercially available braces found that both groups improved, with no meaningful difference between a heel-cup brace and an elastic heel-strap brace. Eleven of 43 enrolled participants did not complete the study. ([PubMed][11])

**Practical interpretation:** inexpensive heel cushioning or a heel raise is a reasonable early option if it helps immediately. Supervised exercise and pain-guided activity modification are also reasonable. Custom orthoses may help selected children, particularly with relevant foot-mechanics problems, but the evidence does not establish that their additional cost is routinely necessary. Replacing otherwise serviceable shoes has not shown an important long-term effect.

### 3. Iselin disease: fifth-metatarsal-base apophysitis

A retrospective study of 27 athletic adolescents treated with combinations of rest, shoe modification, a postoperative shoe, or a removable walking boot reported healing in all patients. Average treatment duration was 24 days and average return to play was 38 days, but the treatments were not randomized or directly compared. Three children experienced reinjury. ([PubMed][12])

A 2026 prospective cohort compared 22 adolescents receiving relative immobilization with early physiotherapy against 22 managed with strict rest alone. Return to sport averaged 5.8 versus 8.3 weeks, and four-week pain was lower with the combined program. Because treatment allocation was not randomized, baseline or clinician-selection differences could partly explain the apparent advantage. ([ScienceDirect][13])

**Practical interpretation:** temporary support with a brace, stiff-soled shoe, or boot plus early symptom-guided rehabilitation may be preferable to prolonged complete inactivity, but this conclusion currently rests on one nonrandomized comparative study.

### 4. Hip and pelvic apophysitis

There are no convincing randomized treatment trials.

A classic case series described 21 adolescent runners with iliac-crest apophyseal overuse injuries; cases resolved after approximately four to six weeks of rest. It did not compare rest with rehabilitation or another treatment. ([Sage Journals][14])

A case series of 26 adolescent footballers with pubic apophysitis used complete initial rest followed by supervised stretching, trunk and pelvic control work, and progressive football-specific exercises. All returned to their prior level without recurrence during at least six months of follow-up; return took six weeks to four months. With no comparison group, the study supports feasibility rather than proving efficacy. ([PMC][15])

Four young athletes with greater-trochanter apophysitis were reported to recover following cessation of sport and physical therapy incorporating flexibility and strengthening. A separate 15-year-old basketball player recovered after relative rest and anti-inflammatory medication. ([ScienceDirect][16])

Recent individual cases of iliac-crest apophysitis have also recovered with reduced loading, limited weight bearing when necessary, physical therapy, and staged return to running or sport, but case reports cannot determine which component is responsible. ([PubMed][17])

**Practical interpretation:** the available evidence supports initial unloading followed by staged rehabilitation, but the optimal duration of rest, exercise selection, and return-to-sport criteria are unknown.

### 5. Sinding-Larsen–Johansson disease

Treatment evidence consists predominantly of individual cases. One 13-year-old footballer was instructed to stop sport and had complete clinical and ultrasound recovery after five months. This does not establish that complete sports cessation is better than modified loading. ([PubMed][18])

## Treatment ranking based on the primary studies

| Treatment approach                                                    | What the primary evidence supports                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pain-guided activity modification plus progressive rehabilitation** | Best overall first-line strategy, although the ideal program is unknown. Particularly reasonable for Osgood–Schlatter and hip/pelvic apophysitis.                               |
| **Heel cup, cushioning, or heel raise for Sever disease**             | Often provides short-term symptom relief. No specific insert is consistently superior over the longer term.                                                                     |
| **Short-term brace, stiff shoe, or removable boot**                   | Reasonable for significant heel or fifth-metatarsal pain, especially when walking hurts; limited comparative evidence.                                                          |
| **Custom foot orthoses**                                              | One large trial favored them over heel lifts at 12 weeks, but other trials found little lasting difference. Best reserved for selected children rather than used automatically. |
| **Supervised physical therapy**                                       | Outcomes are generally favorable, but it has not consistently beaten simple heel raises, usual care, or pain-guided observation.                                                |
| **Kinesiology tape**                                                  | Does not appear to add meaningful pain relief over sham tape.                                                                                                                   |
| **Dextrose injections for Osgood–Schlatter**                          | Conflicting randomized results; not established as routine pediatric treatment.                                                                                                 |
| **Dexamethasone iontophoresis**                                       | No convincing evidence of additional benefit over physical therapy or placebo iontophoresis.                                                                                    |
| **New athletic shoes alone**                                          | No demonstrated meaningful long-term benefit for Sever disease.                                                                                                                 |

Because many children improve over time, within-group improvement should not automatically be attributed to the treatment. Most trials were small, rarely blinded, concentrated on highly active boys, and incompletely reported adverse effects.
