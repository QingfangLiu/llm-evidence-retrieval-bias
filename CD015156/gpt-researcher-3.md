## Bottom line

No nonsurgical treatment has been shown to be **consistently superior across lower-limb apophysitis conditions**. Almost all comparative evidence concerns **calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease)** and **tibial-tubercle apophysitis (Osgood–Schlatter disease, OSD)**.

* **Sever disease:** Heel cups or heel raises can provide short-term symptom relief, but larger pragmatic trials found little or no clinically important difference between inserts, supervised exercise/physical therapy, or a wait-and-see approach by approximately 3–12 months. Thus, inexpensive heel cushioning or elevation appears reasonable, but no device is clearly best long term. ([PubMed][1])
* **OSD:** Education, activity/load modification, progressive strengthening, and gradual return to sport are associated with improvement, but the recent randomized SOGOOD trial did not show that a standardized self-management package was superior to usual care. ([PubMed][2])
* **Dextrose injections for OSD:** Results conflict. Two trials favored dextrose, particularly at short-term follow-up, whereas another double-blind trial found no superiority to saline/lidocaine. The evidence is therefore insufficient to call dextrose the best treatment. ([PubMed][3])
* For **Sinding-Larsen–Johansson disease, Iselin disease, and pelvic apophysitis**, I did not locate comparative pediatric treatment studies capable of determining relative effectiveness.

## Calcaneal apophysitis

### Heel cups, heel raises and foot orthoses

The most reproducible finding is that **heel inserts may relieve pain during activity in the short term**, but the particular insert often matters little.

In a randomized crossover study of 51 boys, a rigid heel cup produced substantially less sports pain than a heel wedge, with more than 75% preferring the heel cup. A preceding randomized-phase study of 38 boys also found substantially less activity pain while either of two inserts was being worn than during periods without an insert. ([PubMed][4])

However, the larger 124-participant factorial trial found only a small physical-function advantage for heel raises over prefabricated orthoses at one to two months. By six and 12 months, there were no differences attributable to insert type or replacement footwear. ([PubMed][5])

A separate three-arm pragmatic RCT of 101 children compared wait-and-see, a heel raise, and supervised eccentric exercise/physical therapy. All three improved. Heel raises produced greater satisfaction and some functional advantages at six weeks, but there was no clinically relevant pain difference at three months. ([PubMed][1])

### Custom orthoses

A 2021 RCT of 208 children reported much greater 12-week pain improvement from custom polypropylene foot orthoses than from off-the-shelf heel lifts. The reported between-group VAS improvement was very large. ([PMC][6])

This result conflicts with the larger pragmatic trial, which found little difference between prefabricated orthoses and heel raises. Differences in diagnosis, selected population, orthosis construction, co-interventions and outcome methods may explain some of the discrepancy. Consequently, **custom orthoses are promising in selected children but cannot yet be considered generally superior or cost-effective**.

### Physical therapy, observation and taping

The wait-and-see versus heel-raise versus physical-therapy trial suggests that supervised eccentric exercise is **not clearly better** than simpler care by three months. It may nevertheless be useful where strength, flexibility, graded loading or return-to-sport support is specifically needed. ([PubMed][1])

In a small sham-controlled trial of 22 young footballers, Kinesio taping did not produce a significant pain advantage over sham tape. A statistically better AOFAS score was reported, but the final difference was only about two points and its clinical importance is doubtful. ([PubMed][7])

Among barefoot athletes, two commercially available braces produced comparable improvements, with no between-brace differences in function or pain at three months. ([PubMed][8])

### Interpretation for Sever disease

The comparative evidence most strongly supports:

1. **A heel cup or heel raise for short-term comfort**, selected according to footwear, sport and preference.
2. **Load adjustment with gradual return to activity**, with supervised rehabilitation where needed.
3. Avoiding assumptions that expensive custom orthoses, new shoes, Kinesio tape or one particular brace routinely provide superior long-term outcomes.

## Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Education, load modification and strengthening

In a prospective uncontrolled cohort of 51 adolescents with longstanding OSD, a 12-week program of activity modification, pain monitoring, progressive knee strengthening and gradual return to sport was associated with improvement in 80% at 12 weeks and 90% at one year. Only 16% had returned to sport at 12 weeks, increasing to 69% at one year. Because there was no control group, maturation, regression to the mean and natural recovery cannot be separated from treatment effects. ([PubMed][2])

More importantly, the 130-participant SOGOOD randomized trial found that a standardized self-management program was **not superior to usual care** for KOOS-Child Sport/Play at five months: the between-group change difference was −4.8 points. Both groups improved by more than 20 points, although 36%–44% still had not reached an acceptable symptom state. Both treatments included varying amounts of exercise, education and activity modification, so the trial does not test these components against no treatment. The findings instead suggest that **individual follow-up may matter more than one standardized program**. Results are currently available as a peer-reviewed conference abstract rather than a full results article. ([BMJ Open Seminars][9])

### Dextrose injections

Three randomized trials provide inconsistent findings.

* **Topol et al., 2011:** Sixty-five symptomatic knees were assigned to 12.5% dextrose plus lidocaine, lidocaine alone, or supervised usual care. At three months, asymptomatic sport was reported in 14/21 dextrose-treated knees, 5/22 lidocaine-treated knees and 3/22 usual-care knees. Subsequent crossover to dextrose makes the one-year comparisons less conventionally randomized. ([PubMed][3])
* **Nakase et al., 2020:** In 43 analyzed knees, 20% dextrose plus lidocaine was not superior to saline plus lidocaine on VISA scores at any measured time. Both groups improved. ([PubMed][10])
* **Wu et al., 2022:** In 70 patients, ultrasound-guided 12.5% dextrose produced substantially greater VISA-P improvement than saline at three months; the difference narrowed but remained statistically significant at six and 12 months. Both groups demonstrated clinically important longer-term improvement. ([PubMed][11])

Differences in injected solution, injection location, outcome instruments, patient selection and control interventions complicate synthesis. Overall, **dextrose may help some refractory cases, but replication in larger independently conducted trials is required before it can be ranked above noninvasive care**.

### Other modalities

A terminated 45-participant randomized trial compared dexamethasone iontophoresis plus physical therapy, saline iontophoresis plus physical therapy, and physical therapy alone. Results were posted only to the registry, and the trial was under-recruited relative to its intended sample, limiting precision and interpretability. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][12])

A Russian three-arm study randomized 90 adolescents to exercise alone, exercise plus calcium-phosphorus iontophoresis, or exercise plus iontophoresis and interferential therapy. The combination group had better reported 12-month pain, flexion and ultrasound outcomes, but the publication provides limited information about allocation concealment, baseline comparability, missing data and between-group estimates. It needs independent replication. ([RCSI Journals Platform][13])

A small randomized study reported better outcomes from shock-wave therapy than interferential therapy when each was added to conventional therapy and a brace. Reporting and methodological details are insufficient to support a confident estimate. ([ResearchGate][14])

A 14-patient uncontrolled ESWT series reported generally favorable long-term outcomes, but four of 16 treated knees still caused sports pain. Without a comparator, the contribution of ESWT cannot be distinguished from natural recovery. ([PubMed][15])

A 2025 retrospective comparison of 40 adolescents with refractory OSD reported better 12-week pain, Lysholm scores and return-to-sport time after one platelet-rich-plasma injection than after three 10% dextrose injections. Treatment was selected according to availability and patient preference rather than randomized, creating substantial confounding-by-indication and selection risk. ([Cureus][16])

Four weeks of cast immobilization was followed by a median return to sport of 11 weeks and return to the previous performance level at 16 weeks in an uncontrolled 30-patient cohort. A subsequent 72-participant randomized cast-versus-sport-rest trial was completed, but no results have been posted, so immobilization cannot be compared reliably with rest or active rehabilitation. ([ScienceDirect][17])

## Overall evidence ranking

**Most defensible noninvasive approach**

For both principal conditions, the evidence favors a **preference-sensitive, low-burden approach consisting of symptom-guided load adjustment, maintenance of tolerable activity and progressive return to sport**. Strengthening or supervised therapy can be added where function or confidence is limited. This is not because one particular program has demonstrated clear superiority, but because improvement occurs with several versions of conservative care and harms are likely to be low.

**Best-supported adjunct for Sever disease**

A **heel cup or heel raise** has the clearest evidence for short-term comfort. There is no convincing evidence that one insert, new footwear, tape or a brace is consistently superior at longer follow-up.

**Uncertain interventions**

Custom orthoses for Sever disease and dextrose injections for OSD each have positive trials, but their results conflict with other comparative studies. They should therefore be characterized as **potentially effective, not established as best**.

**Insufficient evidence**

PRP, ESWT, interferential therapy, mineral iontophoresis, corticosteroid iontophoresis and immobilization have either uncontrolled, underpowered, poorly reported or nonrandomized evidence.
