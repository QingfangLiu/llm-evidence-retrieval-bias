## Bottom line

I found **24 primary study reports or trial records** evaluating nonsurgical treatment outcomes for lower-limb apophysitis in children or adolescents. The evidence is concentrated in **calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease)** and **Osgood–Schlatter disease (OSD)**.

No single treatment can presently be identified as best across conditions:

* **Calcaneal apophysitis:** inexpensive heel cups or heel raises, symptom-guided activity, and exercise/physical therapy generally produce improvement. Most pragmatic trials found little or no long-term difference between approaches. One relatively large 12-week trial strongly favored custom orthoses over heel lifts, but that finding has not been replicated and differs from another orthosis trial.
* **Osgood–Schlatter disease:** education, load modification, progressive strengthening, and graded return to sport have the most favorable risk–benefit profile, but superiority over simpler usual care remains unproven.
* **Dextrose injections for OSD:** randomized results conflict.
* **PRP for OSD:** promising signals come only from uncontrolled or nonrandomized studies.
* **Iselin disease:** one recent nonrandomized comparison favored bracing/strapping plus early rehabilitation over strict rest, but the evidence is very low certainty.
* **Sinding-Larsen–Johansson and pelvic/hip apophysitis:** I found case reports or small series, but no comparative treatment trials.

This is a targeted evidence map rather than a formal PRISMA systematic review. I excluded systematic and narrative reviews, guidelines, opinions, editorials, surgery, and acute apophyseal avulsion-fracture management.

## Calcaneal apophysitis

### Activity modification, heel raise, or physical therapy

A pragmatic randomized trial assigned 101 children aged 8–15 years to wait-and-see, a heel raise, or supervised physical therapy incorporating eccentric exercise. All three groups improved, with **no clinically relevant between-group difference at three months**. At six weeks, there were some transient advantages in satisfaction or questionnaire domains for the heel-raise and physical-therapy groups. ([PubMed][1])

**Interpretation:** For many children, reassurance and symptom-guided activity modification may perform similarly to a prescribed heel raise or supervised exercise by approximately three months. PT may still be valuable when calf weakness, restricted ankle motion, recurrent symptoms, or return-to-sport problems need attention.

### Heel raise versus orthosis and footwear replacement

In a 2×2 factorial randomized trial of 124 children aged 8–14 years, a heel raise produced a small advantage over a prefabricated orthosis in the physical domain at one and two months. There were **no meaningful differences at six or twelve months**, and replacing athletic footwear was not clearly superior to usual footwear. ([PubMed][2])

**Interpretation:** A simple heel raise appears at least as reasonable as a prefabricated orthosis for initial management, particularly when cost and convenience are considered.

### Custom orthosis versus heel lift

A randomized trial of 208 physically active children aged 9–12 years compared custom polypropylene foot orthoses with an 8-mm heel lift. Both groups also received stretching advice, reduced sports intensity, and ice. At 12 weeks, the custom-orthosis group had substantially greater improvements in pain and pressure-pain threshold. ([PMC][3])

Important qualifications include:

* Follow-up was limited to 12 weeks.
* The population had highly pronated average foot posture and predominantly comprised boys.
* The magnitude of benefit was considerably larger than in other orthosis trials.
* Cost-effectiveness was not established.

**Interpretation:** Custom orthoses may benefit selected children—particularly those with persistent symptoms or relevant foot mechanics—but one short-term trial is insufficient to conclude that every child should receive a custom device.

### Heel cup versus heel wedge

A randomized crossover study in 51 boys aged 9–14 years found substantially lower sports pain with a heel cup than with a heel wedge; more than 75% preferred the heel cup. Participants were able to continue their activity. ([Wiley Online Library][4])

A related crossover study involving 38 boys found that active periods using an insole were associated with lower pain than periods without the insole, without requiring reduced physical activity. ([PubMed][5])

A mechanistic report involving 50 boys suggested that the heel cup increased heel-pad thickness and reduced peak pressure, with these changes associated with pain reduction. This may overlap with the preceding research population and should not be counted as fully independent clinical confirmation. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** A heel cup is a plausible low-cost option, especially when impact-related heel pain occurs during sport. Evidence that it is superior to all other approaches is still limited.

### Taping

In a small sham-controlled study of 22 male youth football players, kinesiology taping did **not produce a meaningful pain advantage** over sham taping. A statistically different functional score was very small and likely of doubtful clinical importance. ([PubMed][7])

**Interpretation:** Taping should not be expected to provide a major treatment effect.

### Braces for barefoot athletes

A randomized trial compared a Cheetah heel cup with an X Brace in children aged 7–14 years participating in barefoot sports. Thirty-two of 43 enrolled participants completed the study. Both groups improved, with **no clear difference between braces**. ([PubMed][8])

**Interpretation:** Device selection in barefoot athletes can reasonably be based on comfort, sport compatibility, adherence, and cost.

### Practical conclusion for calcaneal apophysitis

The most defensible stepped approach from the primary evidence is:

1. Education, temporary adjustment of painful running/jumping loads, and a graded return.
2. A low-cost heel cup or heel raise when footwear permits.
3. Calf flexibility and progressive lower-limb strengthening, supervised when needed.
4. A custom orthosis mainly for persistent symptoms, substantial biomechanical indications, or failure of a simpler device.

This hierarchy reflects burden, cost, and safety as well as comparative effectiveness; it is not evidence that the first three components must always be combined.

## Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Education, activity modification, strengthening, and graded return

A prospective cohort of 51 adolescents aged 10–14 years used education, an activity ladder, knee-strengthening exercises, and gradual return to sport. At 12 weeks, 80% reported a successful outcome, increasing to 90% at 12 months. Return to sport was slower: 16% had returned at 12 weeks and 69% at 12 months. There was no control group, so improvement cannot be separated from natural history, regression to the mean, or nonspecific treatment effects. ([PMC][9])

A 2025 conference abstract reported a randomized trial of 130 adolescents aged 10–16 years comparing a standardized self-management intervention with usual care. Both groups received education, exercise, and activity modification. Both improved substantially, but the standardized program **was not superior on the prespecified primary analysis** at five months. Some secondary global-improvement outcomes favored the standardized program, while acceptable-symptom-state results did not. Full peer-reviewed reporting is needed. ([BMJ Open Seminars][10])

A retrospective series of 28 young professional football players treated with physiotherapy and kinesiotherapy without routine immobilization also reported eventual return to sport, but lacked a comparison group. ([PubMed][11])

**Interpretation:** Progressive load management is clinically reasonable and low risk, but current evidence supports it more strongly as a sensible management framework than as a proven superior treatment.

### Dextrose injection

A randomized trial involving 54 adolescent athletes and 65 affected knees compared usual care, lidocaine injection, and lidocaine plus 12.5% dextrose. At three months, return to unrestricted sport and asymptomatic participation favored the dextrose group. Interpretation after the initial blinded period is complicated by crossover treatment. ([PubMed][12])

A later randomized trial involving 38 children and 49 knees compared lidocaine plus 20% dextrose with lidocaine plus saline. Both groups improved, but **there was no significant difference between dextrose and saline at any assessed time point**. ([PubMed][13])

**Interpretation:** The pediatric randomized evidence is inconsistent. Differences in comparators, dextrose concentration, co-interventions, knee-level analyses, and sample size may contribute. Dextrose injection cannot currently be considered the established best treatment.

### Corticosteroid iontophoresis

A triple-masked randomized trial planned to compare dexamethasone iontophoresis plus physical therapy, saline iontophoresis plus physical therapy, and physical therapy alone. Recruitment was terminated because of slow enrollment after approximately 45 of a planned 147 participants. Registry results therefore represent an underpowered study rather than definitive evidence. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][14])

### Platelet-rich plasma

A prospective uncontrolled study of 152 patients with chronic OSD reported improved pain and function after leukocyte-rich PRP combined with standard conservative treatment; approximately three-quarters reported satisfaction and roughly 72% returned to full activity. Because there was no untreated, placebo, or active control, the independent effect of PRP cannot be determined. ([PubMed][15])

A 2025 retrospective, nonrandomized study compared one PRP injection with three 10% dextrose injections in 40 adolescents. PRP was associated with greater short-term pain and functional improvement and earlier return to sport. Treatment selection was not randomized and depended on preference or availability, follow-up was only 12 weeks, and the sample was small. ([PMC][16])

**Interpretation:** PRP remains investigational for OSD. Current studies cannot exclude selection bias, placebo effects, concomitant-treatment effects, or natural improvement.

### Immobilization and infrapatellar straps

An older cohort treated with approximately four weeks of cast immobilization reported return-to-sport outcomes, but did not provide a robust concurrent comparison establishing superiority. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][17])

An older comparative report evaluated an infrapatellar strap worn for approximately six to eight weeks. Limited reporting and dated methods make the effect difficult to quantify or apply to current practice. ([PubMed][18])

**Interpretation:** Routine casting appears difficult to justify for typical OSD without a specific indication, given deconditioning and activity restriction and the absence of strong comparative evidence. A strap may be tried for comfort, but evidence for disease modification is weak.

### Practical conclusion for OSD

Current evidence most strongly supports beginning with:

* Education about symptom-guided load.
* Reduction—not necessarily complete cessation—of provocative jumping, sprinting, kneeling, or high-volume sport.
* Progressive quadriceps, hip, calf, and kinetic-chain strengthening.
* Graded return based on symptoms and function.

Injection therapies should not be described as superior standard care. Dextrose has conflicting randomized results, PRP lacks adequate randomized controls, and the corticosteroid-iontophoresis trial was underpowered.

## Iselin disease

A retrospective series of 27 children with fifth-metatarsal apophysitis used rest, footwear modification, a walking boot, or a postoperative shoe according to clinical need. Mean treatment duration was approximately 24 days and mean return to sport approximately 38 days; all participants healed, although reinjuries occurred. The study did not compare the treatments reliably. ([PubMed][19])

A 2026 prospective but nonrandomized study of 44 children compared bracing or strapping plus early physical therapy with strict rest. The combined rehabilitation group had lower pain at four weeks and returned to sport sooner—approximately 5.8 versus 8.3 weeks. Because allocation was not randomized and the finding is unreplicated, confounding by initial severity, clinician choice, athlete motivation, or other factors remains possible. ([ScienceDirect][20])

**Interpretation:** Relative protection followed by early rehabilitation appears more promising than prolonged strict rest, but this conclusion is based on one small nonrandomized comparison.

## Sinding-Larsen–Johansson and pelvic/hip apophysitis

I did not identify a comparative nonsurgical treatment trial for Sinding-Larsen–Johansson syndrome. A case report of a 13-year-old boy described complete recovery after sports cessation over approximately five months. ([ScienceDirect][21])

For greater-trochanter apophysitis, a four-patient series reported successful management with sports cessation and physical therapy addressing strength and flexibility. ([PubMed][22])

Recent iliac-crest reports describe activity modification, temporary weight-bearing restriction where needed, rehabilitation, and staged return to sport, with eventual recovery. These are individual cases and cannot determine comparative effectiveness. ([PubMed][23])

**Interpretation:** For these less-studied sites, treatment is supported principally by clinical extrapolation and case-level evidence, not comparative trials.

## Overall evidence limitations

The treatment literature is limited by:

* Small or single-center samples.
* Few placebo or no-treatment controls.
* Natural improvement as skeletal maturation proceeds.
* Difficulty blinding children to devices, exercise, or injections.
* Short follow-up in many device and injection trials.
* Different pain, function, satisfaction, and return-to-sport outcomes.
* Analysis by symptomatic knee or heel rather than by participant in some studies.
* Overrepresentation of male competitive athletes.
* Poor reporting of adherence, adverse effects, recurrence, school participation, and cost.
* Very little evidence for Sinding-Larsen–Johansson, Iselin, iliac-crest, pubic, ischial, or greater-trochanter apophysitis.

Therefore, statistically significant findings—particularly from small, unblinded, uncontrolled, or nonrandomized studies—should not automatically be interpreted as proof of clinically important superiority.
