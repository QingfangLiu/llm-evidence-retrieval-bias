As an evidence-synthesis researcher, you already know that pediatric lower-limb apophysitis is typically managed conservatively, but finding primary intervention studies—rather than reviews, expert opinions, or clinical guidelines—can be challenging. Based on the primary literature, high-quality trials and cohort studies evaluating nonsurgical interventions predominantly focus on **Osgood-Schlatter disease** (tibial tuberosity) and **Sever's disease** (calcaneal apophysitis).

Here is a breakdown of the nonsurgical treatments evaluated in key primary studies:

## Osgood-Schlatter Disease

While rest and time are the traditional recommendations, structured physical therapy has been evaluated as an active intervention in primary studies. A prospective cohort study investigated the effects of patient education on activity modification combined with a targeted knee-strengthening program (Rathleff et al., 2020). The study provides primary data on how active rehabilitation and load management affect functional outcomes, emphasizing that adolescents do not necessarily need to cease all athletic activity if mechanical loads are carefully managed and strengthening protocols are applied.

## Sever's Disease (Calcaneal Apophysitis)

Primary studies on Sever's disease frequently compare different types of orthotics and physical therapy against watchful waiting.

* **Orthotic Selection (Heel Cups vs. Wedges):** A randomized crossover study investigated the effectiveness of different in-shoe orthotics during sports activities. The researchers found that firm heel cups reduced pain by 80% compared to cork heel wedges (Perhamre et al., 2011). Notably, when given an active choice, over 75% of the participants preferred the heel cup, which allowed them to maintain high levels of physical activity.
* **Comparing Modalities:** A pragmatic randomized clinical trial evaluated three conventional treatments: a "wait-and-see" protocol, a heel raise inlay, and an eccentric calf-strengthening exercise regimen supervised by a physical therapist (Wiegerinck et al., 2016). The trial demonstrated that all three modalities resulted in clinically relevant and statistically significant reductions in heel pain by three months. The study found no single intervention to be vastly superior for long-term pain reduction, though patients using heel raises reported higher satisfaction scores at the early six-week mark (Wiegerinck et al., 2016).
