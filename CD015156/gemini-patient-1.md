Watching an active child deal with growth-plate pain can be incredibly frustrating, especially when it forces them to step back from the sports and activities they love. Apophysitis occurs when repetitive pulling from a tendon irritates the still-soft cartilage of a growing child's growth plate (the apophysis).

To give you a clear picture of the evidence, I have bypassed the reviews, practice guidelines, and expert opinions to focus strictly on **individual, primary randomized clinical trials**.

The overarching takeaway from the primary literature is that most conservative treatments lead to similar, significant improvements over time. Because these conditions are largely tied to the child's physical growth and mechanical loading, there is rarely a single "magic bullet" intervention that vastly outperforms the others.

Here is what the primary clinical trials reveal about treating the most common types of lower-limb apophysitis.

## Sever's Disease (Heel Apophysitis)

Sever's disease occurs at the back of the heel where the Achilles tendon attaches to the calcaneus. Treatment usually focuses on offloading the tension from the calf muscles.

* **Orthotics vs. Physical Therapy vs. Wait-and-See:** A 2016 randomized trial by Wiegerinck et al. compared three distinct approaches in children: a "wait-and-see" approach with activity modification, wearing a heel-raise insert, and formal physical therapy focusing on calf exercises. The trial found that **all three groups experienced significant reductions in pain** and improvements in function over three months. Importantly, there was no clinically relevant difference in outcomes between the three groups. The study suggests that families can safely choose the treatment that best fits their lifestyle and budget.
* **Bracing for Barefoot Athletes:** Traditional heel cups require shoes, which leaves barefoot athletes (gymnasts, dancers, martial artists) without standard options. A 2023 trial by Sweeney et al. tested two specialized wearable braces (the Cheetah heel cup and The X Brace) designed for barefoot sports. The study found that **both braces significantly improved function and reduced pain** over three months, with neither brace proving superior to the other.

## Osgood-Schlatter Disease (Knee Apophysitis)

Osgood-Schlatter disease affects the tibial tuberosity—the bump just below the kneecap where the patellar tendon attaches.

* **Structured Self-Management vs. Usual Care:** A recent, large-scale 2025 trial by Krommes et al. randomized 130 adolescents to either a novel, structured self-management program (involving specific exercises, education, and guided activity modification) or standard usual care. At the 5-month mark, **both groups showed large, clinically meaningful improvements** in knee function and sports participation. However, the structured program was not statistically superior to usual care.
* **The Reality of Recovery Timelines:** The Krommes study also highlighted a crucial reality for parents: despite meaningful improvements, 36% to 44% of the adolescents in the study still did not consider their symptoms "acceptable" at 5 months. This data confirms that Osgood-Schlatter often requires prolonged patience and individual monitoring, rather than a quick fix.
