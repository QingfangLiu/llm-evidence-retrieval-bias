## Bottom line

The primary evidence is almost entirely for **calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease)** and **tibial-tubercle apophysitis (Osgood–Schlatter disease, OSD)**. I did not locate controlled treatment studies for Sinding-Larsen–Johansson disease, Iselin disease, or pelvic/iliac apophysitis.

**No nonsurgical treatment has been shown convincingly to be the single best treatment.**

* **Sever disease:** Heel cups, heel raises, and foot orthoses can reduce symptoms in the short term. However, larger pragmatic trials found little or no difference among heel raises, physical therapy, activity modification, footwear changes, or observation by approximately 3–12 months.
* **OSD:** Education, load/activity modification, progressive strengthening, and gradual return to sport are supported by improvement within treatment cohorts, but have not clearly outperformed usual care. Dextrose injection findings conflict, and dexamethasone iontophoresis has not demonstrated a reliable advantage.
* Across conditions, the evidence is limited by spontaneous improvement, small samples, short follow-up, nonblinding, co-interventions, heterogeneous outcomes, and—in several reports—analysis by symptomatic limb rather than participant.

This was a targeted search of primary intervention research and trial registries through **July 25, 2026**, not a formal systematic review.

## 1. Calcaneal apophysitis

### Heel raise versus orthosis; footwear replacement versus usual footwear

James and colleagues randomized 124 children aged 8–14 years in a 2×2 factorial trial. A heel raise produced a small advantage over a prefabricated orthosis in a physical-health measure at one and two months, but the treatments did not differ at six or 12 months. Replacing footwear also offered no sustained advantage over continuing usual footwear. This suggests that a relatively inexpensive heel raise is reasonable initially, but neither insert nor footwear strategy alters longer-term outcomes appreciably. ([PubMed][1])

### Observation versus heel raise versus physical therapy

Wiegerinck and colleagues randomized 101 children to:

1. Wait-and-see/activity modification
2. A heel-raise inlay
3. Supervised physical therapy emphasizing eccentric exercise

Some early satisfaction and function measures favored the heel raise or physical therapy, but by three months there were **no clinically relevant differences in pain, function, or satisfaction**. All three groups improved. This is important evidence against assuming that supervised treatment is necessary for every child. ([PubMed][2])

### Custom orthosis versus heel lift

A 2021 trial randomized 208 children aged 9–12 years to custom foot orthoses or off-the-shelf heel lifts. Both groups also received calf stretching, ice, and instructions to reduce sport intensity. At 12 weeks, the custom-orthosis group had substantially greater improvement in pain and pressure-pain threshold. The reported between-group difference in percentage pain reduction was large. ([PMC][3])

This is the strongest positive study for custom orthoses, but it should be interpreted cautiously:

* It compared custom orthoses with heel lifts, not with observation.
* Both arms received additional treatments.
* Its large effect is not reproduced in the longer factorial trial by James et al.
* Long-term durability beyond the trial period remains uncertain.

### Rigid heel cup versus heel wedge

In a randomized crossover trial of 51 boys, Perhamre and colleagues found that a rigid, custom-molded heel cup produced considerably less activity-related pain than a 5-mm heel wedge. More than 75% preferred the heel cup, and participants maintained their activity levels. ([Wiley Online Library][4])

A related biomechanical trial in 50 children found that a heel cup increased functional heel-pad thickness, reduced peak pressure, and reduced pain compared with wearing the sports shoe without the cup. The immediate within-participant comparison supports a plausible cushioning mechanism, although it provides less information about sustained clinical outcomes. ([Wiley Online Library][5])

An earlier randomized insole-type study in 38 boys also found lower pain during the insole-treatment phase than during periods without insoles, while activity remained unchanged. Because there was no concurrent untreated group and pain was compared across sequential phases, temporal effects cannot be excluded. ([PubMed][6])

### Bracing for barefoot athletes

Sweeney and colleagues randomized barefoot-sport athletes to a heel-cup brace or an alternative heel-support brace. Thirty-two of 43 enrolled children completed the study. Both groups improved over three months, but there were no meaningful between-brace differences in physical, play, or emotional function. Thus, either brace may be acceptable when footwear-based inserts cannot be used; neither proved superior. ([PMC][7])

### Kinesiology tape

A small sham-controlled study of 22 male junior footballers found better AOFAS functional scores with kinesiology tape at one and three months, but no significant between-group difference in pain. Given the sample size and discordance between function and pain outcomes, this is weak evidence for a clinically important taping effect. ([PubMed][8])

### Interpretation for Sever disease

The most defensible conclusion is:

> **Heel cups, heel raises, or orthoses can provide short-term symptom relief, but there is no established long-term winner.**

A low-cost heel cup or heel raise appears reasonable before custom orthoses. Custom orthoses may be considered when symptoms persist or when foot mechanics make them appropriate, but the evidence does not establish that every child benefits enough to justify routine custom fabrication.

Physical therapy may be useful when there are meaningful strength, mobility, or load-management deficits, but the randomized evidence does not show that supervised therapy consistently accelerates recovery relative to simpler management.

## 2. Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Activity modification and progressive strengthening

Rathleff and colleagues prospectively followed 51 adolescents receiving a 12-week program consisting of education, pain-guided activity modification, an activity ladder, knee strengthening, and gradual return to sport. Eighty percent reported improvement at 12 weeks and 90% at 12 months. Nevertheless, only 16% had returned to sport at 12 weeks and 69% at 12 months. Strength, jump performance, and patient-reported outcomes also improved. ([PMC][9])

These findings support feasibility and improvement during active rehabilitation, but the absence of a comparator means the improvements cannot be attributed confidently to the program rather than natural history, regression to the mean, or reduced provocative activity.

### Self-management versus usual care

A 2025 randomized trial abstract reported 130 adolescents assigned to a standardized self-management program or usual care. Both approaches included combinations of exercise, education, and activity modification. At five months, self-management was **not superior** for KOOS-Child Sport/Play; the between-group difference in change was −4.8 points. Both groups improved by more than 20 points, although 36%–44% still had not reached an acceptable symptom state. ([BMJ Open Seminars][10])

Because only a conference-abstract report was located, details concerning adherence, missing-data handling, adverse events, treatment contrast, and secondary analyses remain limited.

### Hyperosmolar dextrose injection

Topol and colleagues randomized 54 adolescent athletes, representing 65 symptomatic knees, to monthly injections for three months of:

* Dextrose plus lidocaine
* Lidocaine alone
* Supervised usual care

At three months, asymptomatic return to sport occurred in 14 of 21 dextrose-treated knees, compared with 5 of 22 lidocaine-treated and 3 of 22 usual-care knees. Longer-term results also favored dextrose, but treatment crossover and open-label dextrose use after the initial randomized period substantially weaken the one-year comparison. The limb-level analysis may additionally overstate precision when children contributed more than one knee. ([PubMed][11])

Nakase and colleagues subsequently conducted a prospective double-blind trial comparing dextrose-plus-lidocaine with saline-plus-lidocaine. Forty-three knees were analyzed. Both groups improved, but there were **no significant between-group differences in VISA scores at any follow-up**. No adverse effects were reported. ([PubMed][12])

Taken together, these trials do not establish a specific benefit of dextrose beyond injection-related placebo, local anesthetic effects, natural improvement, or co-interventions.

### Dexamethasone iontophoresis

NCT03606980 randomized children with knee apophysitis to:

* Dexamethasone iontophoresis plus physical therapy
* Saline iontophoresis plus physical therapy
* Physical therapy alone

The trial enrolled 45 participants rather than the planned 147 and was terminated because of slow enrollment. Results were posted to ClinicalTrials.gov in March 2024, but the small group sizes and early termination make the comparative estimates highly imprecise. It does not provide persuasive evidence that adding dexamethasone iontophoresis accelerates return to sport or improves patient-reported outcomes. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][13])

### Interpretation for OSD

No treatment has demonstrated clear superiority. An evidence-aligned starting package is:

**education + symptom-guided load modification + progressive lower-extremity strengthening + a graded return-to-sport plan.**

That is a clinical inference based on feasibility, favorable within-group outcomes, low invasiveness, and the lack of convincing evidence for injections—not a proven treatment ranking.

Dextrose injection and dexamethasone iontophoresis should not be characterized as established superior treatments. The two placebo-controlled injection trials point in different directions, and the iontophoresis trial was substantially under-recruited.

| Is physical therapy superior for Sever disease? | Not clearly; observation, heel raises, and supervised therapy had similar outcomes by three months.                                       |
