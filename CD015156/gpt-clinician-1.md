## Bottom line

The pediatric nonsurgical treatment evidence is concentrated in **calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease)** and **Osgood–Schlatter disease (OSD)**.

* **Calcaneal apophysitis:** Mechanical heel unloading has the clearest short-term evidence. A **heel cup or inexpensive heel raise** is supported more consistently than supervised therapy, footwear replacement, taping, or observation alone. One relatively large trial found substantially better 12-week pain outcomes with custom orthoses than with heel lifts, but this has not been independently replicated or shown to remain superior long term. ([PMC][1])
* **Osgood–Schlatter disease:** No nonsurgical treatment has convincingly proved superior. Pain-guided activity modification, progressive strengthening, and graded return to sport are reasonable first-line choices because they are low risk and have supportive prospective data, but a newer randomized trial found that a standardized self-management program was **not superior to usual care**. ([PubMed][2])
* **Dextrose injections for OSD:** Results conflict—one trial was strongly positive, while a later double-blind placebo-controlled trial found no advantage over saline. This does not support routine first-line injection. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][3])
* Comparative evidence is essentially absent for **Sinding–Larsen–Johansson disease, Iselin disease, and pelvic apophysitis**.

## Calcaneal apophysitis

### Heel cups and heel raises

The strongest specific short-term finding came from a randomized crossover study of 51 boys aged 9–14. A heel cup reduced the odds score for activity-related pain by approximately 80% compared with a heel wedge, and more than 75% preferred the heel cup. Participants maintained their activity levels. ([PubMed][4])

A separate randomized study of 38 boys found significantly less activity-related pain during periods when insoles were worn than during periods without insoles, without requiring cessation of sport. It did not establish that one insole design was superior. ([PubMed][5])

A mechanistic randomized study of 50 children also found that a heel cup increased functional heel-pad thickness, reduced peak heel pressure, and reduced pain compared with a sports shoe alone. ([PubMed][6])

### Heel raise versus prefabricated orthosis or footwear replacement

In a 124-participant factorial RCT, a heel raise produced a small advantage over a prefabricated orthosis in the physical domain of the Oxford Ankle Foot Questionnaire at one and two months. By six and twelve months, there were no differences between heel raises and prefabricated orthoses, or between replacing and not replacing footwear. ([PubMed][7])

This suggests that an inexpensive heel raise may be as reasonable as a more elaborate prefabricated device for most children, particularly when cost and convenience matter.

### Orthosis versus physical therapy versus observation

In a pragmatic RCT of 101 children, a heel raise, supervised eccentric exercise, and a wait-and-see approach all improved pain. Heel-raise recipients reported greater satisfaction and some better function at six weeks, but there were no clinically relevant between-group differences at three months. ([PubMed][8])

Thus, supervised physical therapy has not demonstrated superiority over a simple insert or symptom-guided observation for uncomplicated Sever disease.

### Custom orthoses

A 12-week RCT analyzed 208 children aged 9–12. Both custom polypropylene orthoses and off-the-shelf heel lifts improved symptoms, but custom orthoses produced substantially larger improvements in pain scores and pressure-pain thresholds. ([PMC][1])

This is the most favorable trial for custom orthoses, but important limitations are:

* Follow-up was only 12 weeks.
* Outcomes emphasized pain rather than sustained sport participation or long-term disability.
* The large effect has not been replicated in another independent pediatric trial.
* The comparison was with heel lifts rather than a no-treatment or well-standardized rehabilitation group.

Custom orthoses may therefore be considered for persistent symptoms, marked biomechanical contributors, or failure of a low-cost heel cup/raise, but they cannot yet be called the unequivocally best treatment.

### Taping and braces

A randomized study of only 22 male athletes found slightly better American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society scores with kinesiology taping than sham taping, but both groups reached very high scores and the small study does not establish an important clinical advantage. ([PubMed][9])

Among barefoot athletes, an RCT comparing the Tuli’s Cheetah heel cup with the X Brace found that both groups improved over three months, without a significant difference between braces. Only 32 of 43 enrolled athletes completed the trial. ([PMC][10])

### Interpretation for heel apophysitis

The best-supported practical sequence is:

1. Pain-guided modification of running and jumping, while avoiding unnecessary complete inactivity.
2. A low-cost heel cup or heel raise.
3. Calf flexibility and progressive lower-limb loading as tolerated.
4. Custom orthoses selectively when simpler unloading fails.

The evidence does not show that routine supervised physical therapy, footwear replacement, kinesiology tape, or a particular barefoot brace produces superior long-term outcomes.

## Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Activity modification and strengthening

A prospective uncontrolled cohort included 51 adolescents with longstanding OSD symptoms. Treatment consisted of education, a pain-monitoring/activity ladder, progressive knee strengthening, and graded return to sport. At 12 weeks:

* 80% reported improvement.
* Only 16% had returned to sport.
* At 12 months, improvement was reported by 90% and return to sport by 69%.
* Knee and hip strength and jump performance also improved.

Because there was no control group, improvement cannot confidently be separated from natural history, regression to the mean, or nonspecific clinical attention. ([PubMed][2])

A subsequent pragmatic RCT of 130 adolescents compared a standardized self-management program with usual care; both contained variations of exercise, education, and activity modification. At five months, the standardized program was not superior on KOOS-Child Sport/Play. Both groups improved by more than 20 points, although 36%–44% had not reached an acceptable symptom state. This is currently available as a peer-reviewed conference abstract rather than a full results paper. ([BMJ Open Seminars][11])

The combined interpretation is that **active load management is reasonable, but no particular exercise or self-management protocol has proved best**.

### Dextrose injections

In the 2011 Topol RCT, 54 adolescent athletes representing 65 knees received usual care, lidocaine injection, or lidocaine plus 12.5% dextrose monthly for three months. At three months:

* Unaltered sport was achieved by 21/21 dextrose-treated knees, 20/22 lidocaine-treated knees, and 13/22 usual-care knees.
* Asymptomatic sport occurred in 14/21 dextrose-treated knees, versus 5/22 lidocaine and 3/22 usual care.

The later open-label phase favored knees that eventually received dextrose, but the crossover to open treatment makes the one-year comparisons less secure. The analysis also treated knees rather than necessarily independent patients. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][3])

In contrast, a later double-blind RCT randomized 43 evaluable knees to dextrose plus lidocaine or saline plus lidocaine. Both groups improved, but there were no significant between-group differences at any measured time point. ([PubMed][12])

Given this direct conflict, injection discomfort, and the possibility of substantial placebo or needling effects, dextrose cannot presently be considered the best pediatric OSD treatment.

### Dexamethasone iontophoresis

A three-arm randomized, triple-masked trial compared physical therapy plus dexamethasone iontophoresis, physical therapy plus saline iontophoresis, and physical therapy alone. It was terminated because of slow enrollment after enrolling 45 participants rather than the planned 147. Sponsor-reported results were posted to ClinicalTrials.gov, but the small and incomplete trial does not provide a secure basis for recommending dexamethasone iontophoresis. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][13])

### Immobilization

A cohort study treated children with a four-week long-leg synthetic cast. Thirty participants were available for analysis; the reported median time to return to sport was approximately 11 weeks and return to the same level approximately 16 weeks. There was no non-cast control group, so it is impossible to know whether casting accelerated recovery. ([ScienceDirect][14])

Immobilization therefore has insufficient evidence to justify routine use and is better reserved for unusually painful, function-limiting cases in which simpler load modification is unsuccessful.

## Other apophysitides

For Sinding–Larsen–Johansson disease, I found case-level evidence but no comparative trial. One 13-year-old footballer treated by stopping sport recovered clinically and sonographically after five months, but a single case cannot establish efficacy or determine whether continued modified participation would have produced the same result. ([PubMed][15])

I did not locate eligible comparative pediatric treatment studies for Iselin disease or pelvic apophysitis. I also did not find trials isolating the effects of oral NSAIDs, ice, stretching alone, or patellar straps sufficiently to determine comparative effectiveness.

## Clinically useful conclusion

For an otherwise typical pediatric traction apophysitis, the evidence favors beginning with the **least burdensome load-management strategy**:

* For **Sever disease**, add a heel cup or heel raise; these have the clearest reproducible short-term treatment signal. Custom orthoses are an escalation option rather than a proven universal first choice.
* For **OSD**, use pain-guided activity modification, progressive quadriceps/hip strengthening, flexibility work where limited, and a criteria-based return to running and jumping. No particular protocol has proved superior.
* Routine dextrose injection, corticosteroid iontophoresis, casting, kinesiology tape, or expensive orthoses cannot currently be justified as consistently superior treatments.
