It can be incredibly tough watching your child deal with apophysitis. Because their growth plates (apophyses) are made of cartilage, they are softer than mature bone. During a growth spurt, tight tendons pull on these vulnerable attachment points, causing inflammation and pain that can sideline them from the sports they love.

When looking at the highest-quality primary literature (specifically Randomized Controlled Trials), the data for non-surgical treatments generally points to a reassuring conclusion: **simple, inexpensive interventions often work just as well as complex ones, and time is the ultimate cure.**

Here is what the primary clinical studies show for the two most common types of lower-limb apophysitis.

---

## Calcaneal Apophysitis (Sever’s Disease / Heel)

For growth-plate pain in the heel, studies consistently show that getting pressure off the heel and stretching the calf are the most effective strategies.

* **Heel Cups Provide Immediate Relief:** A crossover trial by Perhamre et al. (2011) evaluated two different types of insoles (heel cups vs. wedges) for boys with Sever's disease. The study found that firm heel cups significantly reduced pain during physical activity, allowing the children to continue their sports without needing to modify their activity levels or immobilize the foot.
* **Simple Options Equal Custom Options Long-Term:** A 12-month randomized trial by James et al. (2016) compared simple heel raises to prefabricated orthotics, as well as changing to new athletic footwear versus keeping current footwear. While the simple heel raises provided a slight advantage in pain relief at the 2-month mark, by 12 months, there was **no difference** between any of the groups. Furthermore, buying new athletic shoes provided no added benefit.
* **Wait-and-See vs. Physical Therapy:** Wiegerinck et al. (2016) ran a randomized trial comparing a "wait-and-see" approach, a heel raise inlay, and physical therapy (eccentric calf stretching). At the 3-month mark, all three approaches significantly reduced pain and improved function, with no single treatment proving statistically superior. However, the children using the heel raises reported higher satisfaction early in the recovery process.

**The Takeaway:** Start with inexpensive, over-the-counter heel cups or lifts and regular calf stretching before exploring expensive custom orthotics or new shoes.

## Tibial Tubercle Apophysitis (Osgood-Schlatter Disease / Knee)

Osgood-Schlatter disease occurs just below the kneecap. While it is usually treated with rest, ice, and time, some cases are "recalcitrant," meaning they stubbornly refuse to heal even after months of rest.

* **Dextrose Prolotherapy for Stubborn Cases:** Topol et al. (2011) conducted a double-blind, randomized controlled trial for adolescent athletes who had failed at least two months of conservative care. They injected the painful knee area with either dextrose prolotherapy (a sugar-water solution meant to stimulate the body's natural healing and collagen production) or lidocaine (a numbing agent), compared against a usual-care group.
* **The Results:** The dextrose injections safely and drastically outperformed the other groups. At the one-year follow-up, **84% of the kids treated with dextrose were playing sports completely pain-free**, compared to just 46% of the lidocaine group and only 14% of the usual-care group.

**The Takeaway:** If standard rest and physical therapy fail after several months, dextrose prolotherapy is a scientifically backed, non-surgical option worth discussing with your pediatric sports medicine physician.
