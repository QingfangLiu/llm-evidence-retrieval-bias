## Bottom line

There is **no single nonsurgical treatment that has been shown to be best for every lower-limb apophysitis**. The evidence depends heavily on location:

* **Heel—calcaneal apophysitis/Sever disease:** heel cups, heel lifts, orthoses, supervised exercise, and activity-guided “wait-and-see” care all help many children. A simple, inexpensive heel cup or lift is a reasonable initial device because more costly or elaborate treatments have not shown consistent long-term superiority.
* **Tibial tubercle—Osgood–Schlatter disease:** education, pain-guided activity modification, progressive strengthening, and graded return to sport are the best-supported low-risk approach, but no particular exercise program has proved superior to good usual care.
* **Fifth metatarsal—Iselin disease:** a recent nonrandomized study suggests that relative immobilization with early rehabilitation may return athletes to sport faster than strict rest.
* **Hip/pelvic apophysitis and Sinding–Larsen–Johansson disease:** evidence consists almost entirely of case reports and small case series. Treatment approaches cannot be reliably ranked.

I searched for clinical primary studies available through **July 25, 2026**, excluding reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions and editorials. I also excluded studies limited to acute apophyseal avulsion fractures, except one mixed iliac-crest case that separately reported contralateral apophysitis.

## 1. Heel: calcaneal apophysitis/Sever disease

### Most defensible conclusion: several conservative choices work, without a consistent overall winner

In a randomized trial of 101 children, a heel-raise insert, supervised eccentric exercise/physical therapy and a pragmatic wait-and-see strategy all produced clinically important improvement. The heel raise produced somewhat better early satisfaction and function, but there was **no clinically relevant difference among the three treatments at three months**. ([PubMed][1])

A separate 12-month factorial randomized trial of 124 children found a small short-term physical-function advantage for heel raises over prefabricated orthoses at one and two months. By six and twelve months, heel raise, prefabricated orthosis, footwear replacement and continued usual footwear produced no meaningful differences. ([PubMed][2])

There is conflicting device evidence:

* In a randomized crossover study of 51 boys, a molded heel cup reduced the odds of sport-related pain by about 80% compared with a heel wedge, and more than 75% preferred the heel cup. ([Wiley Online Library][3])
* A larger 12-week randomized trial of 208 children found greater pain improvement with custom-made orthoses than with off-the-shelf heel lifts. This supports custom orthoses as an option, but it conflicts with the longer trial in which prefabricated orthoses did not outperform heel raises. ([PMC][4])
* Two braces designed for barefoot sports produced similar improvement; neither brace was superior at three months. ([PubMed][5])

Kinesiology taping did not provide a meaningful pain advantage over sham tape in a small randomized trial of 22 male soccer players. There was a statistically better functional score with real tape, but the absolute difference was small and pain outcomes were similar. ([Springer][6])

**Interpretation:** for most children with Sever disease, the evidence supports selecting treatment according to comfort, cost and activities. A heel cup or heel lift is a sensible initial device. Supervised rehabilitation may be helpful when strength, flexibility or return-to-sport guidance is needed, but it has not proved superior to simpler care over several months.

## 2. Knee: Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Activity modification and strengthening

A prospective cohort of 51 adolescents used a 12-week program consisting of education, pain monitoring, an activity ladder, progressive hip and knee strengthening and graded return to sport. At 12 weeks, 80% reported improvement, increasing to 90% at one year. However, only 16% had returned to sport by 12 weeks and 69% by one year. Because there was no comparison group, natural improvement and other factors cannot be separated from treatment effects. ([PubMed][7])

The subsequent SOGOOD randomized trial enrolled 130 adolescents. A standardized self-management program was **not superior to usual care** at five months; both groups received varying combinations of education, exercise and activity modification and both improved by more than 20 points on the KOOS-Child sport/play scale. Only 56% and 64%, respectively, reached an acceptable symptom state, indicating that many children still had clinically important symptoms. These results are currently reported as a conference abstract rather than a complete trial publication. ([BMJ Open Seminars][8])

Thus, load management and progressive strengthening appear reasonable, but the available randomized evidence does not show that one standardized protocol is better than individualized usual care.

### Dextrose injections: inconsistent randomized evidence

Three randomized trials produced conflicting results:

* Topol et al. randomized 54 athletes/65 knees to dextrose plus lidocaine, lidocaine alone or supervised usual care. At three months, asymptomatic sport was achieved in 14/21 dextrose-treated knees, compared with 5/22 lidocaine-treated and 3/22 usual-care knees. The longer-term comparison is difficult to interpret because participants could receive dextrose after the initial three months. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][9])
* Nakase et al. studied 49 knees with persistent symptoms and found **no superiority of dextrose over saline injections** at any measured time point. Both groups improved. ([PubMed][10])
* Wu et al. randomized 70 participants to ultrasound-guided dextrose or saline and reported larger VISA-P improvements with dextrose at three, six and twelve months. ([PubMed][11])

Because one placebo-controlled trial was negative and the others differed in concentration, injection technique, comparator and outcomes, dextrose cannot currently be called the best treatment.

### Other modalities for persistent Osgood–Schlatter symptoms

Extracorporeal shock-wave therapy had promising long-term outcomes in a retrospective pilot involving only 14 adolescents and no control group. ([PubMed][12])

A small nonrandomized retrospective study of 40 adolescents found better 12-week outcomes with platelet-rich plasma than with three dextrose injections, including return to sport at approximately 6.1 versus 8.5 weeks. Allocation depended on availability and patient preference, making selection bias likely. ([PubMed][13])

A 2025 randomized Russian study of 90 adolescents reported better pain, motion and ultrasound outcomes when exercise was combined with calcium-phosphorus iontophoresis and interferential current. Reporting is limited, and independent replication is needed before treating this as established evidence. ([RCSI Journals Platform][14])

A 2026 within-person experiment involving 15 adolescents found that one 10-minute session of passive hip and knee movement temporarily reduced rectus-femoris stiffness; repeating the session produced no additional effect. It did not evaluate durable pain relief or return to sport. ([MDPI][15])

**Interpretation:** injections, shock-wave therapy and electrotherapy remain options with uncertain evidence, principally studied in persistent or refractory cases. They have not displaced load management and rehabilitation as the most defensible initial approach.

## 3. Foot: Iselin disease

A retrospective series of 27 athletes treated with combinations of rest, footwear modification, a walking boot or postoperative shoe reported healing in all cases. Mean treatment duration was 24 days and mean return to play was 38 days, but the study could not determine which component was responsible. ([PubMed][16])

A more informative 2026 prospective cohort compared 22 adolescents receiving removable bracing or strapping plus early physical therapy with 22 receiving strict rest. The combined program produced faster pain-free return to sport—**5.8 versus 8.3 weeks**—and lower pain at four weeks. Because treatment assignment was not randomized, this should be considered promising rather than definitive. ([ScienceDirect][17])

**Interpretation:** among the rarer apophysitides, this is one of the clearest signals favoring an active strategy—relative protection combined with early rehabilitation—over complete activity cessation.

## 4. Hip/pelvis and inferior patella

For greater-trochanter apophysitis, four young athletes treated with cessation of sport and physical therapy returned to sport in approximately 8–12 weeks; one later had a recurrence. This was an uncontrolled case series. ([PubMed][18])

A separate 15-year-old basketball player recovered after relative rest and anti-inflammatory medication, followed by gradual return to activity. ([PubMed][19])

A mixed 2025 iliac-crest report described a triathlete with an avulsion fracture on one side and apophysitis on the other who returned through a four-month staged running progression and remained pain-free at one year. It is only a single case and partly concerns an avulsion fracture rather than isolated apophysitis. ([PubMed][20])

For Sinding–Larsen–Johansson disease, a case report described complete clinical and ultrasound recovery in a 13-year-old soccer player after five months away from sport. There are no comparative treatment data in that report. ([PubMed][21])

## Overall treatment ranking from the primary evidence

1. **Pain-guided load modification with gradual reloading and strengthening:** most broadly applicable and low risk, particularly for Osgood–Schlatter disease, although no standardized program is clearly superior.
2. **Heel cup, heel lift or simple insert for Sever disease:** strongest device evidence; a heel cup may outperform a wedge, while long-term differences between common devices are small.
3. **Relative immobilization plus early rehabilitation for Iselin disease:** promising evidence for faster return to sport, but currently based on one nonrandomized comparative study.
4. **Custom foot orthoses for Sever disease:** may provide greater short-term pain relief than an off-the-shelf lift in selected children, but universal superiority and cost-effectiveness are unproven.
5. **Injections, shock-wave treatment, iontophoresis and PRP for Osgood–Schlatter disease:** evidence is conflicting or observational; none can presently be identified as the best routine treatment.

These findings apply to clinically diagnosed overuse apophysitis. A sudden pop, acute traumatic onset or displaced fragment represents a different clinical question—an apophyseal avulsion injury—and should not be managed by simply extrapolating these studies.
