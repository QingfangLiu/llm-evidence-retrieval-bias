Yes, quit-smoking programs initiated during a patient's stay in a hospital psychiatry ward are effective at helping adults with mental illness achieve smoking abstinence.

Historically, there has been a lingering assumption in psychiatric care that attempting to quit smoking could disrupt a patient's mental health recovery or that patients with severe mental illness are not motivated to quit. However, primary clinical trials demonstrate that capitalizing on the enforced abstinence of a smoke-free psychiatric unit is a highly effective "teachable moment" that does not harm—and may even protect—long-term mental health.

Here is what the primary trial data shows:

### Efficacy of Inpatient-Initiated Programs

Initiating multicomponent smoking cessation treatments while a patient is admitted significantly increases long-term quit rates compared to usual care.

* **Long-Term Abstinence:** A randomized controlled trial in a locked, acute psychiatric unit evaluated an intervention combining motivational counseling with nicotine replacement therapy (NRT). At 18 months post-hospitalization, 20.0% of the patients in the intervention group successfully maintained abstinence, compared to only 7.7% of those receiving usual care (Prochaska et al., 2014).
* **Sustained Care Models:** The "Helping HAND 3" randomized clinical trial found that an inpatient-initiated "sustained care" model—which included inpatient motivational counseling, free transdermal nicotine patches upon discharge, and proactive follow-up telephone counseling—resulted in significantly greater treatment engagement and successful abstinence at 6 months post-discharge compared to standard brief advice (Brown et al., 2021).
* **Group Behavioral Interventions:** Even without the provision of NRT, structured behavioral interventions can be highly effective. A controlled clinical trial evaluating a 24-week group counseling program tailored for patients with mental illness found significantly higher verified abstinence rates at 12 weeks (15.2% vs. 0%) and 24 weeks (9.1% vs. 0%) compared to a control group (Olando et al., 2022).

### Impact on Psychiatric Recovery

Treating tobacco dependence does not destabilize psychiatric patients.

* **Rehospitalization Rates:** The trial by Prochaska et al. (2014) found that patients who received the cessation intervention actually had a *significantly lower* likelihood of being rehospitalized for psychiatric reasons (44%) compared to the usual care group (56%).
* **Symptom Severity as a Factor:** Furthermore, the severity of the patient's psychiatric symptoms upon admission did not diminish the efficacy of the smoking cessation treatment. Instead, standard predictors of quitting—such as the patient's level of nicotine dependence and their personal motivation—were the true drivers of success (Prochaska et al., 2014).
