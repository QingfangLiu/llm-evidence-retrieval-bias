## Bottom line

**Yes—some quit-smoking programmes started during psychiatric hospitalization help, particularly when they combine counseling with nicotine-replacement therapy and continue providing treatment after discharge.** The individual trials are not uniformly positive, however, and lasting abstinence remains difficult.

Among the more informative trials:

* Prochaska and colleagues found higher verified abstinence with motivational treatment plus nicotine replacement at every follow-up through 18 months: **20.0% versus 7.7% at 18 months**. ([PMC][1])
* Metse and colleagues found higher 7-day abstinence at six months—**15.8% versus 9.3%**—but the difference was no longer statistically significant at 12 months, and prolonged abstinence was not improved. ([Sage Journals][2])
* The Helping HAND 3 trial found higher biochemically verified abstinence at six months—**8.9% versus 3.5%**, adjusted odds ratio 2.95—and much greater use of cessation treatment after discharge. ([PMC][3])
* The Stockings trial found no significant abstinence difference at six months, although there was a benefit at four months and improvements in quit attempts, cigarette consumption and nicotine dependence. ([PubMed][4])
* The smaller Hickman feasibility trial had numerically higher abstinence with the intervention at 3, 6 and 12 months, but the overall treatment difference was not statistically significant. ([PMC][5])

Taken together, the distinguishing feature of the more successful programmes was **continuity**: treatment began on the ward but did not end at discharge. Programmes commonly included motivational counseling, nicotine patches or another nicotine-replacement product, and follow-up through telephone calls, text messages, quitlines, counseling or continued medication. ([PMC][1])

### Other types of inpatient intervention

Evidence for medication-only or smoking-reduction approaches is much less certain:

* A five-session behavioral programme reduced cigarette consumption among 53 long-stay inpatients with schizophrenia, but it was designed and measured as a **reduction** intervention rather than a cessation trial. ([PubMed][6])
* A 32-person bupropion trial reported more short-term abstinence with bupropion than placebo after eight weeks, but did not provide usable abstinence counts in its abstract and had no long-term follow-up. ([Minerva Medica][7])
* A 184-person nicotine-patch trial found only **5 participants—2.7%—abstinent** and no evidence that a higher patch dose was better than a lower dose. ([NYMU Academic Hub][8])
* A 310-person cluster trial comparing nicotine inhalers, gum and patches primarily measured carbon-monoxide reduction, withdrawal and psychiatric symptoms—not sustained quitting. The inhaler had the clearest within-group reduction in carbon monoxide. ([ResearchGate][9])
* A 47-person open-label comparison found six-month abstinence in **3 of 17** cytisine recipients and **2 of 30** nicotine-gum recipients. With only five total quitters, this is too small to establish that cytisine is superior. ([Taylor & Francis Online][10])

Thus, the evidence supports asking whether your hospital can provide **both treatment during admission and a concrete continuation plan after discharge**. The studies do not suggest that hospitalization alone, brief advice alone or short-term nicotine replacement without continuing support reliably produces lasting abstinence.
