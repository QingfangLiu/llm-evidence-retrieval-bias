## Summary

Several randomized controlled trials have tested smoking cessation programmes started on adult psychiatric inpatient wards, generally combining brief motivational/behavioral counseling in hospital with pharmacotherapy (usually nicotine replacement therapy) and continued support after discharge. Findings are mixed but generally favorable in the short-to-medium term:

- A randomized trial of tobacco dependence treatment initiated in inpatient psychiatry found verified smoking abstinence was significantly higher for the intervention group than usual care at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months post-discharge.
- Another Australian RCT allocated 205 psychiatric inpatient smokers to usual care versus a postdischarge intervention with psychosocial and pharmacological support, and found point prevalence abstinence significantly higher for the intervention group at 4 months, though this difference did not persist at 6 months.
- A larger four-site Australian RCT (N=754) tested a brief motivational interview and self-help material in hospital followed by a 4-month post-discharge pharmacological/psychosocial intervention, finding higher 7-day abstinence in the intervention group at 6 months but not at 12 months, though secondary quitting behaviors (reduced cigarettes/day, quit attempts) favored the intervention at both timepoints.
- A cost-effectiveness analysis of a stage-based smoking cessation intervention for psychiatric inpatients (223 smokers, randomized 2006–2008) examined costs alongside biochemically verified abstinence over 18 months, drawing on the same trial as the AJPH paper above.
- A pooled analysis of two inpatient psychiatry RCTs focused on the subsample with co-occurring substance use disorders (n=216), evaluating a computer-assisted intervention tailored to readiness to quit, initiated during hospitalization and continued 6 months post-discharge.
