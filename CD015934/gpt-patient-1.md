## Bottom line

**Yes—quit-smoking treatment started during psychiatric hospitalization can help, especially when it continues after discharge.** The clearest evidence comes from programs combining inpatient counseling or motivational support, nicotine-replacement therapy, and proactive follow-up after discharge.

Three randomized trials found statistically significant improvements in **7-day abstinence at six months**:

* **14.4% versus 6.5%** in Prochaska et al.—about 8 additional people abstinent per 100 treated.
* **15.8% versus 9.3%** in Metse et al.—about 7 additional per 100.
* **8.9% versus 3.5%** in Brown et al.—about 5 additional per 100. ([PMC][1])

“Seven-day abstinence” means participants reported no smoking during the preceding seven days at the follow-up assessment, usually with biochemical verification. It does **not necessarily mean uninterrupted abstinence** since leaving hospital.

The longer-term findings were mixed. Prochaska et al. still found an advantage at 18 months—20.0% versus 7.7%—whereas the advantage in Metse et al. was no longer statistically significant at 12 months. Stockings et al. found an advantage at four months but not at six months. A smaller feasibility study found numerically more abstinence with treatment, but it was not sufficiently powered to establish a reliable treatment effect. ([PMC][1])

### What appears to matter

The more successful interventions did not rely solely on the hospital being smoke-free. They generally:

1. Began counseling and nicotine treatment before discharge.
2. Provided medication or nicotine replacement after discharge.
3. Used repeated telephone calls, texts, quitline contact, or other continuing support.

This suggests that **connecting inpatient treatment with several months of post-discharge care** is more promising than a short ward-only intervention. Even with the stronger programs, however, most participants were still smoking at follow-up. ([PMC][1])

### Evidence for particular medications or nicotine products

The evidence here is much less certain:

* A 32-person placebo-controlled bupropion trial reported greater short-term abstinence or smoking reduction, but its small sample and eight-week follow-up prevent firm conclusions. ([Minerva Medica][2])
* In a 47-person open-label trial, 3 of 17 cytisine recipients and 2 of 30 nicotine-gum recipients were abstinent at six months. The numbers are too small to establish superiority. ([Taylor & Francis Online][3])
* A trial of high- versus low-dose nicotine patches in long-term hospitalized people with schizophrenia found only five abstinent participants among 184 overall, without a clear dose-related benefit. ([NYMU Academic Hub][4])
* A cluster-randomized comparison of nicotine inhalers, gum, and patches primarily demonstrated reductions in carbon monoxide, withdrawal, and psychiatric symptoms rather than convincing evidence of sustained cessation. ([OUCI][5])
* A five-session behavioral program reduced cigarette consumption among people with chronic schizophrenia, but did not establish sustained abstinence. ([PubMed][6])

These findings support asking the inpatient team for a plan that starts now and continues after discharge—for example, medication access plus a specific follow-up service—rather than assuming that temporary abstinence on a smoke-free ward will by itself result in long-term quitting.

## Individual primary studies

1. **Prochaska JJ, Hall SE, Delucchi K, Hall SM.** Efficacy of initiating tobacco dependence treatment in inpatient psychiatry: a randomized controlled trial. *American Journal of Public Health.* 2014;104(8):1557–1565. doi:10.2105/AJPH.2013.301403.
   Randomized 224 psychiatric inpatients to a motivational, computer-assisted intervention with nicotine replacement or usual care. Verified abstinence was higher with treatment at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months. ([PMC][1])

2. **Hickman NJ III, Delucchi KL, Prochaska JJ.** Treating tobacco dependence at the intersection of diversity, poverty, and mental illness: a randomized feasibility and replication trial. *Nicotine & Tobacco Research.* 2015;17(8):1012–1021. doi:10.1093/ntr/ntv034.
   Randomized 100 psychiatric inpatients. Abstinence was numerically higher with tailored counseling and post-discharge nicotine replacement, but the overall treatment effect was not statistically significant. ([PMC][7])

3. **Brown RA, et al.** Sustained care smoking cessation intervention for individuals hospitalized for psychiatric disorders: the Helping HAND 3 randomized clinical trial. *JAMA Psychiatry.* 2021;78(8):839–847. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.0707.
   Randomized 353 participants to sustained cessation care or usual care. Verified six-month abstinence was 8.9% versus 3.5%. ([PubMed][8])

4. **Stockings EA, et al.** Impact of a postdischarge smoking cessation intervention for smokers admitted to an inpatient psychiatric facility: a randomized controlled trial. *Nicotine & Tobacco Research.* 2014;16(11):1417–1428. doi:10.1093/ntr/ntu097.
   Randomized 205 inpatients to four months of pharmacological and psychosocial support or usual care. Abstinence was higher at four months but not at six months; smoking and dependence measures nevertheless improved. ([PubMed][9])

5. **Metse AP, et al.** Efficacy of a universal smoking cessation intervention initiated in inpatient psychiatry and continued post-discharge: a randomised controlled trial. *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry.* 2017;51(4):366–381. doi:10.1177/0004867417692424.
   Randomized 754 smokers across four psychiatric facilities. Six-month abstinence was 15.8% with intervention versus 9.3% with usual care; the 12-month difference was not statistically significant. ([Sage Journals][10])

6. **Gelkopf M, et al.** Nonmedication smoking reduction program for inpatients with chronic schizophrenia: a randomized control design study. *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease.* 2012;200(2):142–146. doi:10.1097/NMD.0b013e3182438e92.
   Randomized 53 long-term inpatients to a five-session behavioral group or a waiting-list condition. Cigarette consumption decreased, but sustained cessation was not established. ([PubMed][6])

7. **Chen HK, Lan TH, Wu BJ.** A double-blind randomized clinical trial of different doses of transdermal nicotine patch for smoking reduction and cessation in long-term hospitalized schizophrenic patients. *European Archives of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience.* 2013;263(1):75–82. doi:10.1007/s00406-012-0338-3.
   Compared two nicotine-patch doses in 184 long-term hospitalized participants. Only 2.7% were abstinent, with no convincing dose-related cessation advantage. ([NYMU Academic Hub][4])

8. **Chan HY, Chen JJ, Pan YJ, Hsu CC.** Nicotine replacement therapy and healthy lifestyle psychoeducation for smoking reduction in acute psychiatric inpatients: a cluster-randomized parallel study. *Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology.* 2020;40(2):149–156. doi:10.1097/JCP.0000000000001170.
   Cluster-randomized 310 inpatients to nicotine inhaler, gum, or patch interventions. The principal findings concerned smoking reduction, carbon monoxide levels, and withdrawal rather than sustained abstinence. ([OUCI][5])

9. **Akbarpour F, Rezaei O, Khodaie-Ardakani MR, Sheikhvatan M, Goodarzi H, Dolatshahi B.** A double-blind placebo-controlled trial of bupropion for smoking abstinence and cognition improvement in schizophrenia. *Minerva Psichiatrica.* 2010;51(4):263–269.
   Randomized 32 hospitalized smokers with schizophrenia to bupropion or placebo for eight weeks. The authors reported greater abstinence or reduction with bupropion, but the trial was very small and short. ([Minerva Medica][2])

10. **Tavakoli-Ardakani M, Gholamzadeh Sani Z, Beyraghi N, Najarimoghadam S, Kheradmand A.** Comparison between cytisine and nicotine replacement therapy in smoking cessation among inpatient psychiatric patients. *Journal of Addictive Diseases.* 2024;42(4):352–359. doi:10.1080/10550887.2023.2237395.
    Open-label randomized trial of 47 psychiatric inpatients. At six months, 3 of 17 cytisine recipients and 2 of 30 nicotine-gum recipients reported complete cessation; the study was too small for a dependable comparison. ([Taylor & Francis Online][3])
