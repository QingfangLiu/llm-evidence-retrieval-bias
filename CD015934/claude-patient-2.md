Here's what I found. These are all individual primary studies (RCTs or comparative cohort studies) testing smoking-cessation programs that were started while patients were on a psychiatric ward, in adults with mental illness — not reviews or guidelines.

**What the evidence generally shows:** Programs that start counselling/motivational support (and often nicotine replacement therapy) during the psychiatric admission and then continue support after discharge tend to increase quit rates compared with usual care, though effects often fade somewhat over longer follow-up and post-discharge support seems important for sustaining gains.

**Key findings from individual trials:**

- A randomized trial in a locked acute psychiatry unit with a 100% smoking ban compared motivational tobacco treatment plus nicotine replacement against usual care in 224 patients. Verified abstinence was significantly higher in the intervention group at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months, and the usual-care group had significantly higher odds of psychiatric rehospitalization.

- A four-site Australian RCT (N=754) tested a brief motivational interview and self-help material in hospital followed by 4 months of pharmacological and psychosocial support after discharge, finding it increased 7-day point-prevalence quitting and related quitting behaviors at 6 months, with effects on quitting behavior sustained at 12 months.

- Another Australian RCT (205 patients) tested post-discharge cessation support added to inpatient care, finding it effective for encouraging quit attempts and reducing cigarette consumption up to 6 months post-discharge, though longer-term cessation still needed more support.

- A comparative (non-randomized) study of an inpatient smoking cessation counselor found that patients seen by the counselor had significantly higher point-prevalence abstinence (16% vs 10%) and self-reported quit status (44% vs 30%) at 6 months post-discharge than those not seen by the counselor.

- A pooled analysis of two inpatient-psychiatry-initiated RCTs (combined n=224 and n=100) focused specifically on the subgroup with co-occurring substance use disorders, examining smoking cessation and recovery outcomes.

- A companion cost-effectiveness analysis of the Prochaska trial recruited adult inpatients from a smoke-free psychiatric unit, randomizing them to standard care (pamphlet + brief advice) versus a stage-tailored cessation intervention, with all participants receiving NRT during hospitalization.

- A pilot trial of a digital smoking-cessation program bridging hospital discharge enrolled 58 of 218 eligible psychiatric inpatients, examining feasibility, engagement, and abstinence at 1 and 3 months post-discharge.
