Primary clinical studies consistently demonstrate that initiating smoking cessation programs within hospital psychiatry wards is both effective and safe. Leveraging the enforced abstinence of a smoke-free locked unit as a "teachable moment" leads to significantly higher long-term quit rates compared to usual care.

## Key Findings from Primary Trials

* **Improved Long-Term Abstinence:** Multicomponent interventions—typically combining inpatient motivational counseling, nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), and post-discharge support—significantly outperform standard care. A randomized clinical trial of a sustained-care intervention more than doubled the 6-month abstinence rates (8.9% vs. 3.5%) compared to usual care (Brown et al., 2021). Similarly, another trial demonstrated that motivationally tailored treatment initiated during acute hospitalization achieved a 20% abstinence rate at 18 months, compared to just 7.7% for the control group (Prochaska et al., 2014).
* **No Harm to Psychiatric Recovery:** A historical barrier to inpatient cessation programs is the clinical concern that quitting smoking might worsen psychiatric symptoms or increase ward agitation. Evidence directly refutes this. Initiating tobacco dependence treatment during acute psychiatric hospitalization does not negatively impact psychiatric severity and has actually been associated with a significantly *decreased* risk of psychiatric rehospitalization (Prochaska et al., 2014). Furthermore, implementing evidence-based cessation pathways on mental health wards does not increase violent or aggressive incidents (Lappin et al., 2020).
* **Increased Post-Discharge Engagement:** Starting the program on the ward dramatically improves long-term follow-through. Patients who receive structured inpatient cessation counseling and transitional NRT are significantly more likely to continue using smoking cessation treatments (such as quitlines or outpatient medications) in the months following their discharge (Brown et al., 2021; Lappin et al., 2020).

## Summary for Clinical Practice

The primary evidence indicates that psychiatric hospitalization provides a critical, highly effective window to initiate nicotine dependence treatment. Structured interventions not only support immediate withdrawal management but fundamentally improve long-term cessation outcomes without compromising mental health recovery.
