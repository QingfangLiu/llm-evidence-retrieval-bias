## Bottom line

**Yes—but the benefit is clearest when hospitalization is used to begin a multicomponent cessation programme that continues actively after discharge.** The most informative randomized trials combined inpatient motivational/behavioral treatment with postdischarge pharmacotherapy and proactive follow-up. These programmes generally increased point-prevalence abstinence, quit attempts, or smoking reduction, although absolute quit rates remained modest and some effects diminished by 12 months.

## Interpretation for practice

The direct evidence suggests that a useful ward programme includes more than managing nicotine withdrawal during the admission. The recurring effective components were:

* motivational or cessation-focused counseling while hospitalized;
* cessation medication supplied or arranged at discharge;
* continued medication for approximately 10 weeks to several months;
* proactive calls, texts, quitline linkage, or counseling after discharge.

This conclusion is reinforced by a prospective study of 100 patients in a smoke-free psychiatric unit: although 70% received NRT for withdrawal, counseling was rare and **every participant resumed smoking within five weeks of discharge**. ([Wiley Online Library][8])

Taken together, I would characterize the evidence as:

* **Moderately supportive for increasing short- to medium-term point-prevalence abstinence**, particularly around 4–6 months.
* **Less consistent for prolonged abstinence or sustained effects at 12 months and beyond.**
* **Insufficient to show that simply making the unit smoke-free or providing inpatient-only NRT produces lasting cessation.**
* Suggestive that a relatively brief but well-connected postdischarge intervention may be as useful as a much more intensive programme—the 956-participant study did not find added benefit from extending counseling and NRT beyond the briefer treatment. ([Paperzz][4])

## Smaller or more narrowly focused inpatient studies

Several smaller studies addressed specific medications or smoking reduction rather than a comprehensive discharge programme:

* **Gelkopf 2012:** 53 long-term hospitalized adults with schizophrenia were randomized to a five-session behavioral programme or waiting list. Cigarette consumption decreased relative to control, but abstinence was not established as the principal benefit. ([PubMed][9])
* **Chen 2013:** 184 long-term hospitalized adults with schizophrenia received higher- or lower-dose nicotine patches. Only **5 participants, 2.7%,** achieved 7-day abstinence, and the higher dose was not superior. ([NYMU Academic Hub][10])
* **Akbarpour 2010:** 32 hospitalized adults with schizophrenia were randomized to bupropion or placebo for eight weeks. The article reports greater end-of-treatment smoking discontinuation with bupropion, but its accessible report does not provide usable group-specific abstinence counts and describes the smoking outcome imprecisely. ([Minerva Medica][11])
* **Tavakoli-Ardakani/Kheradmand 2024:** 47 motivated psychiatric inpatients were randomized open-label to cytisine or nicotine gum. Six-month complete abstinence was **3/17 with cytisine versus 2/30 with gum**. The trial was too small and unblinded for a reliable comparative-effect estimate. ([Taylor & Francis Online][12])
* **Chou 2004:** 68 adults with schizophrenia were assigned to an eight-week nicotine-patch programme or control. The patch group had 26.9% point-prevalence and 23.1% continuous abstinence at three months, but the indexed report does not clearly describe randomization or provide sufficiently detailed comparative abstinence data. ([PubMed][13])
