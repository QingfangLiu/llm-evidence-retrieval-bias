## Bottom line

**Yes, some quit-smoking programs started during an adult psychiatric hospitalization help—but the most effective model does not end at discharge.** The better-supported programs combined:

1. motivational or behavioral counseling on the ward;
2. nicotine-replacement therapy, often including medication supplied at discharge; and
3. proactive counseling, texts, calls, or other support for several months afterward.

The improvement was generally modest but clinically meaningful: in several trials, about **5–12 additional people per 100** were abstinent at follow-up compared with usual care. Results were not uniformly sustained, however. Some studies found a benefit at four or six months that was no longer statistically clear by 12 months. ([PMC][1])

“Seven-day point-prevalence abstinence” means that the participant reported no smoking during the previous seven days at the assessment. It is less stringent than continuous or prolonged abstinence.

## What the individual inpatient studies found

### Programs combining counseling, nicotine treatment, and postdischarge support

**Prochaska et al., 2014 — 224 acute psychiatric inpatients**

A motivationally tailored intervention plus access to nicotine replacement was compared with usual care. Verified seven-day abstinence was:

* 14.4% versus 6.5% at six months;
* 19.4% versus 10.9% at 12 months; and
* 20.0% versus 7.7% at 18 months.

This is the clearest trial showing a sustained benefit. Only 16% initially intended to quit within 30 days, suggesting treatment could engage patients who were not already committed to quitting. ([PMC][1])

**Stockings et al., 2014 — 205 psychiatric inpatients**

The intervention began during hospitalization and provided psychosocial and pharmacological support for four months after discharge. Abstinence was higher at four months—11.5% versus 2.0%—but the difference was no longer significant at six months. Intervention participants nevertheless made more quit attempts, smoked fewer cigarettes, and had lower nicotine-dependence scores. ([PubMed][2])

**Hickman et al., 2015 — 100 psychiatric inpatients**

Usual inpatient nicotine replacement and quit advice were compared with computer-tailored motivational treatment, brief counseling, written materials, and ten weeks of posthospital nicotine replacement. Seven-day abstinence was:

* 17.5% versus 8.5% at six months;
* 26.2% versus 16.7% at 12 months.

This was a feasibility trial and was too small to establish a definitive between-group effect, but the direction favored the enhanced intervention. ([PubMed][3])

**Metse et al., 2017 — 754 psychiatric inpatients at four Australian facilities**

Participants received an inpatient motivational interview and self-help materials, followed by four months of pharmacological and psychosocial support. Seven-day abstinence was higher at six months—15.8% versus 9.3%—but not clearly different at 12 months—13.4% versus 10.0%. Prolonged abstinence did not differ, although intervention participants were more likely to attempt quitting and reduce consumption. ([Sage Journals][4])

**Brown et al., 2021, Helping HAND 3 — 353 randomized inpatients**

Sustained care included inpatient motivational counseling, nicotine patches at discharge, access to quitline/text/web counseling, and automated follow-up messages. Biochemically verified seven-day abstinence at six months was 8.9% versus 3.5%, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.95. Use of any cessation treatment after discharge was also much higher—74.6% versus 40.5%. ([JAMA Network][5])

**Bennett et al., 2026 — 58 psychiatric inpatients**

This small pilot compared a digital cessation program introduced in the hospital with brief counseling. At one month, six digital-program participants versus no control participants reported seven-day abstinence. The study was small, had short follow-up, and was intended primarily to test feasibility, so it is promising rather than definitive. ([Psychiatry Online][6])

### Medication or smoking-reduction studies

**Akbarpour et al., 2010 — 32 hospitalized men with schizophrenia**

Participants received bupropion or placebo for eight weeks. The authors reported greater smoking cessation in the bupropion group at the end of treatment, but the accessible primary report does not provide sufficiently clear abstinence counts to judge the size of the effect. The trial was very small and short. ([Minerva Medica][7])

**Gelkopf et al., 2012 — 53 long-term inpatients with schizophrenia**

A five-session behavioral group program reduced the number of cigarettes smoked at three months compared with a waiting list. It did **not establish smoking abstinence**, so it supports smoking reduction rather than quitting. No worsening of psychiatric symptoms or weight gain was observed. ([PubMed][8])

**Chen et al., 2013 — 184 long-term hospitalized patients with schizophrenia**

High-dose and lower-dose nicotine patches were compared, with both groups also receiving brief cessation education. Only five participants—2.7%—achieved seven-day abstinence. Higher-dose patches did not improve cessation or overall reduction compared with the lower dose. ([NYMU Academic Hub][9])

**Tavakoli-Ardakani et al., 2024 — 47 psychiatric inpatients**

This open-label randomized trial compared nicotine gum with cytisine. At six months, complete abstinence was reported in 2 of 30 nicotine-gum participants and 3 of 17 cytisine participants—6.7% versus 17.6%. These numbers are far too small to establish that cytisine is superior. ([Taylor & Francis Online][10])

### Borderline hospital setting

**Chou et al., 2004 — 68 patients with schizophrenia**

Nicotine-patch treatment produced 26.9% point-prevalence abstinence and 23.1% continuous abstinence at three months, compared with no abstinence in the control group. However, participants were recruited from a **psychiatric day-care ward rather than an overnight inpatient ward**, so I would treat this as indirect rather than strictly inpatient evidence. ([PubMed][11])

## Interpretation for someone currently on a psychiatry ward

The evidence most relevant to you supports asking the clinical team for a **combined plan**, rather than relying on temporary smoke-free hospitalization alone:

* treatment of withdrawal during admission, usually with nicotine replacement;
* brief motivational or behavioral counseling before discharge;
* enough cessation medication to continue afterward; and
* scheduled follow-up through counseling, a quitline, text support, outpatient mental-health care, or a tobacco-treatment service.

Medication selection should be coordinated with your psychiatrist, nurse practitioner/physician assistant, and pharmacist; do not alter psychiatric medication or start a cessation medicine independently.
