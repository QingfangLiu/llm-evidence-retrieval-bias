## Evidence scope

I focused on pivotal or clinically informative **primary randomized trials in adults with moderate-to-severe Crohn’s disease**, plus a small number of primary prospective safety studies. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions, and editorials.

Older studies usually defined clinical remission as **CDAI <150**. Newer trials increasingly include patient-reported symptom remission and objective endoscopic outcomes. Maintenance designs vary substantially: many re-randomized only induction responders, whereas several newer IL-23 trials used treat-through designs. Absolute percentages therefore should not be compared directly across trials.

## Main findings

### 1. Anti-TNF therapy

**Infliximab**

In the original induction trial, a single infliximab infusion produced clinical remission at week 4 in approximately 33% of patients versus 4% with placebo. Response was evident within several weeks. ([PubMed][1])

Among initial responders in ACCENT I, scheduled maintenance resulted in remission at week 30 in 39% with infliximab 5 mg/kg every eight weeks and 45% with the 10-mg/kg strategy, versus 21% with episodic/placebo maintenance. Median time to loss of response was substantially longer with scheduled treatment, while serious-infection rates were similar between groups during the trial. ([PubMed][2])

For fistulizing disease, induction infliximab produced at least a 50% reduction in draining fistulas in 68% at 5 mg/kg versus 26% with placebo; complete fistula closure occurred in 55% versus 13%. In ACCENT II responders, maintenance prolonged time to loss of fistula response and increased complete closure at week 54—36% versus 19%. ([PubMed][3])

**Adalimumab**

In anti-TNF–naive patients in CLASSIC-I, the 160/80-mg induction regimen produced week-4 remission in 36% versus 12% with placebo; injection-site reactions were more frequent with adalimumab. ([PubMed][4])

GAIN demonstrated induction efficacy after intolerance or loss of response to infliximab: week-4 remission was approximately 21% with adalimumab versus 7% with placebo. ([PubMed][5])

In CHARM induction responders, remission at week 56 occurred in 36% with every-other-week and 41% with weekly adalimumab versus 12% with placebo. More placebo recipients discontinued because of adverse events, often reflecting worsening Crohn’s disease. CLASSIC-II also favored continued treatment, although its randomized cohort was small: 79%–83% versus 44% remained in remission at week 56. ([PubMed][6])

**Certolizumab pegol**

PRECiSE 1 found a modest improvement in sustained clinical response but did not establish a significant remission benefit in the overall population. Serious infections occurred in approximately 2% with certolizumab versus less than 1% with placebo. ([PubMed][7])

Among induction responders in PRECiSE 2, week-26 remission was 48% with continued certolizumab versus 29% after withdrawal. Serious infection was reported in about 3% versus less than 1%, including one tuberculosis case. ([PubMed][8])

**Combination therapy**

SONIC enrolled patients naive to immunomodulators and biologics. Corticosteroid-free remission at week 26 was:

* Infliximab plus azathioprine: **56.8%**
* Infliximab alone: **44.4%**
* Azathioprine alone: **30.0%**

Mucosal healing was also most frequent with combination therapy. Serious infections occurred in 3.9%, 4.9%, and 5.6%, respectively, during the trial, but SONIC was not large or long enough to determine rare malignancy risks. ([PubMed][9])

### 2. Integrin-directed therapy

**Vedolizumab**

In GEMINI 2, week-6 clinical remission was 14.5% with vedolizumab versus 6.8% with placebo, although the CDAI-100 response endpoint was not significantly different. Among induction responders, week-52 remission was 39.0% with every-eight-week and 36.4% with every-four-week vedolizumab versus 21.6% with placebo. Serious adverse events and serious infections were numerically more frequent in vedolizumab-treated patients in this trial, although many events were related to active Crohn’s disease. ([PubMed][10])

In GEMINI 3, which concentrated on previous anti-TNF failure, the prespecified week-6 remission endpoint was not significantly different from placebo. A greater separation appeared by week 10, supporting a potentially slower onset in treatment-experienced patients. ([PubMed][11])

**Natalizumab**

ENACT-1 did not clearly meet its induction remission endpoint in an unselected population, although ENACT-2 showed benefit among initial responders: sustained response through week 36 was 61% versus 28%, and remission was 44% versus 26%. ENCORE subsequently demonstrated induction benefit in patients with objective inflammation. ([PubMed][12])

A primary case report documented progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy in a patient with Crohn’s disease exposed to natalizumab, establishing a serious mechanism-specific harm that profoundly changes its benefit–harm balance. ([PubMed][13])

### 3. IL-12/23 and selective IL-23 inhibition

**Ustekinumab**

In UNITI-1, involving anti-TNF failures, week-6 response was approximately 34% with ustekinumab versus 21.5% with placebo. In UNITI-2, involving conventional-treatment failures, response was approximately 52%–56% versus 28.7%. Among responders in IM-UNITI, week-44 remission was 53.1% with every-eight-week and 48.8% with every-12-week ustekinumab versus 35.9% after withdrawal. Adverse-event rates were similar across groups. ([PubMed][14])

Five-year follow-up found broadly similar exposure-adjusted rates of serious adverse events, infections, and serious infections between ustekinumab and the original placebo group, although long-term extension attrition and crossover limit causal comparisons. ([PubMed][15])

**Risankizumab**

In ADVANCE, week-12 CDAI remission was 45% with risankizumab 600 mg versus 25% with placebo, while endoscopic response was 40% versus 12%. In the biologic-failure MOTIVATE population, remission was 42% versus 20% and endoscopic response 29% versus 11%. Overall adverse-event rates were similar. ([PubMed][16])

Among induction responders in FORTIFY, week-52 remission was approximately 52%–55% with continued risankizumab versus 41% after withdrawal; endoscopic response was approximately 47% versus 22%. Adverse-event rates were similar among groups. ([PubMed][17])

In the head-to-head SEQUENCE trial after anti-TNF failure, risankizumab was noninferior to ustekinumab for week-24 clinical remission—58.6% versus 39.5%—and superior for week-48 endoscopic remission—31.8% versus 16.2%. Adverse-event incidence appeared similar. ([PubMed][18])

**Guselkumab**

In the fully subcutaneous GRAVITI trial, week-12 clinical remission was 56.1% with guselkumab versus 21.4% with placebo, and endoscopic response was 41.3% versus 21.4%. At week 48, remission was 60.0%–66.1% versus 17.1%, and endoscopic response was 44.3%–51.3% versus 6.8%. Adverse-event rates were not greater with guselkumab. ([ScienceDirect][19])

GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3 also demonstrated benefit with IV induction followed by SC maintenance. The composite of week-12 response and week-48 remission occurred in 47%–55% with guselkumab versus 12%–13% with placebo. Serious adverse events occurred in 7%–11% with guselkumab, 12% with ustekinumab, and 15% with placebo; no deaths were reported. The primary report also found guselkumab favored over ustekinumab on several combined clinical-endoscopic outcomes. ([PubMed][20])

**Mirikizumab**

VIVID-1 was a treat-through trial with placebo and ustekinumab controls. The composite of week-12 symptom response plus week-52 endoscopic response occurred in 38.0% with mirikizumab versus 9.0% with placebo. The composite including week-52 CDAI remission occurred in 45.4% versus 19.6%. Serious adverse events occurred in 10.3% with mirikizumab, 10.7% with ustekinumab, and 17.1% with placebo. None of the three deaths was judged treatment-related. ([PubMed][21])

### 4. Oral JAK inhibition

**Upadacitinib**

In U-EXCEL, week-12 remission was 49.5% with upadacitinib versus 29.1% with placebo, and endoscopic response was 45.5% versus 13.1%. In the more treatment-refractory U-EXCEED population, remission was 38.9% versus 21.1%, and endoscopic response was 34.6% versus 3.5%.

Among induction responders in U-ENDURE, week-52 remission was:

* Upadacitinib 15 mg: **37.3%**
* Upadacitinib 30 mg: **47.6%**
* Withdrawal/placebo: **15.1%**

Endoscopic response was 27.6%, 40.1%, and 7.3%, respectively. Herpes zoster was more frequent with upadacitinib; hepatic abnormalities and neutropenia were more frequent with 30 mg. Gastrointestinal perforation occurred in four induction patients and one patient in each upadacitinib maintenance group. ([PubMed][22])

The subsequently reported full U-ENDURE population produced similar findings and reported no deaths, active tuberculosis, lymphoma, adjudicated major cardiovascular events, or venous thromboembolism during the controlled maintenance period. This does **not** exclude rare or longer-latency risks. ([PubMed][23])

**Filgotinib**

DIVERSITY is an important qualified/negative study. Filgotinib 200 mg did not meet both co-primary induction endpoints: symptom remission was significant only in one induction cohort, and endoscopic response was not significantly improved in either. Among induction responders, however, week-58 symptom remission was 43.8% versus 26.4% after withdrawal, and endoscopic response was 30.4% versus 9.4%. No deaths occurred, and serious adverse-event rates were not consistently higher than placebo. ([PubMed][24])

## Direct comparative and treatment-strategy evidence

SEAVUE found no significant difference between ustekinumab and adalimumab monotherapy in biologic-naive patients: week-52 clinical remission was 65% versus 61%. Serious infections occurred in 2% versus 3%. It was not designed to establish equivalence, and adalimumab dose escalation or concomitant immunomodulators were not permitted. ([PubMed][25])

PROFILE studied newly diagnosed active Crohn’s disease. Early top-down infliximab plus an immunomodulator produced sustained steroid- and surgery-free remission in 79% versus 15% with accelerated step-up treatment. There were fewer serious adverse events and surgeries with top-down treatment, without a statistically evident increase in serious infection. The study was open-label and used a composite outcome, but it supports early effective disease control rather than reserving advanced therapy solely for late failure. ([PubMed][26])

## Interpreting harms

The principal limitation is that pivotal RCTs are generally powered for efficacy, not uncommon harms such as opportunistic infections, malignancy, cardiovascular events, or thrombosis.

The prospective TREAT registry followed 6,273 patients for a mean of 5.2 years. Mortality was similar between infliximab-exposed and other-treatment groups. In adjusted analyses, infliximab was associated with serious infection, HR 1.43, but active disease, prednisone, and narcotic use were also independently associated and had effects of similar or greater magnitude. As an observational study, residual confounding by disease severity remains likely. ([PubMed][27])

A practical trial-level summary is:

* **Anti-TNF:** strongest established concerns are serious infection, tuberculosis/opportunistic infection, infusion or injection reactions, and immunogenic loss of response. Rare malignancies cannot be reliably quantified from individual Crohn’s RCTs.
* **Vedolizumab:** controlled trials do not show a treatment-specific opportunistic neurologic signal, but onset may be slower, particularly following anti-TNF failure.
* **Natalizumab:** PML is a rare but potentially catastrophic mechanism-specific harm.
* **Ustekinumab and IL-23p19 inhibitors:** serious adverse-event rates have generally been similar to placebo or active comparators in trials; available follow-up still cannot exclude uncommon or long-latency events.
* **Upadacitinib:** Crohn’s trials identified herpes zoster, hepatic laboratory abnormalities, neutropenia, and gastrointestinal perforation. The trials were not large enough to settle rare malignancy, cardiovascular, or thromboembolic risks.

## Clinical interpretation

For induction, anti-TNF agents, ustekinumab, selective IL-23 inhibitors, vedolizumab, and upadacitinib all have individual randomized evidence of benefit, although vedolizumab’s early effect is smaller and may be delayed in anti-TNF–experienced patients. Upadacitinib and the selective IL-23 trials show large placebo-adjusted effects on both symptoms and endoscopy, but cross-trial ranking is unreliable.

For maintenance, continuing an effective agent is consistently superior to withdrawal. In responder-enriched withdrawal trials, the absolute remission advantage is commonly approximately 15–30 percentage points, sometimes larger for endoscopic outcomes.

Selection should therefore depend not only on remission percentages but also on prior mechanisms failed, need for rapid symptom control, perianal disease, infection and malignancy history, thrombotic or cardiovascular risk, pregnancy plans, route and dosing preference, and whether combination immunomodulation is acceptable.
