## Scope

This is a focused evidence map of pivotal randomized trials and selected long-term primary safety cohorts published through **July 18, 2026**. I focused on adolescents/adults with moderate-to-severe active luminal Crohn’s disease; fistulizing evidence is noted separately. Postoperative prophylaxis and pediatric-only studies were not included.

A major interpretive issue is that many maintenance trials are **responder-enriched withdrawal trials**: everyone first receives active induction, and only responders are randomized to continue treatment or switch to placebo. Their remission percentages therefore apply to initial responders—not to every patient starting therapy.

## Main findings

### Infliximab

Infliximab has strong evidence for luminal and fistulizing disease.

In **ACCENT I**, patients first received one infliximab infusion; week-2 responders were randomized to scheduled maintenance or episodic/placebo treatment. At week 30, remission occurred in 39% with infliximab 5 mg/kg and 45% with 10 mg/kg, versus 21% with placebo maintenance. Median time to loss of response was also substantially longer with scheduled treatment. Serious infections were similar between randomized groups. ([PubMed][1])

In **ACCENT II**, among patients whose draining fistulas responded to infliximab induction, continued infliximab produced sustained fistula response at week 54 in 46% versus 23% with placebo; complete absence of draining fistulas occurred in 36% versus 19%. This remains the strongest randomized maintenance evidence for fistulizing Crohn’s disease. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

In treatment-naïve patients, **SONIC** found corticosteroid-free remission at week 26 in 56.8% with infliximab plus azathioprine, 44.4% with infliximab alone, and 30.0% with azathioprine alone. The approximate number needed to treat with combination rather than infliximab monotherapy was eight. Serious infections occurred in 3.9%, 4.9%, and 5.6%, respectively. This trial does not establish that indefinite combination treatment is preferable, but it clearly supports combination therapy during initial infliximab treatment in appropriate immunomodulator- and biologic-naïve patients. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])

Long-term observational evidence is less reassuring than the relatively short RCTs. In the **TREAT registry**, adjusted serious-infection risk was higher with infliximab, although active disease, prednisone and narcotic use were also major—and in some analyses stronger—risk factors. Mortality rates were essentially identical between infliximab-treated and other-treatment cohorts. Residual confounding by indication remains important. ([PubMed][4])

### Adalimumab

For biologic-naïve patients in **CLASSIC I**, week-4 remission was 36% with the licensed 160/80-mg induction regimen versus 12% with placebo—an absolute difference of 24 percentage points, or an approximate NNT of five. Injection-site reactions were more frequent, but overall adverse-event rates were similar. ([PubMed][5])

In **GAIN**, involving patients who had lost response to or could not tolerate infliximab, week-4 remission was 21% with adalimumab versus 7% with placebo, an absolute difference of 14 percentage points. No serious infections occurred in the adalimumab arm during the four-week study. ([ICHGCP][6])

Among induction responders in **CHARM**, remission at week 56 was 36% with every-other-week and 41% with weekly adalimumab, versus 12% with placebo withdrawal. Weekly dosing was not significantly more effective than every-other-week treatment in the randomized comparison. ([PubMed][7])

**EXTEND** added endoscopic evidence. At week 52, mucosal healing occurred in 24% with continuous adalimumab versus 0% after randomized withdrawal; clinical remission was 33% versus 9%. The week-12 mucosal-healing primary endpoint narrowly missed statistical significance, 27% versus 13%, P=0.056. ([PubMed][8])

The uncontrolled **PYRAMID registry** found serious infections at approximately 5.0 events per 100 patient-years among adalimumab-naïve participants. Malignancy, lymphoma, tuberculosis and demyelinating events were uncommon, although an uncontrolled registry cannot determine causality or reliably compare therapies. ([PMC][9])

### Certolizumab pegol

The certolizumab induction evidence is weaker than that for infliximab or adalimumab. In **PRECISE 1**, clinical response improved modestly, but remission rates were not significantly different from placebo. Serious adverse events occurred in 10% versus 7%, and serious infections in 2% versus less than 1%. ([PubMed][10])

In the responder-enriched **PRECISE 2** maintenance trial, week-26 remission was 48% with continued certolizumab versus 29% after withdrawal, an approximate NNT of six among induction responders. Serious infectious events occurred in 3% versus less than 1%, including one case of pulmonary tuberculosis. ([PubMed][11])

### Vedolizumab

In **GEMINI 2**, week-6 clinical remission was 14.5% with vedolizumab versus 6.8% with placebo, although the co-primary CDAI-100 response endpoint was not significantly different. Among induction responders, week-52 remission was 39.0% with every-eight-week and 36.4% with every-four-week vedolizumab, versus 21.6% after withdrawal. In this study, serious adverse events and serious infections were numerically more frequent with vedolizumab, although many events reflected Crohn’s disease complications and later safety studies did not reproduce a major systemic-infection signal. ([PubMed][12])

**GEMINI 3**, primarily involving prior anti-TNF failure, illustrates vedolizumab’s slower onset. Week-6 remission was not significantly different, 15.2% versus 12.1%, but by week 10 remission was 26.6% versus 12.1%. ([PubMed][13])

In **VISIBLE 2**, induction responders randomized to subcutaneous maintenance had week-52 remission of 48.0% versus 34.3% with placebo withdrawal. Injection-site reactions occurred in 2.9%; no other important new safety signal emerged. ([PMC][14])

The open-label **GEMINI long-term safety study**, with exposure extending to eight years, reported no progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy and no new pattern of infections, malignancies, infusion reactions or hepatic events. Interpretation is limited by open-label selection and substantial discontinuation over time. ([PubMed][15])

### Ustekinumab

In **UNITI-1**, involving anti-TNF failure, week-6 response was approximately 34% with ustekinumab versus 21.5% with placebo. In **UNITI-2**, involving failure of conventional treatment, response was approximately 52–56% versus 28.7%. Among induction responders in **IM-UNITI**, week-44 remission was 53.1% with dosing every eight weeks and 48.8% every 12 weeks, versus 35.9% after withdrawal. Adverse-event rates were similar between groups. ([PubMed][16])

The head-to-head **SEAVUE** trial found no significant difference between ustekinumab and adalimumab monotherapy in biologic-naïve patients: week-52 remission was 65% versus 61%. Serious infections occurred in 2% and 3%, respectively. ([PubMed][17])

At five years in the **IM-UNITI extension**, serious-infection rates were 3.4 per 100 patient-years with ustekinumab versus 3.9 in the placebo-reference group; anti-drug antibodies developed in 5.8%. Long-term efficacy estimates are affected by survivor and extension-study selection. ([PubMed][18])

### Risankizumab

The **ADVANCE** and **MOTIVATE** trials showed consistent clinical and endoscopic induction efficacy. With the licensed 600-mg induction regimen, week-12 CDAI remission was:

* ADVANCE: 45% versus 25% with placebo.
* MOTIVATE, which enrolled biologic failures: 42% versus 20%.

Endoscopic response was 40% versus 12% in ADVANCE and 29% versus 11% in MOTIVATE. Overall treatment-emergent adverse-event rates were similar across groups. ([PubMed][19])

Among induction responders in **FORTIFY**, week-52 CDAI remission with risankizumab 360 mg was 52% versus 41% after withdrawal, while endoscopic response was 47% versus 22%. The endoscopic absolute benefit was therefore considerably larger than the symptom-defined remission benefit. Adverse-event rates were similar. ([PubMed][20])

In **SEQUENCE**, patients with prior anti-TNF failure were randomized directly to risankizumab or ustekinumab. Risankizumab was noninferior for week-24 clinical remission, 58.6% versus 39.5%, and superior for week-48 endoscopic remission, 31.8% versus 16.2%. Adverse-event incidence appeared similar. This is among the most informative comparative trials for choosing a second-line biologic after anti-TNF failure. ([New England Journal of Medicine][21])

### Upadacitinib

Upadacitinib has high placebo-adjusted clinical and endoscopic efficacy but a more distinctive adverse-event profile.

At week 12:

* **U-EXCEL:** remission 49.5% versus 29.1%; endoscopic response 45.5% versus 13.1%.
* **U-EXCEED**, predominantly prior biologic failure: remission 38.9% versus 21.1%; endoscopic response 34.6% versus 3.5%.

Among induction responders in **U-ENDURE**, week-52 remission was 37.3% with 15 mg and 47.6% with 30 mg, versus 15.1% after withdrawal. Endoscopic response was 27.6%, 40.1%, and 7.3%, respectively. ([PubMed][22])

Herpes zoster occurred more frequently with 45-mg induction and 30-mg maintenance treatment. Hepatic disorders and neutropenia were more frequent with 30 mg. Gastrointestinal perforations occurred in four induction patients receiving upadacitinib and in one patient in each active maintenance-dose group. The trials were too small and short to precisely quantify uncommon malignancy, cardiovascular or thromboembolic outcomes. ([PubMed][22])

### Mirikizumab

**VIVID-1** was a treat-through trial with placebo and ustekinumab comparators. The composite of week-12 symptomatic response followed by week-52 CDAI remission occurred in 45.4% with mirikizumab versus 19.6% with placebo. The corresponding composite ending in week-52 endoscopic response occurred in 38.0% versus 9.0%. Serious adverse events occurred in 10.3% with mirikizumab, 10.7% with ustekinumab and 17.1% with placebo. The coprimary superiority comparisons were against placebo; the presence of an ustekinumab arm should not automatically be interpreted as proof of superiority over ustekinumab. ([PubMed][23])

### Guselkumab

**GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3** were treat-through, placebo- and ustekinumab-controlled phase 3 trials. The composite of week-12 clinical response and week-48 remission occurred in:

* GALAXI-2: 49–55% with guselkumab versus 12% with placebo.
* GALAXI-3: 47–48% versus 13%.

The composite ending in week-48 endoscopic response occurred in 38–39% versus 5% in GALAXI-2 and 34–36% versus 6% in GALAXI-3. Prespecified pooled analyses also supported superiority to ustekinumab across several week-48 outcomes. Serious adverse events occurred in 7–11% with guselkumab, 12% with ustekinumab and 15% with placebo; no deaths were reported. ([PubMed][24])

## Clinical interpretation

**For inducing symptomatic remission**, all major mechanisms have positive placebo-controlled evidence, but absolute efficacy cannot be validly ranked across trials because prior biologic exposure, baseline endoscopic severity, remission definitions, rescue rules and assessment times differ substantially.

**For endoscopic improvement**, the largest placebo-adjusted differences in recent pivotal trials were observed with upadacitinib and selective IL-23 inhibitors. This does not prove universal superiority because only a few direct comparisons exist. ([PubMed][22])

**For maintenance**, infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, risankizumab and upadacitinib all reduced relapse or increased remission relative to treatment withdrawal. However, most maintenance percentages describe patients who had already responded to induction.

**For fistulizing Crohn’s disease**, infliximab has the clearest dedicated randomized evidence. Adalimumab has supportive fistula outcomes, but these were generally secondary or subgroup endpoints rather than dedicated trials. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

**The most actionable direct comparisons are:**

* Infliximab plus azathioprine was more effective than infliximab or azathioprine alone in treatment-naïve disease in SONIC. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])
* Ustekinumab and adalimumab monotherapy were not significantly different in biologic-naïve disease in SEAVUE. ([PubMed][17])
* Risankizumab produced better endoscopic remission than ustekinumab after anti-TNF failure in SEQUENCE. ([New England Journal of Medicine][21])
* Guselkumab outperformed placebo and, in pooled analyses, ustekinumab across several endpoints in GALAXI-2/3. ([PubMed][24])

**Regarding harms**, short-term RCTs generally found similar overall serious-adverse-event rates between biologics and controls. They are poorly powered for tuberculosis, opportunistic infection, lymphoma, demyelination, major cardiovascular events and pregnancy outcomes. Anti-TNF registries suggest a serious-infection signal, strongly modified by corticosteroid exposure and uncontrolled disease. Upadacitinib trials identified herpes zoster, laboratory abnormalities and gastrointestinal perforation as specific concerns. Long-term safety experience is more mature for anti-TNF agents, vedolizumab and ustekinumab than for newer selective IL-23 inhibitors. ([PubMed][4])
