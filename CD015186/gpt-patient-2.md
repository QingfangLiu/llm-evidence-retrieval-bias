# SC-MIGS for open-angle glaucoma: benefits and harms

I searched for **individual primary clinical studies available through July 25, 2026**, prioritizing randomized trials, prospective studies, and larger observational cohorts. I excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee statements, and editorials.

I use **SC-MIGS** broadly for procedures that improve conventional aqueous outflow through the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal:

* **Implants:** Hydrus Microstent and iStent/iStent inject.
* **Tissue-opening or canal-dilating procedures:** Kahook Dual Blade, GATT, ab-interno canaloplasty, OMNI canaloplasty/trabeculotomy, Trabectome, TRAB360, and excimer-laser trabeculotomy.

Traditional ab-externo canaloplasty was not included because it is not consistently classified as MIGS.

## Main conclusions

### 1. Fewer glaucoma drops is the most consistent benefit

When SC-MIGS is added to cataract surgery, patients are generally more likely to become medication-free or use fewer drops. The **additional pressure reduction beyond cataract surgery alone is often modest**, especially when preoperative medicated pressure is already in the mid-teens.

For example:

* In the 556-eye HORIZON trial, Hydrus plus cataract surgery produced an additional **2.3 mmHg of unmedicated pressure reduction** at two years compared with cataract surgery alone. At five years, 66% versus 46% were medication-free. ([PubMed][1])
* In the pivotal iStent inject trial, the additional unmedicated reduction attributable to the stents was approximately **1.6 mmHg** at two years, while the proportion with unmedicated pressure of 18 mmHg or lower was 63.2% versus 50.0%. ([PubMed][2])
* In a later pragmatic iStent inject trial, patients used about **0.6 fewer medications**, but pressure and glaucoma-related quality-of-life outcomes were not significantly different from cataract surgery alone. ([PubMed][3])
* A 2026 three-arm trial similarly found that iStent W and Kahook Dual Blade reduced medications more than cataract surgery alone, but all three groups had similar pressure reductions. ([ScienceDirect][4])

Thus, a procedure can be successful for someone whose main goal is reducing drops even when it produces little additional office-pressure lowering.

### 2. Hydrus has the strongest long-term randomized evidence

Among the procedures studied, Hydrus has particularly substantial randomized follow-up:

* At five years, Hydrus plus cataract surgery resulted in more medication-free patients, more patients meeting unmedicated pressure targets, and fewer subsequent incisional glaucoma operations than cataract surgery alone: **2.4% versus 6.2%** underwent later incisional glaucoma surgery. No significant difference in endothelial-cell loss emerged between years three and five. ([PubMed][5])
* A post-hoc visual-field analysis found slower mean-field progression with Hydrus: approximately **−0.26 versus −0.49 dB/year**. This is encouraging, but it was an exploratory analysis of the HORIZON population rather than a separately powered, prespecified vision-outcome trial. ([PubMed][6])

Most other SC-MIGS studies were too short or too small to determine whether surgery prevents visual disability.

### 3. Cutting more of the trabecular meshwork may lower pressure more—but causes more bleeding

In a randomized comparison of GATT and ab-interno canaloplasty:

* Twelve-month pressure averaged **16.0 mmHg after GATT versus 19.0 mmHg after canaloplasty**.
* Medication-free rates were 77.8% versus 57.2%, although that difference did not reach conventional statistical significance.
* Hyphema was reported in **87% after GATT versus 47% after canaloplasty**. Imaging-detected supraciliary effusions were also frequent in both groups. ([PubMed][7])

This illustrates an important trade-off: 360-degree trabeculotomy may have greater pressure-lowering potential, but blood reflux into the anterior chamber is expected more often.

### 4. Evidence for KDB added to cataract surgery is mixed

A small randomized study of 42 eyes found no significant advantage from adding Kahook Dual Blade to cataract surgery: at 12 months, pressure was 16.0 mmHg with KDB and 15.0 mmHg with cataract surgery alone, with no significant medication difference. ([PubMed][8])

The larger 2026 trial found better composite “success” and greater medication reduction with KDB, but not greater mean pressure reduction. Hyphema occurred in **15%** of KDB eyes. ([ScienceDirect][4])

Uncontrolled KDB studies often report larger reductions, but those estimates include regression to the mean, cataract-surgery effects, medication changes, and patient selection. One prospective series reported pressure falling from 16.8 to 12.4 mmHg and medications from 1.6 to 0.8, with occasional pressure spikes, cystoid macular edema, hypotony, choroidal detachment, and peripheral anterior synechiae. ([PMC][9])

### 5. OMNI results are favorable, but much of the evidence is uncontrolled

In the prospective, single-arm GEMINI study of OMNI combined with cataract surgery:

* Unmedicated diurnal pressure decreased from 23.8 to 15.6 mmHg.
* Medication use decreased from 1.8 to 0.4.
* Eighty percent were medication-free at 12 months.
* Hyphema occurred in 6% and transient pressure elevation in 2%. ([PMC][10])

A selected extension cohort maintained pressures around 16 mmHg and low medication use through three years. However, only a subset entered the extension, and there was no cataract-surgery-only control group. ([PubMed][11])

Retrospective ROMEO studies found postoperative inflammation, pressure spikes and hyphema, with approximately **5–10% requiring additional glaucoma intervention** during early follow-up. ([PubMed][12])

### 6. Stand-alone surgery may produce larger reductions when starting pressure is high

Greater reductions are often seen in stand-alone studies involving higher baseline pressures:

* In the original two-year GATT series, eyes with primary open-angle glaucoma had an average reduction of approximately **9.2 mmHg** and 1.43 medications, although cumulative failure varied substantially among glaucoma subgroups. ([PubMed][13])
* In 81 refractory eyes treated with TRAB360, pressure decreased by 7.3 mmHg and medications by 0.6 at one year. However, mild transient hyphema occurred in 57 eyes and **25% required another intervention within one year**. ([PubMed][14])
* In the randomized COMPARE trial, stand-alone Hydrus provided greater medication reduction than two first-generation iStents, but the trial did not include a no-surgery control group. ([PubMed][15])

These results should not be directly applied to a patient whose pressure is already 14–16 mmHg on medication.

## Harms seen across the primary studies

### Common or expected

**Hyphema or blood reflux:** Particularly common after GATT, TRAB360, and excisional goniotomy. Reported rates range widely because some studies count microscopic or imaging-detected blood while others count only clinically apparent hyphema. ([PubMed][7])

**Early pressure spikes:** Generally transient, but sometimes require additional drops or procedures. Rates around 2–15% were reported in several OMNI, KDB, and comparative studies. ([PMC][10])

**Anterior-chamber inflammation, corneal edema, discomfort and blurred vision:** Usually transient and overlapping with normal cataract-surgery recovery.

### Procedure-specific

**Stent obstruction, malposition or peripheral anterior synechiae:** In the pivotal iStent inject trial, stent obstruction was reported in approximately 6.2%; peripheral anterior synechiae were uncommon. An earlier Hydrus trial identified focal peripheral anterior synechiae as the principal device-specific finding. ([ScienceDirect][16])

**Incomplete or waning effect:** Some patients continue to require drops, laser treatment, repeat MIGS, trabeculectomy or a drainage implant. Reintervention risk is higher in studies involving refractory or advanced disease. ([PubMed][5])

**Rare potentially serious complications:** Cystoid macular edema, persistent inflammation, hypotony, choroidal detachment, corneal problems and meaningful visual-acuity loss were reported occasionally. Individual MIGS trials are generally too small to measure very rare events reliably. ([PMC][9])

## What the evidence does—and does not—answer

The studies most strongly support SC-MIGS for **mild-to-moderate open-angle glaucoma**, particularly when cataract surgery is already planned and the goals are:

* Reducing medication burden.
* Obtaining a modest additional pressure reduction.
* Delaying or avoiding more invasive glaucoma surgery.

There is much less reliable evidence for people who have advanced field loss, rapid progression, normal-tension glaucoma requiring a very low target pressure, or previous major glaucoma surgery. Most trials used pressure, medication count and composite “success” rather than blindness, reading ability, driving function or long-term quality of life as outcomes.

For an individual decision, the most important distinction is whether your primary objective is **fewer drops** or whether your optic nerve requires a substantially lower target pressure. Those are not necessarily achieved equally by SC-MIGS.
