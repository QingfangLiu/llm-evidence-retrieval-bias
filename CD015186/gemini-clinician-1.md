When evaluating Schlemm’s canal minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (SC-MIGS) for patients with open-angle glaucoma (OAG), the primary literature points to a strong safety profile and meaningful reductions in both intraocular pressure (IOP) and medication burden. These procedures—which include trabecular micro-bypass stents (e.g., iStent), excisional goniotomy (e.g., Kahook Dual Blade [KDB]), and ab interno trabeculotomy—work by bypassing or removing the juxtacanalicular trabecular meshwork, the site of highest resistance to aqueous outflow.

Here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms based on recent primary clinical trials and comparative studies.

## Benefits of SC-MIGS

**1. Sustained IOP Reduction**
SC-MIGS consistently lowers IOP, particularly when combined with phacoemulsification. In a landmark pivotal trial for the iStent inject, 75.8% of patients receiving the stent plus cataract surgery achieved a ≥20% reduction in unmedicated IOP at 24 months, compared to 61.9% in the cataract-surgery-alone control group (Samuelson et al., 2019). Similarly, ab interno trabeculotomy (using systems like VISCO360, a predecessor to OMNI) combined with cataract extraction yielded a 60.1% peak reduction in IOP at one month, with sustained significant reductions followed out to 2 years (Chen et al., 2020).

**2. Significant Reduction in Medication Burden**
One of the most immediate benefits for patients is independence from topical hypotensive drops, which improves quality of life and reduces ocular surface toxicity. In a prospective randomized trial comparing KDB goniotomy to iStent implantation, both groups saw mean IOP-lowering medications drop from ~1.3 at baseline to 0.3 or 0.4 at 12 months (Falkenberry et al., 2020).

**3. Comparable Efficacy Across SC-MIGS Modalities**
While device designs differ, clinical outcomes are largely comparable, though some nuanced differences exist in primary endpoints. When KDB excisional goniotomy was compared directly to iStent implantation, goniotomy resulted in a slightly higher proportion of eyes meeting the primary clinical success outcome (≥20% IOP reduction or ≥1 medication reduction) at 12 months—93.7% for KDB versus 83.3% for iStent (Falkenberry et al., 2020). A separate retrospective study comparing KDB to the Trabectome found that while both were highly effective, the KDB cohort experienced a slightly greater reduction in the number of required glaucoma medications (Fliney et al., 2023).

## Harms and Clinical Limitations

**1. The Episcleral Venous Pressure "Floor"**
Because SC-MIGS relies on the eye’s physiological distal outflow pathway (Schlemm's canal and the collector channels), these procedures generally cannot lower IOP below episcleral venous pressure (typically ~15 mmHg). A randomized clinical trial by Ventura-Abreu et al. (2021) highlighted this ceiling effect: in patients whose OAG was already well-controlled with drops prior to surgery, adding KDB trabeculectomy to phacoemulsification was not significantly more effective than phacoemulsification alone at driving IOP down further into the mid-teens.

**2. Transient Adverse Events**
SC-MIGS avoids the bleb-related complications of traditional filtering surgeries like trabeculectomy (e.g., hypotony maculopathy, blebitis, choroidal effusion). However, they are not complication-free. The most common adverse events are transient postoperative hyphema (due to blood reflux from Schlemm's canal) and early postoperative IOP spikes (Ventura-Abreu et al., 2021). These are generally self-limiting and resolve with standard postoperative medical management.

**3. Endothelial Cell Loss**
Any anterior segment surgery carries a risk of corneal endothelial cell loss. While the 2-year pivotal data for micro-bypass stents demonstrated an excellent overall safety profile comparable to cataract surgery alone (Samuelson et al., 2019), clinicians should monitor endothelial cell counts closely in patients with compromised corneas, as reductions can occasionally precipitate corneal decompensation (Ventura-Abreu et al., 2021).
