## Scope

I treated **Schlemm’s canal–based MIGS (SC-MIGS)** as procedures that improve conventional aqueous outflow through the trabecular meshwork, Schlemm’s canal, or collector channels:

* Trabecular bypass implants: **iStent/iStent inject, Hydrus**
* Trabecular incision or removal: **GATT, Kahook Dual Blade, Trabectome, BANG**
* Canal viscodilation/canaloplasty: **ABiC/iTrack, OMNI**

I excluded subconjunctival devices, suprachoroidal devices, cyclophotocoagulation, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, opinions, and editorials. The search was current through **July 25, 2026**. The studies below are pivotal and representative primary studies rather than every small retrospective case series ever published.

## Main conclusions

**The most reliable evidence is for Hydrus or iStent added to cataract surgery in mild-to-moderate open-angle glaucoma.** They provide a modest additional pressure reduction beyond cataract surgery alone, but often a clinically meaningful increase in the chance of using fewer or no glaucoma drops. Hydrus has randomized follow-up through five years showing fewer subsequent incisional glaucoma operations. ([PubMed][1])

For **standalone SC-MIGS**, or procedures such as GATT, KDB, Trabectome, ABiC, and OMNI, the reported pressure reductions can be larger, particularly when preoperative pressure is high. However, much of that evidence comes from uncontrolled cohorts, and durability and reoperation rates are less certain. ([PubMed][2])

Most studies measured **IOP, medication use, and reoperation**, not prevention of visual-field loss or blindness. Most participants had mild-to-moderate disease, so the evidence applies less directly to advanced glaucoma requiring a very low target pressure.

## Harms and disadvantages

### Bleeding and temporary blurred vision

Blood reflux from Schlemm’s canal and **hyphema** are expected after trabeculotomy or goniotomy. Usually this clears spontaneously, but it can temporarily blur vision and occasionally requires an anterior-chamber washout.

Reported hyphema rates vary considerably with procedure and how aggressively it was looked for:

* GATT vs ABiC trial: 87% after GATT versus 47% after ABiC.
* GATT vs BANG trial: 56.5% versus 40.9%.
* Original adult GATT series: transient hyphema in approximately 30% at one week.
* OMNI GEMINI: clinically reported transient hyphema in 6%. ([PubMed][2])

This makes the exact procedure and the patient’s anticoagulant/antiplatelet situation important—not simply the umbrella term “MIGS.”

### Early IOP elevation

Temporary pressure spikes can occur from retained viscoelastic, inflammation, blood, steroid response, or blockage of the new outflow opening. Rates in selected studies included approximately 2% with OMNI in GEMINI, 3.8% in a prospective KDB series, and roughly 9%–11% after different extents of GATT in a 2026 comparative cohort. ([PMC][10])

### Implant-specific problems

Hydrus and iStent can become malpositioned or obstructed, or develop peripheral anterior synechiae around the implant. In the Hydrus HORIZON continuation data, PAS was reported in 14.6%, obstruction in 8.4%, and malposition in 1.4%; these events generally did not produce serious sequelae or require explantation. ([FDA Access Data][14])

In the iStent inject pivotal trial, PAS was reported in 1.8%, no stent migration or dislocation was reported, and incisional glaucoma surgery occurred in about 1% in both the implant and cataract-only groups over two years. ([PMC][15])

### Corneal endothelial cells

The five-year iStent inject extension found no significant difference from cataract surgery alone in endothelial-cell density or the percentage with major cell loss. ([PubMed][16])

Hydrus findings require more nuance. The main five-year HORIZON report found no significant difference in the **rate of endothelial loss from months 3–60**, but a later patient-level analysis found that 20.8% of Hydrus eyes versus 10.6% of cataract-only eyes had substantial cumulative endothelial-cell loss at five years. Most of the difference appeared early after surgery, the subsequent rate paralleled controls, and no Hydrus patient developed persistent corneal edema in that analysis. ([PubMed][3])

### Inflammation and less-common complications

Transient inflammation, pain, cystoid macular edema, hypotony, choroidal detachment, iridodialysis, or Descemet membrane injury have been reported, but generally at low rates. Serious events such as endophthalmitis, sustained hypotony, or severe permanent visual loss were uncommon in the randomized trials. Small studies are not large enough to exclude rare harms reliably. ([PMC][17])

### Failure or insufficient pressure lowering

SC-MIGS does not create a new external drainage pathway; therefore, pressure remains dependent on the downstream collector channels and episcleral venous pressure. Some eyes obtain little benefit or lose benefit over time and subsequently require laser, repeat MIGS, trabeculectomy, or a tube shunt. The four-year GATT cohort’s 42% reoperation rate is an important example, although it included a relatively difficult and heterogeneous referral population. ([PubMed][18])

## How this applies clinically

The evidence most strongly supports considering SC-MIGS when:

* Glaucoma is mild or moderate.
* The desired pressure is in the mid-teens rather than extremely low.
* Reducing medication burden is an important goal.
* Cataract surgery is already planned.
* Preserving the conjunctiva for possible future filtration surgery is valuable.

The decision is less straightforward when glaucoma is advanced, progression is occurring at an IOP already in the low teens, the corneal endothelium is compromised, or bleeding would be especially hazardous. Those situations may favor a different procedure or require a very explicit discussion of the likelihood that another glaucoma operation will eventually be needed.
