It’s completely understandable to want a clear picture of what minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) entails before making a decision. Because you have open-angle glaucoma, targeting Schlemm’s canal is a common and logical approach.

The fluid in your eye (aqueous humor) normally drains through a spongy tissue called the trabecular meshwork and into Schlemm’s canal. In open-angle glaucoma, that meshwork becomes resistant and clogged, elevating your eye pressure. SC-MIGS procedures—such as the iStent, Hydrus Microstent, or canaloplasty—work by either bypassing, stretching, or cutting through that clogged meshwork to restore fluid flow directly into Schlemm's canal.

Based strictly on primary, peer-reviewed clinical trials and comparative cohort studies, here is a breakdown of the observed benefits and harms of these procedures.

## Benefits of SC-MIGS

* **Significant Pressure Reduction:** The primary goal of SC-MIGS is to safely lower your intraocular pressure (IOP). Large-scale pivotal trials demonstrate that placing an SC-MIGS device (such as the iStent inject) during cataract surgery yields a clinically significant decrease in unmedicated IOP compared to doing cataract surgery alone (Samuelson et al., 2019). Two-year comparative data between popular devices like Hydrus and iStent show sustained pressure reductions averaging 2 to 3 mmHg further below the patient's baseline (Holmes et al., 2022).
* **Reduced Medication Burden:** For many patients, the greatest quality-of-life improvement is dropping the daily routine of glaucoma eye drops. Primary studies have shown that placing a micro-bypass stent can be as effective as using two separate ocular hypotensive medications (Giamporcaro et al., 2014). Across multiple prospective and retrospective studies, patients typically see a 70% to 80% reduction in their need for medications, with a large majority remaining completely drop-free 12 to 24 months after surgery (Chee et al., 2023; Jabłońska et al., 2023).
* **Sparing of Eye Tissue:** Because these procedures are done *ab interno* (from inside the eye, through the same tiny incision used for cataract surgery), they avoid cutting the outer conjunctiva or creating a "bleb" (a surgically created fluid blister). This translates to faster recovery and preserves your eye tissue intact in case you ever need more traditional, invasive surgeries down the road (Samuelson et al., 2019).

## Harms and Risks

* **Transient IOP Spikes:** It is not uncommon to experience a temporary surge in eye pressure shortly after the procedure. This is typically short-lived and self-resolving, though it requires close monitoring by your ophthalmologist in the days immediately following surgery (Laroche et al., 2022).
* **Hyphema (Bleeding in the Eye):** Because the surgery interacts directly with the eye's delicate vascular drainage system, minor bleeding into the front chamber of the eye (hyphema) can occur. Like IOP spikes, this usually clears up on its own over a few days or weeks without causing long-term vision damage (Laroche et al., 2022).
* **Device Malposition or Need for Reoperation:** While long-term, sight-threatening complications are exceedingly rare with SC-MIGS, microstents can occasionally become blocked by scar tissue or malpositioned. In a two-year study tracking both iStent and Hydrus implants, roughly 5% to 7.5% of eyes required a subsequent glaucoma procedure to regain optimal IOP control (Holmes et al., 2022).
