Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery targeting Schlemm’s canal (SC-MIGS) aims to reduce intraocular pressure (IOP) by bypassing the juxtacanalicular trabecular meshwork. This enhances the natural outflow of aqueous humor directly into the canal. These procedures (which include microstents like Hydrus and iStent, or excisional tools like the Kahook Dual Blade) are frequently performed alongside phacoemulsification for cataracts, though they are increasingly validated as standalone treatments.

Based on primary clinical studies and randomized controlled trials, here is a synthesis of the benefits and harms of SC-MIGS for patients with open-angle glaucoma.

## Benefits

* **Significant IOP Reduction:** SC-MIGS procedures effectively and sustainably lower IOP. In the HORIZON randomized controlled trial, 77.3% of patients who received a Hydrus microstent combined with cataract surgery achieved a ≥20% reduction in unmedicated IOP at 24 months, compared to only 57.8% of the control group receiving cataract surgery alone (Samuelson et al., 2019). Long-term data for standalone excisional goniotomy with the Kahook Dual Blade (KDB) demonstrated a sustained average IOP reduction of 28.0% over six years (Vasu et al., 2024).
* **Reduced Medication Burden:** A major clinical benefit is decreasing a patient's reliance on daily topical hypotensive drops. At three years post-surgery, 73% of patients with the Hydrus microstent were completely medication-free, compared to 48% of the cataract-surgery-only group (Ahmed et al., 2021). Similarly, 360° ab-interno viscodilation of Schlemm's canal showed significant medication reductions, leaving 86% of eyes medication-free at 12 months (Ondrejka & Körber, 2019).
* **Lower Risk of Future Invasive Surgery:** By stabilizing IOP and visual field function early, SC-MIGS reduces the probability that a patient will require traditional, more invasive filtering surgeries (such as a trabeculectomy). The 3-year cumulative probability of needing incisional glaucoma surgery was significantly lower in microstent patients (0.6%) versus the control group (3.9%) (Ahmed et al., 2021).

## Harms and Risks

* **Hyphema:** The most frequently reported adverse event across primary SC-MIGS studies is hyphema (bleeding into the anterior chamber). Because these procedures interact directly with the vascular Schlemm's canal, some reflux bleeding is expected. However, these hyphemas are generally small (<1 mm) and tend to self-resolve within a week without requiring secondary intervention (Ondrejka & Körber, 2019).
* **Need for Secondary Surgical Intervention:** While device success rates are high, there remains a small risk of failure necessitating further glaucoma surgery. In the COMPARE trial evaluating standalone microstents, secondary glaucoma surgery was required within 12 months in 3.9% of eyes receiving two iStent devices, whereas no eyes in the Hydrus group required secondary surgical intervention (Ahmed et al., 2020).
* **Corneal Endothelial Cell Loss:** Protecting the corneal endothelium is a primary safety metric for any anterior segment implant. Fortunately, long-term RCT data is reassuring; 3-year findings from the HORIZON trial found no statistically significant difference in postoperative corneal endothelial cell loss between eyes with microstent placement and those that had standard cataract surgery alone (Ahmed et al., 2021).
* **Visual Acuity Loss:** Severe complications are quite rare, but a very small percentage of patients may experience a loss in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of ≥2 lines. Across studies, this is typically attributed to overlapping pathologies or generalized postoperative complications rather than catastrophic device failure (Ahmed et al., 2020).
