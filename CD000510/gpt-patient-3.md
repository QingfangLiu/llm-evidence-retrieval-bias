## Bottom line

The answer depends heavily on **what “selective treatment” involves**.

In older trials, selective surfactant was often delayed until an infant had substantial oxygen and ventilator requirements. Compared with that delayed-rescue approach, prophylactic surfactant given immediately after birth sometimes reduced severe RDS, air leaks, and mortality—particularly among the most immature infants. The benefit was not consistent across every trial. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

In more recent trials, infants assigned to selective treatment usually started with **early CPAP**, close monitoring, and prompt surfactant if respiratory failure progressed. Under those conditions, routinely intubating every infant to give prophylactic surfactant did **not clearly reduce death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia**, while many infants in the selective groups avoided intubation, mechanical ventilation, and surfactant altogether. ([PubMed][2])

Therefore, for a spontaneously breathing preterm infant who can be stabilized noninvasively, the modern randomized trials generally support **CPAP with early selective surfactant rather than routine prophylactic intubation**. That inference does not apply as cleanly when an infant already requires intubation during resuscitation or has severe respiratory failure immediately after birth.

## Findings from the older direct-comparison trials

**Kendig et al., 1991 — infants under 30 weeks**

Surfactant was given either immediately after delivery or several hours later if oxygen or ventilator criteria were reached. Survival to hospital discharge was 88% with prophylaxis versus 80% with rescue treatment, an absolute difference of 8 percentage points. Among infants at or below 26 weeks, survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax occurred in 7% versus 18%. These results favor prophylaxis, but rescue treatment was relatively delayed by contemporary standards. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

**Merritt/Hallman et al., 1991 — infants 24–29 weeks**

This three-arm trial compared prophylactic human surfactant, later rescue surfactant, and placebo. Surfactant improved early oxygenation and reduced pulmonary interstitial emphysema compared with placebo, but the investigators found no statistically significant overall advantage of prophylactic administration over rescue administration. ([PubMed][3])

**Egberts et al., 1993 — infants 26–29 weeks**

Prophylaxis improved early oxygenation and reduced severe RDS from 32% to 19%. However, mortality, pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, brain injury, duration of ventilation, and duration of oxygen treatment did not differ significantly. Rescue surfactant was not given until infants required mechanical ventilation and an oxygen concentration of at least 60%, which represents relatively late treatment. ([PubMed][4])

**Kattwinkel et al., 1993 — infants 29–32 weeks**

Prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS, repeat surfactant treatment, and the combined outcome of death or oxygen use at 28 days, which occurred in 5% versus 9%. The prophylaxis group had lower one-minute Apgar scores, although the difference was no longer present at five minutes and was not associated with reported asphyxia complications. Importantly, only 43% of infants managed selectively ultimately received surfactant, demonstrating that routine prophylaxis exposed many infants who would not otherwise have needed it. ([PubMed][5])

**Walti et al., 1995 — infants 25–31 weeks**

Survival without BPD was 60% with prophylaxis and 46% with rescue therapy, but the confidence interval included no effect. Severe intracranial hemorrhage and retinopathy were less frequent in the prophylaxis group; these were secondary outcomes and should be interpreted more cautiously than the principal outcome. ([EBSCO OpenURL][6])

**Bevilacqua et al., 1996 — infants 24–30 weeks**

Prophylaxis reduced severe RDS and pulmonary interstitial emphysema. Mortality was 21% with prophylaxis versus 35% with rescue treatment, an absolute difference of 14 percentage points. Again, both groups were treated in an era in which infants were commonly intubated and rescue treatment depended on established RDS and mechanical ventilation. ([PubMed][7])

## Findings from CPAP-era randomized trials

**SUPPORT trial, 2010 — infants 24–27 weeks**

Infants were assigned either to intubation and surfactant within the first hour or to delivery-room CPAP with selective intubation and surfactant according to protocol. Death or BPD occurred in 47.8% of the CPAP group and 51.0% of the prophylactic-surfactant group, with no statistically significant difference. CPAP reduced intubation, postnatal steroid exposure, and duration of mechanical ventilation without increasing the overall rate of major neonatal complications. ([PubMed][2])

**Sandri et al., 2010 — infants 25–28 weeks**

All infants were initially breathing spontaneously and suitable for CPAP. Prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation was compared with CPAP and early selective surfactant. Mechanical ventilation during the first five days was required in 31.4% versus 33.0%, respectively. Death, BPD, and other major complications were also similar. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][8])

**Dunn et al., 2011 — infants 26–29 weeks**

The trial compared prophylactic surfactant with continued ventilation, prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation, and initial bubble CPAP with selective surfactant. There was no statistically significant difference in death or BPD among the strategies. In the CPAP/selective group, 48% avoided intubation and ventilation, and 54% avoided surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][9])

**COIN trial, 2008 — infants 25–28 weeks**

This was primarily a CPAP-versus-intubation trial rather than a strict prophylactic-versus-selective surfactant trial. CPAP approximately halved surfactant use and reduced ventilation duration, with no significant difference in death or BPD. Pneumothorax, however, was more common with CPAP: 9% versus 3%. This shows that noninvasive selective management also has potential harms and requires careful monitoring. ([PubMed][10])

**POPART trial, 2024 — infants under 29 weeks**

This trial tested prophylactic **oropharyngeal**, rather than intratracheal, surfactant in addition to CPAP. It did not reduce intubation by 120 hours: 63.5% versus 64.8%. Pneumothorax was more frequent with oropharyngeal surfactant, 16.6% versus 6.4%. Because the delivery route was experimental, this result should not be generalized directly to standard intratracheal surfactant. ([PubMed][11])

## Potential benefits of prophylactic surfactant

The individual studies suggest that prophylaxis can:

* Improve oxygenation during the first several hours.
* Reduce the incidence or severity of RDS when rescue therapy would otherwise be delayed.
* Reduce some air-leak complications in selected very immature populations.
* Reduce mortality in certain older trials, particularly among extremely premature infants. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

These benefits were largest when the comparison group did not receive surfactant until RDS was already relatively advanced.

## Potential harms and burdens

**Unnecessary intubation and treatment:** A substantial fraction of infants managed with CPAP and selective surfactant never require surfactant. Routine prophylaxis therefore exposes some infants to laryngoscopy, endotracheal intubation, medication administration, and at least brief positive-pressure ventilation without evidence that they personally would have benefited. ([PubMed][5])

**No clear modern reduction in death or BPD:** In the large CPAP-era trials, prophylactic intubation and surfactant did not significantly improve these major outcomes. ([PubMed][2])

**Procedure-related physiologic disturbance:** The lower one-minute Apgar score in the Kattwinkel trial illustrates that immediate intubation and treatment can transiently interfere with delivery-room stabilization, although no persistent five-minute difference or associated asphyxia complications were reported. ([PubMed][5])

**Selective CPAP is not risk-free:** In COIN, pneumothorax was more frequent in the CPAP arm. A selective strategy therefore depends on close assessment and timely escalation rather than continuing CPAP despite worsening disease. ([PubMed][10])

## Long-term outcomes

Follow-up of children from the Kendig trial found fewer reported abnormal pulmonary histories in the prophylaxis group, but formal pulmonary function was similar. Cognitive, neurologic, motor, behavioral, and educational outcomes did not differ significantly at school age. ([PubMed][12])

A one-year follow-up of survivors from the Merritt trial found similar growth, neurologic, and neurosensory outcomes among prophylactic, rescue, and placebo groups. ([PubMed][13])

## What matters most for an individual baby

The most relevant questions for the NICU team are whether the baby is breathing spontaneously, whether intubation is already required for resuscitation, gestational age, antenatal steroid exposure, oxygen requirement on CPAP, blood-gas findings, and whether the unit can administer surfactant through a less-invasive catheter.

For an infant already intubated, giving surfactant promptly does not impose an additional intubation merely for prophylaxis. For a stable, spontaneously breathing infant, the main tradeoff is between possibly preventing deterioration and subjecting the infant to an invasive procedure that might never have been needed.
