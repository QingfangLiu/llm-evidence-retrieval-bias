## Clinical takeaway

The direct primary-study evidence changes substantially by treatment era:

**Older trials**, in which prophylaxis usually meant routine delivery-room intubation and selective surfactant was sometimes withheld until relatively severe RDS developed, found that prophylaxis improved early oxygenation and reduced RDS severity. Some reported fewer air leaks or lower mortality, particularly among the most immature infants. Results were not uniform, however.

**Contemporary trials**, in which spontaneously breathing infants received immediate CPAP and surfactant was administered selectively for prespecified respiratory deterioration, have not demonstrated a clinically important advantage from routine prophylactic surfactant. CPAP/selective strategies avoided intubation and surfactant exposure in many infants without increasing death, BPD, or neurodevelopmental impairment. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][1])

This is a qualitative synthesis of individual studies—not a pooled analysis.

## How the individual trials compare

I considered surfactant **prophylactic** when administered before clinical RDS criteria were met, generally immediately after birth. I excluded trials comparing two rescue thresholds after RDS was already present and trials comparing prophylaxis only with placebo/no subsequent surfactant.

### Earlier intubation-and-ventilation era

**Dunn et al., 1991 — infants <30 weeks**

This randomized trial allocated 182 infants to air/placebo at birth, surfactant immediately after birth, or selective surfactant during the first six hours. Prophylaxis improved the PaO₂/FiO₂ relationship during the first 12 hours and reduced the need for subsequent rescue surfactant. There were no clear differences in air leak, intracranial hemorrhage, infection, or other major complications. ([EBSCO OpenURL][2])

**Kendig et al., 1991 — infants <30 weeks**

Among 479 infants, survival to discharge was 88% with immediate prophylaxis and 80% with rescue therapy. The apparent benefit was concentrated among infants ≤26 weeks: survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax occurred in 7% versus 18%. Only 58% of the rescue group ultimately received surfactant, so this was partly a comparison of universal treatment with treatment restricted to infants developing sufficiently severe RDS. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])

**Merritt et al., 1991 — infants 24–29 weeks**

In this placebo-controlled trial, rescue surfactant was given relatively late—at a mean age of approximately 220 minutes and only after substantial oxygen and ventilatory requirements developed. In the intention-to-treat prophylaxis-versus-rescue comparison, prophylaxis did not produce a significant overall clinical advantage. Surfactant improved oxygenation compared with placebo and reduced pulmonary interstitial emphysema, but these findings did not establish superiority of prophylaxis over rescue use. ([PubMed][4])

**Egberts et al., 1993 — infants <30 weeks**

Seventy-five infants received prophylactic poractant alfa and 72 were assigned to selective rescue, which required mechanical ventilation and an FiO₂ of at least 0.60. Prophylaxis produced better oxygenation at six hours and less severe radiographic and clinical RDS. Severe RDS occurred in 19% versus 32%. Pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, cerebral injury, duration of ventilation, oxygen exposure, and mortality were not clearly different. ([PubMed][5])

**Kattwinkel et al., 1993 — infants 29–32 weeks**

This large randomized trial analyzed 1,248 infants. Prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS, repeat surfactant dosing, and mechanical ventilation or oxygen exposure during the first four days. Death or oxygen requirement at 28 days occurred in 5% with prophylaxis and 9% with early treatment. The prophylaxis group had lower one-minute Apgar scores, although five-minute scores and measures of asphyxia were not different. In the selective group, only 43% received surfactant. ([PubMed][6])

**Walti et al., 1995 — infants 25–31 weeks**

Prophylaxis reduced oxygen and ventilator requirements during the first 72 hours. Survival without BPD was 60% versus 46%, but the confidence interval included no difference. Severe periventricular/intraventricular hemorrhage and ROP were less frequent in the prophylaxis group, but these were secondary outcomes with small event numbers and should be interpreted cautiously. ([EBSCO OpenURL][7])

**Bevilacqua et al., 1996 — infants 24–30 weeks**

In this multicenter randomized study of 287 infants, prophylactic poractant alfa reduced severe grade 3–4 RDS, peak oxygen requirements, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, and mortality. Mortality was reported as 21% with prophylaxis and 35% with selective treatment. As in other older trials, the selective-treatment protocol permitted significant RDS to develop before treatment, limiting direct applicability to modern CPAP-based early rescue practice. ([Publicatt][8])

### Contemporary CPAP era

**SUPPORT Study Group, 2010 — infants 24⁰⁄₇–27⁶⁄₇ weeks**

A total of 1,316 infants were randomized to delivery-room CPAP with selective surfactant or routine intubation and surfactant within one hour. Death or BPD occurred in 47.8% with CPAP/selective treatment and 51.0% with prophylactic surfactant, a nonsignificant difference. CPAP reduced intubation, postnatal corticosteroid exposure, and days of mechanical ventilation and increased survival without mechanical ventilation by day seven. Other major neonatal adverse outcomes were similar. ([New England Journal of Medicine][9])

At 18–22 months, death or neurodevelopmental impairment occurred in 27.9% of the CPAP group and 29.9% of the prophylactic-surfactant group, again without a significant difference. ([PMC][10])

**CURPAP, Sandri et al., 2010 — infants 25–28 weeks**

This trial enrolled 208 spontaneously breathing infants who had not been intubated at birth. Mechanical ventilation within five days was required in 31.4% after prophylactic surfactant followed by CPAP and 33.0% after CPAP with early selective surfactant. Mortality, major neonatal morbidities, and survival in room air at 36 weeks were essentially identical. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][1])

**Vermont Oxford Network DRM Trial, Dunn et al., 2011 — infants 26–29 weeks**

This three-arm randomized trial compared:

1. Prophylactic surfactant followed by ventilation
2. Prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation
3. Initial bubble CPAP with selective surfactant

Death or BPD was not significantly different between strategies. In the CPAP/selective group, **48% avoided intubation and mechanical ventilation and 54% never received surfactant**. The trial stopped early because of declining enrollment, so confidence intervals did not exclude moderate benefit or harm, but there was no signal favoring routine prophylaxis. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][11])

**POPART, Murphy et al., 2024 — infants <29 weeks**

This trial evaluated a distinct noninvasive prophylactic approach: oropharyngeal surfactant at birth plus CPAP versus CPAP alone with selective intratracheal surfactant. Intubation by 120 hours occurred in 63.5% versus 64.8%, with no differences in mechanical ventilation, intratracheal surfactant use, BPD, chronic lung disease, or mortality. Pneumothorax was more frequent with prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant: 16.7% versus 6.4%. This safety signal is specific to the oropharyngeal intervention and should not automatically be generalized to conventional endotracheal prophylaxis. ([JAMA Network][12])

## Benefits suggested by the primary studies

Prophylaxis can produce **faster improvement in early oxygenation** and reduce the clinical or radiographic severity of RDS. This was a consistent physiologic finding in several older trials. ([EBSCO OpenURL][2])

Some older studies also found reductions in:

* Pulmonary air leak
* Need for repeat surfactant or prolonged respiratory support
* Mortality, particularly in extremely immature subgroups

However, these benefits were most evident when selective surfactant was delayed until infants required high FiO₂ or substantial mechanical ventilation. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])

## Harms and disadvantages

The most reproducible contemporary disadvantage is **unnecessary invasive treatment**. When CPAP/selective management was used, approximately one-half of infants in the Vermont Oxford trial avoided both intubation and surfactant, without a detectable worsening of death or BPD. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][11])

Across modern studies, routine prophylaxis did not consistently reduce:

* Death
* BPD
* Mechanical ventilation
* Major prematurity-related morbidity
* Later neurodevelopmental impairment

Thus, the procedure and drug exposure generally occurred without demonstrated longer-term benefit in infants who could initially be supported noninvasively. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][1])

The POPART study also identified an increased pneumothorax rate with prophylactic **oropharyngeal** surfactant. ([JAMA Network][12])

## Applicability to present-day practice

For a **spontaneously breathing infant who can be stabilized with CPAP**, the contemporary randomized studies support CPAP with protocolized selective surfactant rather than routine prophylactic intubation and surfactant. This conclusion is an inference from the individual CPAP-era trials, not a guideline recommendation. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][1])

For an infant who **already requires intubation during resuscitation**, the contemporary CPAP trials do not directly determine whether surfactant should be administered immediately or after additional evidence of RDS. The older prophylaxis trials are more relevant to that circumstance, but their respiratory-support strategies, surfactant thresholds, and background care differ substantially from current practice.

Also, in SUPPORT and other modern studies, the randomized intervention was not surfactant timing alone: it was a broader strategy of CPAP/selective treatment versus intubation/prophylaxis. The benefits of selective treatment therefore cannot be completely separated from avoidance of routine intubation and ventilation.
