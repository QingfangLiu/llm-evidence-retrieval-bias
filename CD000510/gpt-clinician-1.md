## Bottom line

The individual trials give **different answers depending on what “selective treatment” involved**:

* In older trials, selective treatment commonly meant waiting several hours until infants had established, often relatively severe RDS while mechanically ventilated. Against that comparator, prophylactic surfactant improved early respiratory status and, in some studies, reduced air leak, oxygen dependence, or mortality.
* In later trials using delivery-room CPAP with protocolized early selective surfactant, routine prophylaxis did **not** improve death, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, or mechanical-ventilation outcomes. It did expose substantially more infants to intubation and surfactant administration.

Thus, the apparent advantage of prophylaxis in the older literature is not readily transferable to a setting where infants receive antenatal corticosteroids, immediate CPAP, and prompt selective surfactant.

## Findings from the older direct-comparison trials

**Kendig et al., 1991:** In 479 infants below 30 weeks, prophylaxis was compared with rescue surfactant given when FiO₂ reached at least 0.40 or mean airway pressure reached at least 7 cm H₂O. Survival to discharge was 88% with prophylaxis versus 80% with rescue. Among infants at or below 26 weeks, survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax occurred in 7% versus 18%. Only 58% of the rescue group ultimately received surfactant, so prophylaxis treated approximately four in ten infants who never met the rescue criteria. Prophylaxis was also associated with a lower 1-minute Apgar score, although 5-minute scores were similar. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

**Merritt et al., 1991:** This three-arm study enrolled infants of 24–29 weeks to prophylactic surfactant, delayed rescue surfactant, or air placebo. Rescue treatment occurred at a mean of about 220 minutes and required stringent criteria, including FiO₂ above 0.50 and mean airway pressure of at least 7 cm H₂O. The intention-to-treat analysis found no significant advantage of prophylaxis over rescue. Nineteen percent of infants did not develop RDS, illustrating the potential for prophylactic overtreatment. ([PubMed][2])

The 1-year follow-up of that trial found broadly similar growth, neurosensory, and neurologic outcomes across groups. However, infants assigned prophylaxis had lower mean Bayley mental and psychomotor scores than rescue-treated infants. Because this was a relatively small survivor follow-up with several comparisons, it is a concerning but not definitive long-term harm signal. ([PubMed][3])

**Egberts et al., 1993:** Among 147 infants of 26–29 weeks, prophylaxis improved oxygenation at 6 hours and reduced severe radiographic RDS from 32% to 19%. It also shortened exposure to FiO₂ above 0.40. There were no significant differences in pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, brain injury, duration of ventilation, duration of oxygen therapy, or mortality. Antenatal corticosteroid exposure appeared to diminish the incremental benefit of prophylaxis. ([PubMed][4])

**Kattwinkel et al., 1993:** Of 1,398 randomized infants at 29–32 weeks, 1,248 were analyzed. Prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS from 12% to 7%, retreatment from 9% to 5%, and death or oxygen use at 28 days from 9% to 5%. The 1-minute Apgar score was lower with prophylaxis, but there was no difference at 5 minutes or in asphyxia-related complications. Only 43% of the selective-treatment group ultimately received surfactant, meaning that most infants in that group avoided surfactant altogether. Benefits were concentrated among infants at or below approximately 30 weeks or under 1,500 g. ([PubMed][5])

**Walti et al., 1995:** In 256 infants of 25–31 weeks, prophylaxis reduced early oxygen and ventilatory requirements but did not significantly improve the primary outcome of survival without BPD: 60% versus 46%, odds ratio 1.53, 95% CI 0.90–2.61. Lower rates of severe peri-intraventricular hemorrhage and retinopathy were reported, but these were secondary findings requiring confirmation. ([EBSCO OpenURL][6])

**Bevilacqua et al., 1996:** In 287 infants of 24–30 weeks, prophylaxis reduced severe RDS, pulmonary interstitial emphysema from 14% to 7%, mortality from 35% to 21%, and a composite unfavorable outcome from 58% to 41%. Both groups could receive rescue surfactant, but rescue required clinical RDS and mechanical ventilation. ([PubMed][7])

Taken together, these older studies show plausible benefits from avoiding several hours of untreated surfactant deficiency and ventilation-related lung injury. However, their selective arms frequently used rescue thresholds—FiO₂ of 0.40–0.60, mechanical ventilation, and delays of several hours—that would now be considered relatively late treatment.

## Findings when selective treatment begins with CPAP

**CURPAP, Sandri et al., 2010:** A total of 208 spontaneously breathing infants of 25–28 weeks were assigned to prophylactic surfactant followed by CPAP or initial CPAP with early selective surfactant. Mechanical ventilation within five days occurred in 31.4% versus 33.0%, relative risk 0.95, 95% CI 0.64–1.41. Mortality, major prematurity-associated morbidities, and survival in room air at 36 weeks were also similar. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][8])

**SUPPORT, 2010:** In 1,316 infants of 24 weeks through 27 weeks 6 days, early intubation and surfactant were compared with delivery-room CPAP and protocolized selective intubation/surfactant. Death or BPD occurred in 51.0% of the early-surfactant group and 47.8% of the CPAP group, with no statistically significant difference. CPAP infants had less intubation, fewer mechanical-ventilation days, less postnatal corticosteroid treatment, and were more likely to be alive without ventilation at day 7. Other major adverse neonatal outcomes, including pneumothorax, did not differ significantly. ([New England Journal of Medicine][9])

**Dunn et al., 2011:** In 648 infants of 26 weeks through 29 weeks 6 days, three strategies were compared: prophylactic surfactant followed by mechanical ventilation, prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation to CPAP, and initial CPAP with selective surfactant. There were no statistically significant differences in death/BPD, mortality, or other major complications. In the initial-CPAP group, 48% avoided intubation and ventilation and 54% avoided surfactant. The trial stopped before reaching its planned sample size, limiting precision. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][10])

These trials indicate that when an infant can be stabilized on CPAP and monitored for early failure, prophylaxis does not confer a demonstrated mortality or BPD advantage and leads to unnecessary intubation and surfactant exposure in a substantial proportion.

## Benefits and harms suggested by the primary studies

### Potential benefits of prophylaxis

Prophylaxis can provide:

* Better oxygenation during the first hours after birth.
* Less severe radiographic or clinical RDS.
* Less exposure to high FiO₂ and ventilatory pressure.
* Possible reductions in air leak and mortality when the alternative is delayed rescue for advanced RDS.
* A potentially larger benefit in the most immature infants, particularly in older studies with limited antenatal corticosteroid exposure.

The mortality benefit was inconsistent: it was present in Kendig and Bevilacqua, but not in Merritt, Egberts, or the modern CPAP-era trials. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

### Potential harms or disadvantages

The clearest disadvantage is **overtreatment**. Depending on the study, approximately 42%–57% of selectively managed infants never required surfactant. In modern CPAP management, many also avoided intubation and mechanical ventilation. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

Classic prophylaxis generally required immediate intubation, which can delay stabilization and expose an infant who might breathe successfully on CPAP to laryngoscopy, positive-pressure ventilation, airway trauma, and invasive ventilation. Lower 1-minute Apgar scores were observed in more than one older prophylaxis trial, although differences did not persist at 5 minutes. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

There is no consistent signal that conventional intratracheal prophylaxis increases severe IVH, air leak, death, or BPD. Nevertheless, neither the older nor contemporary direct trials demonstrate a convincing long-term neurodevelopmental advantage.

## Nonintubation prophylactic routes

These interventions should not be assumed to have the same effects as intratracheal prophylaxis.

The small **SUNSET** trial tested prophylactic nebulized poractant alfa in infants of 26–31 weeks. It found no significant improvement in early lung aeration or clinical cardiorespiratory outcomes; two intervention-related adverse events were reported. ([PubMed][11])

The **POPART** trial randomized 251 infants below 29 weeks to oropharyngeal surfactant plus CPAP or CPAP alone. Intubation by 120 hours was nearly identical—63.5% versus 64.8%—but treated pneumothorax was more frequent with oropharyngeal surfactant, 16.6% versus 6.4%. This route-specific harm signal should not automatically be generalized to standard intratracheal surfactant. ([PubMed][12])

## Interpretation for clinical decision-making

At the individual-study level, prophylaxis appears most advantageous when the alternative is **late rescue after substantial oxygen and invasive-ventilation exposure**. It does not show an advantage over **immediate CPAP with close assessment and prompt selective surfactant**, and it predictably increases the number of infants intubated and treated.

Important applicability limitations include changing antenatal corticosteroid use, different gestational-age ranges, different surfactant preparations and doses, widely varying rescue thresholds, open-label designs, and the fact that modern trials compare entire respiratory-management strategies—not simply “surfactant now versus surfactant later.”
