## Clinical takeaway

The answer depends strongly on **what “selective use” means**.

In older trials, nearly all infants were intubated and rescue surfactant was often withheld until substantial oxygen and ventilator requirements developed. Under those conditions, prophylactic surfactant frequently reduced severe RDS and sometimes reduced pneumothorax, oxygen dependence, or mortality.

In trials using **delivery-room CPAP with prompt, protocol-directed selective surfactant**, routine prophylactic intubation and surfactant did **not** improve death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia. Selective strategies allowed many infants to avoid surfactant, intubation, and mechanical ventilation. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

## Randomized evidence

### Older intubation/delayed-rescue era

**Kendig et al., 1991 — 479 infants <30 weeks**

Immediate calf-lung surfactant was compared with rescue treatment several hours later when infants reached an FiO₂ of at least 0.40 or mean airway pressure of at least 7 cm H₂O.

Survival to discharge was 88% with prophylaxis versus 80% with rescue—an absolute difference of 8%, corresponding to an approximate number needed to treat of 13. Among infants ≤26 weeks, survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax occurred in 7% versus 18%. However, only 58% of infants assigned to rescue ultimately received surfactant, meaning approximately 42% would have been exposed unnecessarily under a prophylactic strategy. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

**Merritt/Hallman et al., 1991 — infants 24–29 weeks**

Infants were assigned to surfactant at birth, rescue surfactant after severe RDS criteria were met, or placebo. Rescue treatment occurred at a mean age of approximately 220 minutes and required relatively severe disease: FiO₂ >0.50 plus mean airway pressure ≥7 cm H₂O.

The intention-to-treat analysis did not demonstrate a significant overall advantage of prophylaxis over rescue therapy. About 19% of enrolled infants did not develop RDS, illustrating the overtreatment inherent in gestational-age-based prophylaxis. A one-year follow-up found no meaningful differences in growth, neurologic, neurosensory, or developmental outcomes between prophylactic and rescue groups. ([PubMed][2])

**Egberts et al., 1993 — 147 infants, 26–29 weeks**

Prophylactic poractant alfa given within ten minutes of birth was compared with rescue treatment for ventilated infants reaching FiO₂ ≥0.60 at 6–24 hours.

Prophylaxis improved early oxygenation and reduced severe RDS from 32% to 19—an absolute reduction of 13%, or an approximate number needed to treat of 8. It did not significantly reduce pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, intracranial hemorrhage, periventricular leukomalacia, duration of ventilation or oxygen, or mortality. ([PubMed][3])

**Kattwinkel et al., 1993 — 1,248 analyzed infants, 29–32 weeks**

Prophylactic calf-lung surfactant reduced moderate RDS from 12% to 7%, retreatment from 9% to 5%, and the composite of death or oxygen requirement at 28 days from 9% to 5%. Infants also required less respiratory support during the first four days.

Only 43% of selectively managed infants received surfactant, so prophylaxis treated a large number of infants who would not otherwise have needed it. The prophylaxis group had lower one-minute Apgar scores, although the difference resolved by five minutes and was not associated with subsequent asphyxia complications. ([PubMed][4])

**Walti et al., 1995 — 256 infants, 25–31 weeks**

Survival without BPD was 60% with prophylaxis and 46% with rescue, but the confidence interval included no effect. Prophylaxis was associated with less severe intraventricular hemorrhage and retinopathy of prematurity and with lower respiratory-support requirements during the first 72 hours. These secondary outcome findings should be interpreted cautiously because the principal composite outcome was not statistically significant. ([EBSCO OpenURL][5])

**Bevilacqua et al., 1996 — 287 infants, 24–30 weeks**

Prophylactic poractant alfa was compared with no delivery-room surfactant; control infants could receive rescue treatment after developing ventilated RDS.

Prophylaxis reduced severe RDS, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, mortality—21% versus 35%—and a composite unfavorable outcome—41% versus 58%. These results came from a treatment era characterized by routine intubation and substantially later rescue than would generally be used in a CPAP-based selective protocol. ([PubMed][6])

### CPAP and early-selective era

**SUPPORT trial, 2010 — 1,316 infants, 24–27 weeks**

Infants were assigned before birth to delivery-room CPAP with protocolized limited ventilation and selective surfactant, or routine intubation and surfactant within one hour.

Death or BPD occurred in 47.8% of the CPAP/selective group and 51.0% of the prophylactic-surfactant group; the difference was not significant. CPAP/selective management resulted in less intubation, less postnatal corticosteroid exposure, fewer mechanical-ventilation days, and more infants alive and free of ventilation by day seven. Other major neonatal adverse outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][7])

At 18–22 months, death or neurodevelopmental impairment occurred in 27.9% with CPAP/selective management and 29.9% with prophylactic intubation and surfactant, also not significantly different. ([New England Journal of Medicine][8])

**Sandri et al., 2010 — 208 spontaneously breathing infants, 25–28 weeks**

All infants were initially supported with nasal CPAP. One group underwent prophylactic intubation, surfactant, and rapid extubation; the other received surfactant selectively after evidence of RDS.

Mechanical ventilation during the first five days occurred in 31.4% versus 33.0%, respectively. Mortality, BPD-related outcomes, and other major morbidities were similar. Thus, adding prophylactic surfactant to initial CPAP did not produce a measurable clinical advantage. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][9])

**Vermont Oxford Network Delivery Room Management trial, 2011 — 648 infants, 26–29 weeks**

Three strategies were compared:

1. Prophylactic surfactant followed by continued mechanical ventilation
2. Prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation
3. Nasal CPAP with selective intubation and surfactant

Neither rapid-extubation prophylaxis nor CPAP/selective treatment significantly changed death or BPD compared with prophylactic surfactant and ventilation. In the CPAP/selective group, 48% avoided intubation/mechanical ventilation and 54% avoided surfactant entirely. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][10])

**POPART trial, 2024 — 251 infants <29 weeks**

This trial tested prophylactic **oropharyngeal**, rather than intratracheal, surfactant before CPAP.

Intubation by 120 hours occurred in 63.5% with oropharyngeal surfactant and 64.8% with CPAP alone. There was no reduction in mechanical ventilation, subsequent intratracheal surfactant, or chronic lung disease. Pneumothorax requiring treatment was more frequent with prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant—16.6% versus 6.4%, an approximate number needed to harm of 10. Because the route was unusual, this safety signal should not automatically be generalized to intratracheal surfactant. ([JAMA Network][11])

## Benefits and harms supported by the primary studies

### Potential benefits of prophylaxis

Prophylaxis can produce faster improvement in oxygenation and reduce progression to severe RDS, particularly when the alternative is delayed rescue after substantial oxygen and ventilator requirements have developed. Some older trials also found reductions in pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, oxygen dependence, or mortality, with the clearest mortality signal coming from studies enrolling extremely preterm infants and using comparatively late rescue thresholds. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

### Potential harms or disadvantages

The most consistent disadvantage is **overtreatment**. Depending on the trial, approximately 19% to more than 50% of selectively managed infants never required surfactant. Routine prophylaxis exposes these infants to laryngoscopy, intubation, intratracheal drug administration, positive-pressure ventilation, and associated physiologic instability without an opportunity for benefit. ([PubMed][2])

Prophylactic intubation also increases exposure to invasive respiratory management. In CPAP-era trials, avoiding prophylaxis did not increase death/BPD but reduced intubation, mechanical-ventilation exposure, postnatal steroid treatment, and overall surfactant use. ([PubMed][7])

Individual trials reported transient procedural effects such as a lower one-minute Apgar score or hypoxemia during dosing. Most randomized studies did not demonstrate consistent differences in NEC, PDA, major intracranial injury, or other neonatal complications, but many were underpowered for uncommon harms. ([PubMed][4])

## Interpretation for contemporary decision-making

The older findings do not show that surfactant should be withheld until advanced RDS. Rather, they show that **prophylaxis was preferable to delayed rescue** under invasive ventilation protocols with high treatment thresholds.

The more applicable comparison for a spontaneously breathing infant is prophylactic intubation/surfactant versus immediate CPAP with close monitoring and **early**, protocolized selective surfactant. In that comparison, the randomized trials found no convincing improvement in survival or BPD from prophylaxis, while selective management allowed a substantial proportion of infants to avoid invasive treatment. Long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes were also similar in the largest trial. ([PubMed][7])

The evidence is less definitive for the most immature infants who cannot be stabilized on noninvasive support, and there is limited completed direct evidence comparing **prophylactic less-invasive surfactant administration** with selective less-invasive administration.
