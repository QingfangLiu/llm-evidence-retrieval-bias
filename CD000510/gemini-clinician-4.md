The approach to surfactant administration in preterm infants has undergone a significant paradigm shift. Understanding the benefits and harms of prophylactic versus selective (rescue) therapy requires looking at how the baseline respiratory management of preterm infants has evolved over the past few decades.

Here is a breakdown of the evidence drawn strictly from primary clinical trials.

## The Historical Case for Prophylactic Surfactant (The "Pre-CPAP" Era)

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, before continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) and antenatal steroids were routinely used, prophylactic surfactant administration at birth was the preferred strategy. The primary trials from this era demonstrated clear benefits for immediate administration over waiting for clinical signs of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) to develop.

* **Improved Gas Exchange & Mechanics:** Giving surfactant at birth immediately reduced early oxygen and ventilatory requirements during the first week of life compared to late treatment (Dunn et al., 1991).
* **Reduced Complications:** The prophylactic strategy consistently lowered the incidence of severe pulmonary air leaks (like pneumothorax) and severe chronic lung disease (Dunn et al., 1991; Kendig et al., 1991).
* **Lower Mortality:** In several landmark trials, infants treated prophylactically had less severe RDS and a significantly lower overall neonatal mortality rate than those who received rescue treatment (Egberts et al., 1993; Kendig et al., 1991).

## The Modern Paradigm: Harms of Prophylaxis and the Shift to Selective Rescue

Today, because most high-risk mothers receive antenatal steroids and neonatologists routinely stabilize premature infants in the delivery room with early nasal CPAP, the risk-benefit calculation has flipped.

* **The Harm of Unnecessary Intubation:** The fundamental drawback of the prophylactic strategy is that it requires intubating the infant immediately at birth. Because many extremely premature infants can now be successfully managed with non-invasive CPAP alone, prophylactic protocols inevitably expose a subset of infants to unnecessary endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation (Chun et al., 2017). This airway trauma and early volutrauma can trigger the inflammatory cascades that lead to bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).
* **Loss of Prophylactic Advantage:** Recent primary data confirm that when comparing prophylactic surfactant to an early selective rescue strategy (where infants are placed on CPAP and given surfactant only if their oxygen requirements rise), there is no longer any difference in the duration of mechanical ventilation, the incidence of BPD, or neonatal mortality (Chun et al., 2017).
* **Alternative Delivery Methods Haven't Solved This:** Even when clinicians attempt to provide prophylactic surfactant non-invasively—such as instilling it into the oropharynx at birth alongside CPAP to avoid endotracheal intubation—it does not reduce the rate of subsequent intubation for respiratory failure compared to using CPAP alone (Murphy et al., 2024).

Ultimately, the primary literature demonstrates that while prophylactic surfactant was highly beneficial in the era of primary mechanical ventilation, modern non-invasive stabilization techniques have made early, selective rescue the safer strategy to prevent unnecessary airway trauma.
