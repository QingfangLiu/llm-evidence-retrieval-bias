As an evidence-synthesis researcher, you are examining a critical paradigm shift in neonatology. The management of preterm infants at risk of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) has largely transitioned from routine prophylactic surfactant to early stabilization with non-invasive ventilation and selective surfactant therapy.

## Prophylactic vs. Selective Strategies

* **Prophylactic surfactant administration** involves intubating high-risk infants (e.g., <30 weeks gestation) and administering surfactant immediately after birth, prior to the onset of RDS symptoms.
* **Selective (rescue) surfactant administration** involves initially stabilizing the infant—often with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)—and only intubating and administering surfactant if the infant meets clinical or radiographic criteria for RDS.

## Benefits and Harms

### Harms of Prophylactic Administration

* **Overtreatment and Intubation Trauma:** The primary harm of a prophylactic strategy is that it mandates elective endotracheal intubation for all extremely preterm infants at delivery. This exposes them to the immediate physical trauma of intubation and the high risk of ventilator-induced lung injury (barotrauma and volutrauma). Modern primary studies demonstrate that prophylactic protocols significantly increase both intubation rates and the total number of surfactant doses administered, often overtreating infants who may have never developed severe RDS (Chun et al., 2017).
* **Lack of Superiority Over Modern Selective Protocols:** Historically, prophylactic surfactant reduced mortality and pneumothorax rates. However, with the widespread adoption of maternal antenatal corticosteroids and early delivery-room CPAP, the prophylactic approach no longer offers a clear survival advantage. Recent prospective comparisons show no difference in the duration of mechanical ventilation, the incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), or the combined risk of BPD or death between prophylactic and early selective approaches (Chun et al., 2017).

### Potential Benefits and Long-Term Outcomes

* **Historical Pulmonary Advantages:** Early primary trials conducted before the routine use of CPAP suggested some long-term benefits to prophylactic administration. A school-age follow-up of a randomized trial found that infants who received prophylactic surfactant had fewer clinical pulmonary complications in childhood compared to those who received rescue therapy (Sinkin et al., 1998).
* **Neurodevelopmental Equivalence:** Despite the initial differences in short-term respiratory management, studies evaluating long-term cognitive outcomes reveal that the timing of surfactant administration (prophylactic vs. rescue) does not significantly alter formal neurodevelopmental outcomes, intelligence scores, or special education needs at school age (Sinkin et al., 1998).

Currently, the clinical consensus has moved away from prophylactic administration because the harms of unnecessary intubation and invasive ventilation outweigh the historical benefits when early CPAP and early rescue surfactant can be effectively utilized.
