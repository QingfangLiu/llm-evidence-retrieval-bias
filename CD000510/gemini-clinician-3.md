It is a difficult clinical balance to strike—weighing the clear physiological benefits of opening the premature lung early against the risk of unnecessarily intubating and medicating an infant who might have transitioned fine on their own.

To answer your question directly based on primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prophylactic administration (giving surfactant immediately in the delivery room) generally yields better respiratory outcomes and survival for the most immature infants, but it comes at the steep cost of overtreating a significant percentage of babies.

Here is a breakdown of the specific benefits and harms demonstrated in the foundational primary trials.

## Benefits of Prophylactic Surfactant

The original RCTs that established surfactant as a standard of care strongly favored prophylaxis over waiting for established respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) to develop, particularly for the most premature infants.

* **Improved Survival:** Immediate prophylaxis significantly increases the proportion of infants who survive to hospital discharge. A landmark multicenter trial found this survival benefit was most pronounced in extremely premature infants (≤26 weeks' gestation), where survival was 75% in the prophylaxis group versus 54% in the rescue group (Kendig et al., 1991).
* **Decreased Barotrauma (Air Leaks):** Because the lung epithelium of a very premature infant can be damaged within minutes of mechanical ventilation, giving surfactant *before* those first pressurized breaths reduces shear stress. Infants receiving prophylactic surfactant have significantly lower rates of pneumothorax compared to those receiving late rescue therapy (Dunn et al., 1991; Kendig et al., 1991).
* **Improved Early Gas Exchange:** Infants treated prophylactically exhibit a significant improvement in gas exchange during their first week of life. This translates to lower supplemental oxygen requirements and better ventilation indices compared to infants whose treatment is delayed (Dunn et al., 1991).
* **Fewer Long-Term Pulmonary Complications:** The benefits of immediate prophylaxis appear to extend into childhood. When survivors of the Kendig cohort were evaluated at school age (4.5 to 8 years old), those who had received prophylactic surfactant had a lower incidence of abnormal clinical pulmonary histories compared to those who received rescue treatment (Sinkin et al., 1998).

## Harms and Disadvantages of Prophylactic Use

While the physiological benefits of giving surfactant early are clear, a purely prophylactic approach carries distinct clinical drawbacks.

* **Significant Overtreatment:** This is the most substantial argument against universal prophylaxis. When surfactant is administered to all infants below a certain gestational age, many babies receive a drug they do not need. In one major trial, 48% of the infants randomized to the late/rescue treatment group avoided surfactant entirely because their endogenous surfactant production proved sufficient (Dunn et al., 1991).
* **Delivery Room Destabilization:** Administering prophylactic surfactant requires endotracheal intubation on the resuscitation table. This can transiently destabilize a newly born infant and commits them—at least briefly—to mechanical ventilation, which carries its own risk of lung injury.
* **Resource Utilization:** Universal prophylaxis consumes expensive medication and intensive care resources for a subset of infants who might have been successfully managed with non-invasive support alone.
