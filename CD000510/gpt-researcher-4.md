## Scope

I treated **prophylactic surfactant** as surfactant given routinely at or shortly after birth, before an infant met diagnostic or treatment criteria for RDS. **Selective use** means surfactant was withheld unless respiratory distress, oxygen need, radiographic findings, CPAP failure, or ventilation criteria developed.

I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee statements, editorials, and studies comparing earlier versus later treatment only after RDS was already present.

## Overall interpretation

The results are strongly dependent on the respiratory-care era:

* In older trials, selective surfactant was commonly delayed until infants were mechanically ventilated with an FiO₂ of approximately 0.40–0.60 or substantial airway-pressure requirements. Several trials found that prophylaxis reduced severe RDS, early oxygen and ventilation requirements, air leaks, or mortality. Other trials found physiological improvement without a significant mortality or BPD advantage.
* In trials incorporating delivery-room CPAP and relatively early selective surfactant, prophylactic intubation and surfactant did **not** improve death, BPD, mechanical-ventilation requirement, or neurodevelopment. Selective strategies allowed a substantial proportion of infants to avoid both intubation and surfactant.
* Consequently, the older estimates of benefit are not directly transportable to a contemporary strategy of CPAP stabilization with prompt selective surfactant.

## Individual randomized studies

### Older ventilation-era trials

**Dunn et al., 1991 — 182 infants under 30 weeks**

Infants were randomized before delivery to sham treatment, surfactant at birth, or selective surfactant before 6 hours. In the selective group, 29 of 60 infants—48%—were judged surfactant-sufficient and received no surfactant. Both surfactant strategies improved gas exchange and reduced air leak and severe chronic lung disease compared with the sham group, but prophylactic and selective surfactant did not differ on these outcomes. Mild chronic lung disease was more frequent with prophylaxis than with selective treatment. ([ScienceOpen][1])

**Kendig et al., 1991 — 479 infants under 30 weeks**

Surfactant was given at delivery or selectively when FiO₂ reached at least 0.40 and/or mean airway pressure reached at least 7 cm H₂O. Survival to discharge was 88% with prophylaxis and 80% with rescue treatment. The difference was concentrated among infants of 26 weeks or less—75% versus 54% survival—with pneumothorax occurring in 7% versus 18% in that gestational-age subgroup. The subgroup findings were not based on stratified randomization and therefore warrant more caution than the overall comparison. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

**Merritt et al., 1991 — 246 treated infants, 24–29 weeks**

Singletons were assigned to placebo, prophylactic human surfactant, or rescue surfactant; multiples were assigned to prophylaxis or rescue. Rescue was given at a mean of 220 minutes under relatively severe treatment criteria. Intention-to-treat analyses found no significant advantage of prophylaxis over rescue for mortality or major morbidity. Nineteen percent of enrolled infants did not develop RDS, illustrating overtreatment under a prophylactic policy. Transient oxygen desaturation to approximately 60% was reported in 1.5% of rescue-treated infants. ([ScienceDirect][3])

At 1 year, growth, neurosensory findings, and neurological outcomes were similar among prophylaxis, rescue, and placebo groups. ([PubMed][4])

**Egberts et al., 1993 — 147 infants, 26–29 weeks**

Prophylactic poractant alfa within 10 minutes improved oxygenation and reduced severe radiographic RDS—19% versus 32%. Fewer infants met criteria for an additional rescue dose, 11% versus 32%. There were no significant differences in mortality, pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, cerebral injury, PDA, duration of ventilation, or duration of oxygen treatment. ([PubMed][5])

**Kattwinkel et al., 1993 — 1,248 analyzed infants, 29–32 weeks**

Compared with waiting until mild RDS developed, prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS from 12% to 7%, retreatment from 9% to 5%, and death or oxygen use at 28 days from 9% to 5%. Effects were concentrated among smaller and less-mature infants. In the selective group, only 43% received surfactant and approximately 60% were intubated, demonstrating the substantial additional treatment exposure caused by prophylaxis. ([PubMed][6])

**Walti et al., 1995 — 256 infants, 25–31 weeks**

Prophylaxis reduced early oxygen and ventilatory requirements. The primary outcome, survival without BPD, was 60% versus 46%, but its confidence interval included no effect: OR 1.53, 95% CI 0.90–2.61. Severe peri-intraventricular hemorrhage and ROP were less frequent in the prophylaxis group, but these secondary findings were based on small numbers and were identified by the investigators as needing confirmation. ([EBSCO OpenURL][7])

**Bevilacqua et al., 1996 — 287 infants, 24–30 weeks**

Infants received prophylactic poractant alfa or no delivery-room surfactant; both groups could receive rescue surfactant after RDS developed and mechanical ventilation was required. Prophylaxis reduced severe RDS by 32%, pulmonary interstitial emphysema from 14% to 7%, mortality from 35% to 21%, and a combined adverse-outcome measure from 58% to 41%. This was one of the clearest older trials favoring prophylaxis, but its rescue strategy required established RDS and mechanical ventilation. ([PubMed][8])

**Bevilacqua et al., 1997 — 93 infants, 26–30 weeks**

This Italian-Bulgarian-Romanian trial stopped early because of logistical difficulties. Prophylaxis improved early oxygenation and reduced subsequent rescue treatment, but did not produce statistically significant differences in mortality, severe RDS, air leak, IVH, or infection. Because the original report is poorly indexed and not readily available electronically, I would obtain the full paper before extracting numerical outcome data for a formal review.

### CPAP-era randomized trials

**Sandri et al., 2010 — 208 infants, 25–28 weeks**

Spontaneously breathing infants not intubated at birth were assigned to prophylactic surfactant followed by CPAP or initial CPAP with early selective surfactant. Mechanical ventilation within five days occurred in 31.4% versus 33.0%, RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.64–1.41. Mortality, survival without oxygen at 36 weeks, and major neonatal morbidities were similar. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][9])

**SUPPORT Study Group, 2010 — 1,316 infants, 24–27 weeks**

Delivery-room CPAP with protocol-directed selective ventilation was compared with intubation and surfactant within one hour. Death or BPD occurred in 47.8% with CPAP/selective treatment and 51.0% with prophylactic surfactant, adjusted RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.85–1.05. CPAP-treated infants were less frequently intubated, received fewer postnatal corticosteroids, spent fewer days mechanically ventilated, and were more likely to be alive without ventilation at day 7; other major neonatal adverse outcomes did not differ. ([PubMed][10])

At 18–22 months, death or neurodevelopmental impairment occurred in 27.9% versus 29.9%, RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.78–1.10. ([PMC][11])

**Dunn et al., 2011 — 648 infants, 26–29 weeks**

Three strategies were compared: prophylactic surfactant followed by ventilation, prophylactic surfactant with rapid extubation to CPAP, and initial CPAP with selective surfactant. Relative to prophylactic surfactant plus ventilation, the RR for death or BPD was 0.78, 95% CI 0.59–1.03, with rapid extubation and 0.83, 95% CI 0.64–1.09, with initial CPAP. Mortality and major prematurity complications did not differ significantly. In the initial-CPAP group, 48% avoided intubation and ventilation and 54% avoided surfactant. The trial ended before its planned sample size because enrollment declined. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][12])

### Different prophylactic route

**Murphy et al., POPART, 2024 — 251 infants under 29 weeks**

This trial evaluated oropharyngeal rather than intratracheal prophylaxis. Oropharyngeal poractant alfa plus CPAP did not reduce intubation within 120 hours compared with CPAP alone: 63.5% versus 64.8%, RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.81–1.18. Pneumothorax was more frequent after prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant, 16.6% versus 6.4%. Because deposition and dosing differ, these results should not be pooled uncritically with endotracheal prophylaxis trials. ([PubMed][13])

## Benefits suggested by the primary studies

The most reproducible benefit of prophylaxis in the older studies was **better early pulmonary physiology**: improved oxygenation, less severe radiographic RDS, and lower initial ventilator requirements. ([PubMed][5])

Mortality and air-leak benefits were **inconsistent between individual trials**. Kendig and Bevilacqua reported significant survival advantages, particularly in the least-mature infants, whereas Dunn, Merritt, Egberts, and Walti did not demonstrate a clear prophylaxis-versus-selective mortality advantage. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

In contemporary CPAP-era trials, the direct evidence did not demonstrate improved death/BPD, ventilation, major morbidity, or early childhood neurodevelopment with routine prophylactic endotracheal surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][12])

## Harms and burdens suggested by the primary studies

The most consistent disadvantage is **overtreatment and exposure to intubation**. Depending on the study population, approximately 19%–54% of infants managed selectively avoided surfactant, and many avoided intubation altogether. ([PubMed][14])

Directly reported adverse signals included:

* More mild chronic lung disease with prophylaxis in the Dunn 1991 trial, despite no difference in severe chronic lung disease between prophylactic and delayed surfactant. ([ScienceOpen][1])
* Transient marked desaturation during surfactant administration in the Merritt trial. ([ScienceDirect][3])
* No consistent increase or decrease in IVH, PDA, NEC, infection, pneumothorax, or other major neonatal complications in the CPAP-era endotracheal trials. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][12])
* Increased pneumothorax with prophylactic **oropharyngeal** surfactant in POPART. ([PubMed][13])

## Nonrandomized comparative primary studies

These studies may be useful for contextual or sensitivity analyses but are vulnerable to confounding by indication, historical-era changes, and differences in respiratory practice.

* **Bevilacqua et al., 2003, OBSERVES:** retrospective multicenter cohort of 965 treated infants; prophylaxis was associated with less severe RDS and pulmonary interstitial emphysema, but mortality and most complications were similar. The prophylaxis group was less mature and smaller. ([PubMed][15])
* **Koch et al., 2010:** retrospective historical-control study of 65 infants at 23–26 weeks; prophylaxis was associated with fewer mechanical-ventilation hours, while differences in air leak and chronic lung disease were not statistically significant. ([Wiley Online Library][16])
* **Kim et al., 2014:** nationwide Korean before-and-after cohort of 2,540 infants; mortality was 19.9% after introduction of prophylaxis versus 22.5% in the preceding selective-treatment year. The design cannot separate the surfactant policy from contemporaneous changes in care. ([PubMed][17])
* **Feng et al., 2016:** prospective but nonrandomized Chinese study; prophylaxis improved respiratory measures and reduced severe RDS, BPD, PDA, and ventilation duration, without a mortality difference. Forty-seven of 254 enrolled infants were excluded from analysis, and treatment was not randomly allocated. ([ScienceDirect][18])
* **Chun et al., 2017:** retrospective-versus-prospective era comparison of 384 infants; prophylaxis increased intubation, surfactant exposure, and repeat dosing without improving mechanical-ventilation duration, death/BPD, or other major outcomes. ([PubMed][19])
