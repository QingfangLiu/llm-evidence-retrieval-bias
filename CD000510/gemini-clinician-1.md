The clinical debate between prophylactic (immediate) surfactant administration and selective (rescue) treatment centers on balancing the protective benefits of early intervention against the risks of overtreating infants who might never develop respiratory distress syndrome (RDS).

Based on primary randomized controlled trials, here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms of each approach.

## Benefits of Prophylactic Administration

* **Improved Survival in Extreme Prematurity:** Prophylactic surfactant has been shown to significantly improve survival rates compared to rescue therapy, particularly for the youngest infants. A pivotal multicenter trial demonstrated that in infants born at 26 weeks' gestation or earlier, survival to discharge was 75% in the prophylaxis group compared to 54% in the rescue group (Kendig et al., 1991).
* **Reduced Risk of Air Leaks (Pneumothorax):** Instilling surfactant into fluid-filled lungs before the first mechanical breath distributes the surfactant more uniformly, which helps prevent alveolar rupture. Prophylactic administration reduced the incidence of pneumothorax from 18% to 7% in extremely premature infants compared to delayed rescue treatment (Kendig et al., 1991).
* **Immediate Improvement in Gas Exchange:** Providing surfactant immediately at birth drastically lowers initial ventilatory and oxygen requirements during the critical first week of life compared to waiting for RDS to manifest (Dunn et al., 1991).

## Harms and Disadvantages of Prophylaxis

* **Significant Overtreatment:** The most glaring drawback of the prophylactic approach is that it requires routinely intubating every high-risk infant in the delivery room. In a trial comparing immediate prophylaxis against a delayed selective approach, nearly half (48%) of the infants in the delayed group never required surfactant at all because their lungs were sufficiently mature (Dunn et al., 1991).
* **Increased Risk of Mild Chronic Lung Disease:** By intubating and treating infants who might have otherwise avoided the ventilator, prophylaxis can inadvertently increase the risk of mild lung injury. Dunn et al. (1991) noted that the early-treatment group actually experienced a significantly higher incidence of mild chronic lung disease (defined as needing respiratory support beyond 28 days of age) than the selective-treatment group, who were extubated earlier and had shorter stays in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).
* **Interference with Immediate Resuscitation:** Administering a fluid bolus directly into the endotracheal tube immediately at birth can briefly complicate airway stabilization. While trials note this can cause transiently lower 1-minute Apgar scores, the scores typically normalize by the 5-minute mark (Dunn et al., 1991).

## Summary Comparison

| Approach | Primary Advantage | Primary Drawback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Prophylactic (Immediate)** | Maximizes lung protection before barotrauma occurs; higher survival in extreme prematurity (≤26 weeks). | Mandates delivery-room intubation for all; exposes surfactant-sufficient infants to unnecessary mechanical ventilation. |
| **Selective (Rescue)** | Spares roughly half of preterm infants from unnecessary intubation and surfactant administration. | Delays therapy until lung injury (RDS) has already begun; higher risk of pneumothorax in the youngest neonates. |
