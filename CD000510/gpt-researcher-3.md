# Scope

I searched for **comparative primary studies** in preterm infants at risk of RDS in which:

* **Prophylactic surfactant** was administered at or shortly after birth, before established RDS; and
* The comparator used **selective surfactant**, triggered by clinical, oxygen, CPAP, radiographic, or ventilation criteria.

The search was updated through **July 25, 2026**. I excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions, protocols without results, placebo/no-surfactant trials in which rescue treatment was unavailable, and studies comparing earlier versus later treatment after RDS was already established.

The studies fall into two clinically different eras:

1. Older trials comparing prophylaxis with relatively **delayed rescue**, often after intubation and high oxygen or airway-pressure thresholds.
2. Modern trials comparing prophylaxis with **early CPAP plus selective surfactant**, sometimes delivered by LISA.

Because the comparator changed substantially, results from the two eras should not be treated as one homogeneous treatment contrast.

### Interpretation of the historical trials

Several older trials found clinically important prophylactic benefits, especially among the most immature infants. However, prophylaxis was often being compared with rescue delayed until infants required intubation, FiO₂ of 0.40–0.60, or substantial airway pressure. Thus, these trials partly address **immediate surfactant versus delayed treatment of relatively advanced RDS**, not immediate surfactant versus contemporary CPAP-supported surveillance and early selective LISA.

## Nonrandomized comparative primary studies

These studies are useful for implementation or hypothesis generation but are substantially more vulnerable to confounding by gestational age, illness severity, epoch, antenatal-steroid use, and changes in respiratory practice.

| Study                                | Design and main findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bevilacqua et al./OBSERVES, 2003** | Retrospective multicenter comparison of 244 prophylactically treated and 721 early-rescue infants. Severe RDS was less frequent with prophylaxis, but mortality and most other complications were similar. Treatment groups differed in baseline maturity and risk. ([PubMed][14])                                                                      |
| **Koch et al., 2010**                | Retrospective comparison in infants at 23–26 weeks. Prophylactic treatment was associated with fewer hours of mechanical ventilation, but the groups were small—31 versus 34 infants—and most morbidity differences were not significant. ([PubMed][15])                                                                                                |
| **Kim et al., 2014**                 | National Korean before-and-after comparison across 53 NICUs: 1,291 infants during a selective-treatment period and 1,249 after introduction of prophylaxis. Mortality and several complications were lower in the later prophylaxis period, but temporal changes in care prevent confident causal attribution. ([PubMed][16])                           |
| **Kong et al., 2016**                | Prospective but nonrandomized Chinese study of 116 prophylactically treated and 91 selectively treated infants under 32 weeks. Prophylaxis was associated with less severe RDS, BPD, PDA, mechanical ventilation, and oxygen treatment; mortality was similar. Baseline imbalance and post-enrollment exclusions limit inference. ([ScienceDirect][17]) |
| **Chun et al., 2017**                | Historical-period comparison of 193 selective-treatment and 191 prophylaxis-period infants. Prophylaxis increased intubation, surfactant exposure, and repeat dosing without reducing ventilation duration, death/BPD, or other major adverse outcomes. ([PubMed][18])                                                                                  |

# Benefit–harm conclusions from the individual studies

**Mortality:** Mortality benefits were reported in some older studies, particularly Kendig and Bevilacqua, in which rescue was delayed until relatively severe respiratory failure. In contemporary CPAP-based trials, routine prophylactic intubation and surfactant have not demonstrated a mortality advantage. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

**RDS and early respiratory deterioration:** Prophylaxis consistently improves very early oxygenation and can reduce severe RDS when selective treatment is delayed. Against contemporary CPAP and prompt rescue, the advantage is much less consistent. The CaLI trial provides a notable positive signal for prophylactic LISA, but Sandri, SUPPORT, the Vermont Oxford Network trial, POPART, and the 2026 DR-LISA pilot did not show a clear primary-outcome benefit. ([PubMed][4])

**BPD:** No consistent reduction is evident across individual modern studies. CaLI reported less BPD, whereas larger or similarly relevant CPAP-era trials found no significant prophylaxis advantage. Definitions and competing mortality differ between studies, making cross-trial numerical comparisons problematic. ([PubMed][11])

**Air leak:** Some historical studies reported fewer pneumothoraces or less pulmonary interstitial emphysema with prophylaxis. The small 2026 DR-LISA trial also reported fewer pneumothoraces. Conversely, prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant in POPART increased pneumothorax, emphasizing that delivery method matters. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

**Unnecessary treatment and procedural harm:** Selective strategies allow a substantial proportion of infants to avoid surfactant and airway instrumentation. In Kattwinkel, only 43% of selectively managed infants required surfactant; in the Vermont Oxford Network trial, 54% of the CPAP/selective group avoided it. Even LISA can cause bradycardia, desaturation, repeated catheterization attempts, and treatment of infants who would otherwise stabilize without surfactant. ([PubMed][5])

**Long-term outcomes:** Available randomized follow-up reports have not demonstrated a statistically significant neurodevelopmental advantage of prophylaxis. This applies to the Merritt cohort at 12 months, SUPPORT at 18–22 months, and CaLI at approximately two years, although confidence intervals remain compatible with modest benefit or harm in the smaller studies. ([ScienceDirect][19])

**Overall:** The individual studies suggest that prophylactic surfactant was beneficial relative to **late rescue after advanced RDS**, particularly in extremely immature infants. That historical finding does not translate directly to current care. When the alternative is effective delivery-room CPAP with close monitoring and prompt selective surfactant, routine prophylactic intubation does not appear beneficial and exposes more infants to treatment. Whether **prophylactic LISA without intubation** improves important outcomes remains unresolved: CaLI is encouraging, while other route- and timing-specific trials have been neutral or too small for definitive conclusions.
