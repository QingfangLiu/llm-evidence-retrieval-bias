## Bottom line

My synthesis of the **individual primary studies**, without pooling their results, is:

**Older trials often favored prophylactic surfactant**, especially among extremely preterm infants. In those studies, however, “selective” treatment was frequently delayed until an infant needed substantial oxygen or mechanical ventilation. Antenatal corticosteroids and delivery-room CPAP were also less consistently used than they are today. Under those conditions, prophylaxis reduced severe RDS, air leak, and—in some trials—mortality. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

**In trials reflecting more contemporary care, routine prophylactic intubation and surfactant did not improve death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia compared with starting CPAP and giving surfactant selectively.** CPAP-first strategies allowed many infants to avoid intubation, mechanical ventilation, and surfactant altogether, without evidence of worse neurodevelopment at 18–22 months. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][2])

This does not mean surfactant is unhelpful. It means the evidence increasingly supports **early surfactant for infants who show that they need it**, rather than intubating every at-risk infant preventively—particularly when a baby is breathing spontaneously and can be stabilized on CPAP.

### Trials incorporating early CPAP and contemporary stabilization

**Sandri and colleagues, 2010:** Among 208 spontaneously breathing infants at 25–28 weeks, prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation was compared with nasal CPAP and early selective surfactant. Mechanical ventilation by day 5 occurred in 31.4% versus 33.0%; there were no significant differences in mortality, chronic lung disease, or major neonatal complications. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][2])

**SUPPORT trial, 2010:** In 1,316 infants born at 24–27 weeks, early CPAP with selective surfactant was compared with routine intubation and surfactant. Death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia occurred in 47.8% of the CPAP group and 51.0% of the surfactant group—not a statistically significant difference. CPAP infants were intubated less often, received fewer postnatal corticosteroids, and spent fewer days mechanically ventilated. Other major neonatal adverse outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][9])

**Dunn and colleagues, 2011:** In 648 infants born at 26–29 weeks, three strategies were compared: prophylactic surfactant with continued ventilation, prophylactic surfactant with rapid extubation, and CPAP with selective surfactant. Neither alternative significantly changed death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia compared with routine prophylactic surfactant. In the CPAP group, 48% avoided intubation/mechanical ventilation and 54% avoided surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][10])

**SUPPORT neurodevelopmental follow-up, 2012:** Among children with follow-up information at 18–22 months, death or neurodevelopmental impairment occurred in 27.9% after the CPAP-first strategy and 29.9% after routine intubation/surfactant. This was not a statistically significant difference. ([PMC][11])

**Kong and colleagues, 2016:** This prospective multicenter controlled study in China reported shorter respiratory support and less severe RDS and bronchopulmonary dysplasia with prophylaxis, but mortality was similar. Because treatment was not clearly randomized and local respiratory practices may have differed from contemporary CPAP-first protocols, this provides less certain evidence than the randomized trials. ([PubMed][12])

### Newer attempts to provide prophylaxis without routine intubation

**SUNSET trial, 2023:** Thirty-five infants born at 26–31 weeks received nebulized surfactant or no nebulized surfactant while being supported noninvasively. Prophylaxis did not significantly improve the primary measure of lung aeration or the reported clinical respiratory outcomes. Two adverse events occurred in the intervention group. The trial was small and primarily designed to study feasibility and lung aeration. ([PubMed][13])

**POPART trial, 2024:** In 251 infants born before 29 weeks, prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant did not reduce intubation by 120 hours—63.5% versus 64.8%. Pneumothorax was more frequent with prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant, 16.7% versus 6.4%, while bronchopulmonary dysplasia and mortality were not improved. ([JAMA Network][14])

## Potential benefits of prophylaxis

The older studies suggest that prophylaxis can:

* Establish surfactant in the lungs before substantial injury from severe RDS or mechanical ventilation occurs.
* Improve early oxygenation and reduce the severity of RDS.
* Reduce air-leak complications in some very immature populations.
* Improve survival when the alternative is relatively late rescue treatment. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

These benefits are most relevant when selective surfactant would otherwise be delayed until the infant has marked oxygen or ventilator requirements.

## Potential harms and trade-offs

The clearest strategy-level harm is **overtreatment**. Many preterm babies initially considered at risk will stabilize on CPAP and never require surfactant. Routine prophylaxis may therefore expose them to laryngoscopy, intubation, surfactant administration, positive-pressure ventilation, and potential airway instability without a demonstrated benefit. In the Dunn 2011 trial, approximately half of CPAP-first infants avoided both intubation and surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][10])

Routine intubation may also increase exposure to mechanical ventilation. In SUPPORT, CPAP-first care reduced intubation and ventilation duration without increasing death, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, or later neurodevelopmental impairment. ([PubMed][9])

The evidence does not show a consistent increase in major complications such as severe intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, or mortality from either strategy in modern trials. However, the POPART trial raises a specific concern that prophylactic oropharyngeal administration may increase pneumothorax; that result should not automatically be applied to standard intratracheal surfactant or minimally invasive surfactant administration. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][2])

## Long-term findings

Follow-up of the Merritt trial found similar growth, neurologic, and sensory outcomes at approximately one year after prophylactic or rescue treatment. ([PubMed][15])

A school-age follow-up from one center participating in the Kendig trial found fewer reported abnormal pulmonary histories among prophylaxis recipients, but formal pulmonary-function abnormalities were similar. Cognitive, motor, neurologic, behavioral, and educational outcomes did not differ appreciably. Because this represented only a subset of the original trial population, the finding is less definitive than the neonatal trial result. ([PubMed][16])

## How applicable is this to a particular baby?

The most relevant comparison depends on whether the baby:

* Received antenatal corticosteroids.
* Is breathing spontaneously at birth.
* Can be stabilized on CPAP.
* Has rising oxygen needs or worsening work of breathing.
* Can receive surfactant through a thin catheter while continuing to breathe, rather than through conventional intubation.
* Is extremely immature or already requires intubation for resuscitation.

A baby who must be intubated for resuscitation is different from a stable, spontaneously breathing preterm baby. Once an infant is already intubated and showing evidence of surfactant deficiency, prompt surfactant is not the same question as routinely intubating an otherwise stable infant solely for prophylaxis.
