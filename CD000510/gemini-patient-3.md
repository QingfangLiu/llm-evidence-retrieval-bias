Having a baby born preterm is incredibly stressful, and it is completely understandable that you want to deeply understand the treatments being proposed for your little one's lungs. You are doing exactly the right thing by asking these questions and looking at the primary evidence.

To understand the two approaches, it helps to know what **surfactant** actually does. Surfactant is a natural, slippery liquid that coats the inside of the alveoli (the tiny air sacs in the lungs). It acts like a delicate soap that lowers surface tension, keeping these tiny sacs from collapsing every time we exhale. Preterm babies often haven't produced enough of their own surfactant yet, which leads to Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS).

Doctors have two main ways to give replacement surfactant: **prophylactic** (giving it to all at-risk babies immediately at birth to prevent RDS) and **selective** (waiting to see if the baby develops RDS and only treating those who need it).

Here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms of each approach, based entirely on individual, primary randomized controlled trials.

## Preventive (Prophylactic) Surfactant

In this approach, the baby is given surfactant through a breathing tube (intubation) within the first 10 to 30 minutes of life, before they have a chance to show severe signs of struggling to breathe.

* **Benefits:** Early trials showed that giving surfactant right at birth led to a faster initial improvement in oxygen levels and less severe signs of RDS on early X-rays (Egberts et al., 1993). It can also reduce the likelihood that the baby will require multiple, later "rescue" doses of the medication.
* **Harms & Risks:** The primary drawback is that the baby must be intubated to receive the medication. Intubation is an invasive procedure that can cause airway irritation. Because doctors don't know exactly which babies will get sick at the moment of birth, this approach over-treats a significant number of infants. One major trial found that nearly half (48%) of the babies in the "wait-and-see" group stabilized completely on their own and avoided treatment altogether, meaning a prophylactic approach would have subjected those babies to an unnecessary invasive procedure (Dunn et al., 1991).

## Selective (Rescue) Surfactant

In this approach, doctors support the baby's breathing immediately after birth using non-invasive methods—usually a soft mask or nasal prongs delivering Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP). If the baby still struggles to breathe, they are *then* given surfactant.

* **Benefits:** The main benefit is that it spares many babies from unnecessary intubation and medication. A large trial comparing early CPAP alongside selective surfactant against prophylactic surfactant found that waiting to see if a baby actually needed surfactant was just as effective (Sandri et al., 2010). It did not increase the likelihood that the baby would end up needing mechanical ventilation, meaning many infants safely avoided intubation entirely.
* **Harms & Risks:** If a baby does end up struggling on CPAP, they will eventually have to undergo intubation later (usually within a few hours of birth) to receive the surfactant. There was also historical concern that delaying treatment might lead to worse lung damage, though recent trials utilizing modern CPAP have largely shown that waiting is just as safe (Sandri et al., 2010).

## The Big Picture

Over the last decade, neonatology has largely shifted away from universal prophylactic surfactant for most preterm infants. Because non-invasive support like CPAP has become highly effective at stabilizing premature lungs, a selective approach is now often preferred to spare fragile babies from unnecessary intubation (Sandri et al., 2010). However, your care team will tailor this choice specifically to your baby based on their exact gestational age, size, and how robustly they are breathing at the moment of birth.
