## Bottom line

Across individual comparative studies, **no advanced sperm-selection method has consistently and reproducibly increased live-birth rates compared with standard sperm preparation/selection**. PICSI has the largest randomized evidence base and did **not** significantly improve live birth. IMSI and microfluidic sorting have conflicting trial results. MACS and zeta-potential selection show possible benefits in selected male-factor populations, but the supporting studies are generally small, single-center, or observational.

I treated “standard selection” as density-gradient centrifugation or swim-up, followed by selection based mainly on motility and visible morphology. All studies below are **primary research reports**, not reviews or guidelines.

## Hyaluronic-acid binding: PICSI/HA-ICSI

**Miller et al., 2019 — HABSelect trial, *The Lancet*.** Multicenter randomized trial of 2,772 couples undergoing ICSI. Term live birth was 27.4% with PICSI versus 25.2% with standard ICSI, which was not statistically significant (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.95–1.34). Miscarriage after clinical pregnancy was lower with PICSI, but this did not translate into a demonstrated live-birth advantage. This is the most informative trial because of its size and multicenter design. ([PubMed][1])

**Worrilow et al., 2013 — *Human Reproduction*.** Multicenter double-blind randomized trial enrolling 802 couples. HA-selected sperm did not significantly improve the overall primary clinical-pregnancy outcome. In the subgroup with relatively low sperm HA-binding scores, pregnancy loss was lower with HA selection—3.3% versus 15.1%—but the trial stopped early for financial reasons and the favorable finding was subgroup-dependent. ([PubMed][2])

**Alegre et al., 2025 — *Biomedicines*.** Prospective, triple-blinded study of 277 donor-oocyte couples. Fresh-transfer live birth was 52.9% with PICSI versus 57.3% with ICSI, with no significant difference; miscarriage was also not significantly different. Cumulative pregnancy after four transfers favored PICSI, but cumulative live birth was not shown to improve. Results may not generalize well to patients using their own oocytes. ([PubMed][3])

**Interpretation:** PICSI may possibly reduce miscarriage in some circumstances, but the best-powered trial did not establish a meaningful improvement in live birth.

## High-magnification morphology: IMSI/MSOME

**Antinori et al., 2008 — *Reproductive BioMedicine Online*.** Randomized study of 446 couples with severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia. Clinical pregnancy was higher with IMSI than standard ICSI, 39.2% versus 26.5%. A subgroup with at least two previous failed attempts also appeared to benefit. Live birth was not reported as a complete prespecified comparative endpoint; only 35 births had occurred when the study was published. ([PubMed][4])

**Leandri et al., 2013 — *Andrology*.** Multicenter randomized trial of 255 couples undergoing their first ART attempt for male-factor infertility. IMSI did not improve implantation, clinical pregnancy, or live birth: live birth was 27% with IMSI versus 30% with ICSI. No benefit was identified according to sperm DNA fragmentation, nuclear maturity, or morphology. ([PubMed][5])

**De Vos et al., 2013 — *Human Reproduction*.** Prospective randomized sibling-oocyte study involving 350 ICSI cycles. Fertilization was 79.1% with IMSI versus 77.3% with ICSI, and embryo development through day 5 was similar. Clinical pregnancy was also similar among transfers containing only IMSI or only ICSI embryos, although the embryo-transfer assignment itself was not randomized. ([PubMed][6])

**Shakeri et al., 2026 — *Scientific Reports*.** Double-blind randomized trial of 100 couples with severe teratozoospermia. There were no statistically significant differences in fertilization, clinical or ongoing pregnancy, live birth, miscarriage, selected-sperm DNA fragmentation, or telomere length. This report was published online in June 2026 and was still presented by the publisher as an early, unedited version. ([PubMed][7])

**Interpretation:** An early large study was favorable, but subsequent multicenter, sibling-oocyte, and recent randomized studies have not reproduced a reliable live-birth benefit.

## Microfluidic sperm sorting, including Fertile Chip/ZyMōt-type methods

**Yetkinel et al., 2019 — *Journal of Assisted Reproduction and Genetics*.** Randomized trial of 122 couples with unexplained infertility. Microfluidic selection increased the number of grade-1 embryos, but clinical pregnancy was 48.3% versus 44.8% and live birth was 38.3% versus 36.2%; neither difference was significant. ([PMC][8])

**Aydın et al., 2022 — *Andrologia*.** Prospective randomized study of 128 couples with male-factor infertility. Fertilization and embryo-quality results were similar, but the Fertile Chip group had higher clinical pregnancy—59.4% versus 35.9%—and live birth—46.8% versus 25%. This positive result comes from a small, single-center study and conflicts with other randomized trials. ([Wiley Online Library][9])

**Huong et al., 2026 — *JBRA Assisted Reproduction*.** Randomized trial of 119 IVF couples comparing microfluidics with density-gradient centrifugation. Microfluidics reduced sperm DNA fragmentation more effectively, especially when baseline fragmentation was elevated, but live birth was 59.3% with microfluidics versus 63.3% with density-gradient preparation; pregnancy and ongoing-pregnancy outcomes were also similar. Some baseline characteristics remained imbalanced despite randomization. ([PubMed][10])

**Thanapongpibul et al., 2026 — *Scientific Reports*.** Randomized sibling-oocyte study of 55 couples with abnormal semen parameters. Microfluidic selection increased fertilization, 82.3% versus 74.7%, but blastocyst formation, good-quality blastocysts, and embryo euploidy were similar. Because this was an embryo-level sibling-oocyte study, it did not determine whether the method increases live birth per couple. ([PubMed][11])

**Interpretation:** Microfluidic sorting often improves sperm DNA-fragmentation or certain laboratory measures, but these improvements do not consistently produce more pregnancies or live births.

## Magnetic-activated cell sorting: MACS/Annexin-V selection

**Troya and Zorrilla, 2015 — *JBRA Assisted Reproduction*.** Prospective randomized study of 136 ICSI patients assigned to conventional ICSI, PICSI, or MACS. Fertilization and embryo-development measures were similar, while clinical pregnancy was 58.1% with MACS, 40.4% with PICSI, and 27.3% with standard ICSI. Live birth was not reported, and the individual treatment groups were small. ([PubMed][12])

**Hozyen et al., 2021 — *Journal of Assisted Reproduction and Genetics*.** Randomized trial of 302 patients with abnormal sperm DNA fragmentation, comparing standard density-gradient processing, testicular sperm, PICSI, and MACS. Ongoing pregnancy was higher with PICSI and MACS—62.8% and 62.0%—than with density-gradient processing, 45.8%; miscarriage did not differ. The four-arm design left relatively modest numbers in each comparison, and a definitive live-birth difference was not reported. ([PubMed][13])

**Gil Juliá et al., 2020 — *Journal of Clinical Medicine*.** Retrospective study of 724 cycles involving high sperm DNA fragmentation. MACS was associated with lower miscarriage in autologous cycles and higher live birth in both autologous and donor-oocyte cycles. Because the treatment was not randomized, differences in patient selection and clinical management could explain part of the association. ([PubMed][14])

**Gil Juliá et al., 2022 — *Reproductive BioMedicine Online*.** Large multicenter observational study of 41,397 donor-oocyte ICSI cycles: 1,240 with MACS and 40,157 with standard processing. Pregnancy and live birth per embryo transfer were not significantly improved, and differences in cumulative live birth were considered too small to be clinically meaningful. ([PubMed][15])

**Interpretation:** MACS may be more plausible in patients with demonstrably elevated sperm DNA fragmentation, but positive randomized evidence is limited and larger observational data do not support indiscriminate use.

## Surface-charge selection: zeta potential

**Nasr-Esfahani et al., 2016 — *International Journal of Fertility & Sterility*.** Double-blind randomized trial of 203 male-factor ICSI cycles comparing density-gradient plus zeta selection with density-gradient alone. The investigators reported higher embryo quality, pregnancy, and live-birth outcomes with zeta selection. They also found a change in the sex ratio at birth, making replication and longer-term safety/outcome evaluation particularly important. ([PubMed][16])

**Karimi et al., 2020 — *Cell Journal*.** Randomized single-blind trial analyzing 205 couples. Clinical pregnancy was 35.0% with density-gradient/zeta selection versus 20.7% with density-gradient alone; miscarriage was reported as 8.9% versus 19.4%. Implantation was not significantly different, and the study did not report a definitive live-birth comparison. ([PubMed][17])

**Interpretation:** Two relatively small trials reported favorable pregnancy outcomes, but robust multicenter live-birth confirmation is lacking.

## What this means for an IVF/ICSI patient

The most defensible conclusion from these individual studies is:

* **PICSI:** best studied; no proven live-birth improvement, although miscarriage might be reduced.
* **IMSI:** inconsistent findings, with later trials generally negative.
* **Microfluidics:** may select sperm with less DNA damage and occasionally improves fertilization, but live-birth findings conflict.
* **MACS:** possible benefit in selected patients with high sperm DNA fragmentation, but evidence remains vulnerable to small samples and selection bias.
* **Zeta selection:** promising pregnancy results, but inadequate replicated live-birth evidence.

A useful question for your clinic is: **“What specific abnormality in our case is this add-on intended to address, and what randomized live-birth evidence applies to patients like us?”** The answer may differ substantially for severe male-factor infertility, elevated sperm DNA fragmentation, recurrent pregnancy loss, donor eggs, and an otherwise unselected first ICSI cycle.
