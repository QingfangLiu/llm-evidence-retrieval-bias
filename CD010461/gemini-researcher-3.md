Based on your constraints, I have excluded all secondary literature and focused strictly on individual primary studies (randomized controlled trials and large cohort studies) evaluating advanced sperm selection against standard processing (like density-gradient centrifugation or swim-up).

Across the primary literature, a consistent theme emerges: while advanced selection techniques often succeed in isolating sperm with better morphological or biochemical parameters, they struggle to demonstrate significant improvements in the most critical clinical endpoints—clinical pregnancy rates (CPR) and live birth rates (LBR)—for unselected patient populations.

Here is a breakdown of the primary evidence by technique.

## Microfluidic Sperm Selection

Microfluidic devices (such as ZyMōt or Fertile Chip) isolate motile sperm via micro-channels and chemotaxis. Because they avoid the centrifugation steps required in standard density-gradient centrifugation (DGC) and swim-up, they theoretically reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage and DNA fragmentation.

* **Compared to DGC:** A pragmatic, prospective RCT by Quinn et al. (2022) compared microfluidic separation to standard DGC in 386 unselected patients undergoing IVF with ICSI. The study found no significant differences in the fraction of high-quality cleavage-stage embryos or blastocysts. Crucially, there were no differences in clinical or ongoing pregnancy rates.
* **Compared to Swim-Up:** An RCT of 122 couples with unexplained infertility compared the microfluidic Fertile Chip against standard swim-up (Yetkinel et al., 2018). While the microfluidic group yielded a higher absolute number of grade-1 embryos and surplus top-quality embryos to freeze, there was no statistical difference in actual fertilization rates, clinical pregnancy rates (48.3% vs. 44.8%), or live birth rates (38.3% vs. 36.2%).

## Magnetic-Activated Cell Sorting (MACS) and PICSI

MACS targets and filters out apoptotic sperm expressing phosphatidylserine using annexin V-conjugated microbeads. Physiological ICSI (PICSI) selects mature sperm based on their ability to bind to hyaluronic acid.

* **MACS vs. Standard Processing:** To assess real-world impact, a large multicenter retrospective study analyzed 48,586 ICSI cycles, comparing standard processing against standard processing plus MACS (Gil Juliá et al., 2021). The authors found no significant differences in overall clinical pregnancy or live birth rates between the groups. They observed only a very subtle statistical increase in the cumulative live birth rate per transferred embryo, concluding that MACS has a limited clinical impact for unselected males.
* **MACS vs. PICSI:** An RCT of 413 ICSI patients specifically targeting those with abnormal sperm DNA fragmentation (>20.3%) compared MACS directly against PICSI (Hasanen et al., 2020). The study found both techniques were similarly efficient, with no overall difference in clinical or ongoing pregnancy rates. A sub-analysis suggested age-related nuances: MACS yielded slightly better results in females under 30, while PICSI trended better for females aged 30–35.

## Intracytoplasmic Morphologically Selected Sperm Injection (IMSI)

IMSI utilizes ultra-high magnification (typically >6000x) to select sperm devoid of subtle morphological defects (such as head vacuoles) prior to injection, aiming to improve upon standard ICSI (which uses ~400x magnification).

* **First-Attempt ART:** A multicenter RCT restricted to 255 couples undergoing their first ART cycle due to male infertility found that IMSI provided no advantage over conventional ICSI (Leandri et al., 2013). The researchers found no statistical differences in implantation (24% vs. 23%), clinical pregnancy (31% vs. 33%), or live birth rates (27% vs. 30%), and noted these results held regardless of baseline sperm morphology or DNA fragmentation levels.
* **Severe Male Factor Exception:** A prospective RCT of 168 infertile couples similarly found that IMSI did not provide a statistically significant overall improvement in clinical outcomes compared with ICSI for an unselected population (Balaban et al., 2011). However, a sub-analysis revealed that a specific cohort—patients with severe male factor infertility—did experience significantly higher implantation rates with IMSI (29.6% vs. 15.2%), suggesting the technique's value is likely restricted to highly targeted populations.
