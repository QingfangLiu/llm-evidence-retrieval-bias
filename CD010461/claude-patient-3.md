Here's a curated set of individual primary studies (RCTs, prospective/retrospective cohort or comparative studies) on advanced sperm selection techniques vs. standard selection in IVF/ICSI. I've excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, and editorials.

## PICSI (hyaluronan-binding selection)

- **HABSelect trial (Miller et al., *The Lancet*, 2019)** — the largest RCT of PICSI. 2,772 couples across 16 UK clinics randomized to PICSI vs. standard ICSI. This parallel, two-group, randomised trial found no significant difference in term livebirth rate between PICSI (27.4%) and ICSI (25.2%) (OR 1.12; 95% CI 0.95–1.34; p=0.18), concluding PICSI does not significantly improve term livebirth rates and that its wider use is not currently recommended. However, there was a significant reduction in miscarriage with PICSI compared with standard ICSI (4.3% vs 7.0%; OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.43–0.84; P=.003), though the study wasn't powered for that outcome.

- **IVI-RMA Valencia triple-blind trial (2025, *J. Clin. Med.* / MDPI)** — a prospective, randomized, triple-blind cohort study in an egg-donation program found a higher pregnancy rate with PICSI (74.04%) vs. ICSI (70.87%), and cumulative pregnancy rate was significantly higher in the PICSI group (88% vs. control), though most clinical outcomes and blastocyst formation rates were comparable between groups.

## MACS (magnetic-activated cell sorting)

- **Prospective randomized trial of MACS-DGC vs. DGC alone** — 62 semen samples allocated to MACS combined with density gradient centrifugation (n=29) vs. DGC alone (n=33); high-quality embryo percentage, pregnancy, and implantation rates were significantly higher in the MACS-DGC group, though fertilization rates did not differ.

- **MACS in donor IUI, RCT (2022, PMC)** — a prospective randomized multicenter study of 181 donor-IUI treatments comparing MACS+DGC vs. DGC alone found no significant differences in gestation, live birth, or miscarriage rates between groups.

- **Retrospective cohort, high sperm DNA fragmentation (PMC7763893)** — 724 ART cycles divided into MACS-after-DGC (n=366) vs. DGC-alone (n=358); the MACS group showed significantly lower miscarriage rates in autologous ICSI cycles, higher pregnancy rates in oocyte-donation cycles, and significantly higher live-birth rates in both autologous and donation cycles.

- **Retrospective multicenter cumulative live birth study (Gil Juliá et al., PMC8150702)** — comparing standard practice (46,807 patients) vs. added MACS (1,779 patients) in autologous ICSI cycles; only cumulative live birth rate per embryo transferred and per MII oocyte was significantly higher with MACS, with no significant differences in per-cycle pregnancy or live birth rates.

- **Retrospective observational study, Kerala (2024)** — 116 ICSI patients (58 MACS-after-DGC vs. 58 DGC-alone), evaluating fertilization, embryo quality, blastocyst formation, and pregnancy rates.

## Microfluidic sperm sorting

- **Aydın et al., RCT (*Andrologia*, 2022)** — 128 patients with male-factor infertility randomized to swim-up (n=64) vs. Fertile Chip microfluidic selection (n=64); no significant difference in fertilization rate or top-grade embryos, but implantation rate was significantly higher with the Fertile Chip (50% vs. lower in controls).

- **Retrospective cohort, double-strand breaks (ScienceDirect, 2022)** — 28 couples with elevated sperm DNA double-strand breaks underwent a first ICSI cycle with conventional preparation and a second with the ZyMōt/FertileChip microfluidic device; biochemical pregnancy rose 28.3%, clinical pregnancy 35.6%, and live births 35.3% in the microfluidic cycle.

- **Paired-cycle cohort, FERTILE PLUS™ (PMC11111481)** — couples acting as their own controls across consecutive cycles (DGC vs. FERTILE PLUS™); blastocyst, utilization, and euploidy rates were significantly higher with the microfluidic chip (76% vs 56%, p=0.002; 60% vs 41%, p=0.005; 40% vs 20%, p=0.001), though fertilization rate improvement was not significant.

- **Fertilization-failure cohort (PubMed 35650258)** — comparing microfluidic sorting to conventional DGC in patients with prior fertilization failure, sperm selected by the microfluidic chip showed better morphology, motility, and lower DNA fragmentation, with improved subsequent fertilization rate.

- **Sperm-chip vs. swim-up cohort, normal semen analysis (IMR Press, 2023)** — 102 ICSI patients using a sperm chip vs. 111 using swim-up, comparing reproductive outcomes in men with normal semen parameters.

## IMSI (high-magnification morphological selection)

- **Leandri et al., multicentric RCT (*Andrology*, 2013)** — 255 couples in their first ART attempt for male infertility randomized to IMSI vs. ICSI; IMSI did not provide significant improvement in implantation or other clinical outcomes.

- **Antinori et al., prospective RCT (*RBM Online*, 2008)** — 446 couples randomized to ICSI (n=219) vs. IMSI (n=227), comparing pregnancy, miscarriage, and implantation rates.

- **Open prospective trial, single center (PMC8560014, 2016–2017)** — 446 ICSI cycles vs. 79 IMSI cycles; ICSI was statistically better than IMSI for chemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, live birth, and fertilization rates, with no significant difference in miscarriage — concluding IMSI is not superior to ICSI as a first-attempt technique.

- **Repeated-failure cohort study (ScienceDirect, 2012)** — 75 infertile couples offered IMSI after ≥2 prior IVF/ICSI failures, using couples as their own controls, evaluating embryo quality and blastocyst numbers.

- **Repeated implantation failure cohort (PMC3156729)** — found IMSI did not significantly improve clinical outcomes vs. ICSI in couples with repeated implantation failure, though there were non-significant trends toward lower miscarriage and higher ongoing pregnancy/live birth rates.

## A note on IVF vs. ICSI itself

- **INVICSI trial (Denmark, RCT, 2025)** — 824 women without severe male-factor infertility randomized to ICSI (n=414) or conventional IVF (n=410) across six clinics, comparing cumulative live birth rates. (Relevant as context, though this compares ICSI vs. conventional insemination rather than a sperm-selection technique within ICSI.)

---

**Bottom line across techniques:** Evidence is mixed and technique-dependent. The largest, most rigorous RCT (HABSelect, PICSI) found no live-birth benefit despite a miscarriage reduction. MACS and microfluidic sorting show more consistent signals of benefit in select outcomes (embryo quality, implantation, or specific subgroups like elevated DNA fragmentation), but effects are inconsistent for headline live-birth rates. IMSI trials have largely been disappointing for unselected populations, with possible benefit limited to repeated-failure subgroups.
