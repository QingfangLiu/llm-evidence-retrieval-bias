## Bottom line

The individual primary studies do **not show that any advanced sperm-selection technique consistently increases live-birth rates for IVF/ICSI patients overall**.

The clearest finding comes from a large randomized trial of hyaluronan-based selection/PICSI: it **reduced miscarriage but did not significantly improve term live birth**. Microfluidic selection, IMSI, MACS, and Zeta selection have produced conflicting results—often positive in small or highly selected populations, but neutral in other randomized trials.

Every publication below is an **individual primary comparative study**; no review, meta-analysis, guideline, committee opinion, or editorial is included.

## 1. Hyaluronan binding/PICSI

**Miller et al., 2019 — HABSelect trial**

* Multicenter randomized trial of 2,772 couples undergoing ICSI.
* PICSI was compared with standard visual sperm selection.
* Term live birth: **27.4% with PICSI versus 25.2% with standard ICSI**, OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.95–1.34; not statistically significant.
* Clinical pregnancy was virtually identical.
* Miscarriage per randomized couple was lower: **4.3% versus 7.0%**, p=0.003.
* This is the most informative PICSI trial because it was much larger than the others and used term live birth as its primary outcome. ([PMC][1])

**Worrilow et al., 2013**

* Randomized, double-blind multicenter study enrolling 802 couples.
* The prespecified primary outcome, clinical pregnancy, was not significantly improved.
* Among patients whose semen had relatively low hyaluronan-binding scores, pregnancy loss was lower with hyaluronan-selected sperm—for example, **3.3% versus 15.1%** using one low-binding definition.
* Important limitations: the favorable miscarriage result was a secondary/subgroup finding, and enrollment stopped early for financial reasons. Live birth was not the main reported endpoint. ([PMC][2])

**Hozyen et al., 2022**

* Randomized four-arm study of 302 patients with abnormal sperm DNA fragmentation.
* Compared density-gradient centrifugation, PICSI, MACS, and testicular sperm.
* Ongoing pregnancy was higher with **PICSI, 62.8%, and MACS, 62.0%, versus 45.8% with density-gradient selection**.
* Miscarriage did not differ significantly.
* This suggests a possible benefit in a specifically selected high-DNA-fragmentation population, but the trial does not establish a general live-birth benefit. ([PubMed][3])

**Interpretation:** PICSI does not appear to produce a clinically important general increase in live births. A reduction in miscarriage is plausible, particularly in some patients with poorer hyaluronan binding or abnormal sperm DNA fragmentation.

## 2. Microfluidic sperm selection, including FertileChip/ZyMōt

**Yetkinel et al., 2019**

* Randomized trial of 122 couples with unexplained infertility undergoing ICSI.
* Microfluidic selection was compared with conventional swim-up.
* Live birth: **38.3% versus 36.2%**, p=0.48.
* Clinical pregnancy: 48.3% versus 44.8%, also not significant.
* The microfluidic group produced more grade-1 embryos, but this did not translate into more births. ([PubMed][4])

**Aydın et al., 2022**

* Randomized study of 128 couples undergoing ICSI for male-factor infertility.
* FertileChip was compared with conventional swim-up.
* Live birth was significantly higher: **46.8% versus 25.0%**, p=0.009.
* Clinical pregnancy was also higher: 59.4% versus 35.9%.
* Fertilization and embryo-quality measures did not differ significantly. This is an encouraging result, but it is a relatively small, single-center trial. ([PubMed][5])

**Do Thuy Huong et al., 2026**

* Randomized trial of 119 couples comparing microfluidics with density-gradient centrifugation.
* Microfluidics reduced sperm DNA fragmentation more effectively, particularly when baseline fragmentation was elevated.
* It did **not** improve fertilization, clinical pregnancy, ongoing pregnancy, or live birth.
* Among patients who had embryo transfer, live birth was **59.3% with microfluidics versus 63.3% with density-gradient selection**, p=0.676. ([PMC][6])

**Balakier et al., 2026**

* Retrospective comparison of 1,091 ZyMōt cycles and 1,176 density-gradient cycles.
* ZyMōt was associated with higher fertilization and blastocyst-development rates.
* Following euploid-embryo transfers, reported live birth was higher with ZyMōt in donor cycles, **68.0% versus 49.7%**, and nondonor cycles, **62.6% versus 52.3%**.
* Because treatment was not randomized, differences in patient selection, treatment period, laboratory practice, or other confounders could account for part of the benefit. ([PubMed][7])

**Interpretation:** Microfluidic devices reliably select sperm with favorable laboratory characteristics, such as lower DNA fragmentation, but randomized trials are inconsistent regarding live birth. The positive studies do not yet establish which patients are most likely to benefit.

## 3. High-magnification selection/IMSI

**Antinori et al., 2008**

* Randomized trial of 446 couples with severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia.
* IMSI selected sperm under approximately ×6,600 magnification and was compared with standard ICSI.
* Clinical pregnancy was higher: **39.2% versus 26.5%**, p=0.004.
* A particularly large difference was reported among couples with at least two previous failed attempts.
* The study reported pregnancy and miscarriage results but did not provide a robust comparative live-birth endpoint for all randomized couples. ([PubMed][8])

**Leandri et al., 2013**

* Multicenter randomized trial of 255 couples having their first ART attempt for male-factor infertility.
* IMSI did not improve:

  * Implantation: 24% versus 23%.
  * Clinical pregnancy: 31% versus 33%.
  * Live birth: **27% versus 30%**.
* Results remained neutral when evaluated by sperm DNA fragmentation, nuclear maturity, and morphology. ([PubMed][9])

**Setti et al., 2013**

* Small randomized study of 66 couples in which the woman was at least 37 years old.
* Clinical pregnancy was **60.0% with IMSI versus 13.8% with standard ICSI**.
* Implantation was also higher.
* This was a very small, selected subgroup study and did not establish an improvement in cumulative live birth. ([PubMed][10])

**Interpretation:** IMSI results vary markedly by study. Positive findings have come from selected groups such as severe male-factor infertility or advanced maternal age, whereas the multicenter first-cycle trial found no live-birth improvement.

## 4. Magnetic-activated cell sorting/MACS

**Romany et al., 2014**

* Prospective, randomized, triple-blind study of 237 couples using donor oocytes.
* MACS plus swim-up was compared with swim-up alone.
* Fertilization, embryo development, implantation, pregnancy, and live birth were similar.
* Live birth: **48.4% with MACS versus 56.4% with control**, not statistically significant.
* Use of donor oocytes reduced variation from oocyte quality, but the male participants were unselected rather than specifically chosen for high DNA fragmentation. ([ScienceDirect][11])

**Sánchez-Martín et al., 2016**

* Prospective randomized study of 136 ICSI patients comparing standard ICSI, PICSI, and MACS.
* Fertilization and embryo-development measures were similar.
* Clinical pregnancy was reported as **58.1% with MACS, 40.4% with PICSI, and 27.3% with standard ICSI**, p=0.019.
* Live birth was not reported, and the groups were small. ([PubMed][12])

**Pacheco et al., 2020**

* Retrospective study of 724 cycles involving patients with high sperm DNA fragmentation.
* In autologous-oocyte cycles, live birth was **40.9% with MACS versus 24.6% with density-gradient selection alone**; miscarriage was lower.
* In donor-oocyte cycles, live birth was 51.8% versus 29.4%.
* These results apply to a selected high-fragmentation population, but nonrandomized allocation leaves substantial potential for confounding. ([PMC][13])

**Interpretation:** MACS did not help unselected men in the strongest live-birth RCT. Benefits remain possible in men with demonstrably high DNA fragmentation, but the positive live-birth evidence is mainly observational or from small trials.

## 5. Zeta-potential/surface-charge selection

**Nasr-Esfahani et al., 2016**

* Double-blind randomized trial of 203 ICSI cycles involving male-factor infertility.
* Density-gradient plus Zeta selection was compared with density-gradient selection alone.
* The Zeta group had more top-quality embryos and higher pregnancy and live-birth rates.
* The study also unexpectedly reported a change in the sex ratio at birth, an additional reason the results warrant independent confirmation. ([PMC][14])

**Karimi et al., 2020**

* Randomized single-blind study of 205 evaluable couples with male-factor infertility.
* Clinical pregnancy was **35.0% with DGC/Zeta versus 20.7% with DGC alone**, p=0.007.
* Miscarriage was lower, 8.9% versus 19.4%.
* Fertilization was not significantly different, and the study did not provide a definitive live-birth comparison. ([PMC][15])

**Interpretation:** Two relatively small trials reported favorable pregnancy outcomes, but large multicenter live-birth trials and broad replication are lacking.

## Practical conclusion for an IVF/ICSI decision

Based on these primary studies:

* **PICSI:** may reduce miscarriage, but the largest trial did not show a significant live-birth improvement.
* **Microfluidics/ZyMōt:** improves sperm DNA-quality measurements; evidence for more live births is conflicting.
* **IMSI:** no consistent benefit; possible subgroup effects have not been reliably reproduced.
* **MACS:** no demonstrated benefit for unselected patients; possibly more relevant when sperm DNA fragmentation is clearly elevated.
* **Zeta selection:** promising small-trial results, but insufficient independent live-birth confirmation.

The most important distinction is whether the add-on is being proposed **routinely** or for a defined problem—such as repeatedly elevated sperm DNA fragmentation, severe teratozoospermia, recurrent pregnancy loss, or previous failed ICSI. Laboratory improvements alone do not necessarily mean a higher chance of taking home a baby.
