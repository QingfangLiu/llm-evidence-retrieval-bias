## Primary comparative studies

I searched for human IVF/ICSI studies comparing an advanced sperm-selection method with conventional selection—typically density-gradient centrifugation, swim-up, and embryologist assessment of motility/morphology. I prioritized live birth, ongoing pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, and miscarriage over laboratory surrogates. All studies below are **individual primary studies**, current through **July 17, 2026**.

### Overall interpretation

My reading of the primary literature is that **no advanced sperm-selection method has yet demonstrated a consistent, independently replicated live-birth advantage for routine use in unselected IVF/ICSI patients**.

The strongest evidence is for hyaluronan-based selection/PICSI. The large HABSelect trial found no significant improvement in term live birth or clinical pregnancy, although miscarriage was lower. A newer donor-oocyte trial also found no live-birth improvement. ([PubMed][1])

IMSI, microfluidic selection, and MACS have each produced positive results in selected populations, but results are inconsistent across trials. Some of the largest effects come from small, single-center or nonrandomized studies and have not yet been convincingly replicated. ([PubMed][2])

## 1. Hyaluronan-binding sperm selection: PICSI/HA-ICSI

| Primary study                                 | Design and population                                                                                   | Main reproductive findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Miller et al., HABSelect, 2019, Lancet**    | Multicenter RCT; 2,772 couples undergoing ICSI with fresh transfer at 16 UK centers                     | Term live birth: **27.4% PICSI vs 25.2% ICSI**, OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.95–1.34, *p*=0.18. Clinical pregnancy was essentially identical. Miscarriage per randomized couple was lower: **4.3% vs 7.0%**, OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43–0.84. This is the most informative trial and does not support a meaningful live-birth benefit. ([PubMed][1]) |
| **Worrilow et al., 2013, Human Reproduction** | Prospective, multicenter, double-blind RCT; 802 couples                                                 | The primary clinical-pregnancy outcome was not clearly improved overall. In a prespecified/stratified population with lower hyaluronan-binding scores—≤65%—HA-selected sperm were associated with lower pregnancy-loss rates. This suggested a possible biomarker-selected effect rather than a general benefit. ([PubMed][3])      |
| **Majumdar and Majumdar, 2013**               | Prospective RCT; 156 first-cycle ICSI patients with unexplained infertility and normal semen parameters | No differences in fertilization, top-quality embryos, or clinical pregnancy. Live birth was **31% with PICSI vs 26.3% with ICSI**; pregnancy loss was 12% vs 25%, but neither difference was statistically significant. ([PubMed][4])                                                                                               |
| **Alegre et al., 2025**                       | Prospective triple-blinded study; 277 couples in an oocyte-donation program                             | PICSI did **not increase live-birth rate**. Pregnancy rate was numerically similar, and cumulative pregnancy after four transfers was reported as 88% vs 72%, but this did not translate into improved live birth. Fertilization per oocyte was actually lower in the PICSI arm. ([PubMed][5])                                      |

**Clinical signal:** PICSI may reduce miscarriage in some settings, but the reduction has not produced a clearly significant improvement in live birth. Evidence for using hyaluronan-binding scores to select patients remains exploratory.

## 2. High-magnification morphology: IMSI/MSOME

| Primary study             | Design and population                                                                               | Main reproductive findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Antinori et al., 2008** | Prospective randomized study; 446 couples with severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia, women ≤35 years | Clinical pregnancy was **39.2% with IMSI vs 26.5% with ICSI**, *p*=0.004. The apparent benefit was greatest among patients with at least two previous failures; miscarriage was also lower in that subgroup. Live birth was not reported as a complete randomized endpoint. ([PubMed][2]) |
| **Balaban et al., 2011**  | Prospective randomized study; 168 unselected ICSI cycles                                            | No statistically significant improvement overall. Live birth was **43.7% vs 38.3%** and clinical pregnancy 54.0% vs 44.4%, both nonsignificant. A subgroup with severe male factor had better implantation with IMSI, but this was a subgroup finding. ([RBMO Journal][6])                |
| **Leandri et al., 2013**  | Multicenter RCT; 255 couples undergoing their first ART cycle for male-factor infertility           | No benefit for implantation, clinical pregnancy, or live birth: **live birth 27% IMSI vs 30% ICSI**. Outcomes remained similar across sperm DNA-fragmentation, nuclear-maturity, and morphology strata. ([PubMed][7])                                                                     |

**Clinical signal:** IMSI may have a signal in severe male-factor infertility or repeated failures, but trials in first-cycle or unselected patients are neutral. Subgroup-positive studies have not established a reliable live-birth indication.

## 3. Microfluidic sperm sorting

| Primary study                               | Design and population                                                                   | Main reproductive findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Yetkinel et al., 2019**                   | Prospective RCT; 122 couples with unexplained infertility, microfluidic chip vs swim-up | Clinical pregnancy: **48.3% vs 44.8%**, *p*=0.35. Live birth: **38.3% vs 36.2%**, *p*=0.48. More grade-1 embryos were obtained with the chip, but no clinical effectiveness benefit was shown. ([PubMed][8])                                                                                                           |
| **Aydın et al., 2022**                      | Prospective RCT; 128 couples with male-factor infertility, Fertile Chip vs swim-up      | Live birth was **46.8% vs 25.0%**, *p*=0.009; clinical pregnancy was 59.4% vs 35.9%. Fertilization and embryo-quality outcomes were not different. This is a positive study, although it was small and came from the same general research setting as the earlier neutral unexplained-infertility study. ([PubMed][9]) |
| **Ugur et al., 2019, sibling-oocyte study** | Sibling oocytes from 81 patients allocated to microfluidic sorting or swim-up           | No significant differences in fertilization, blastocyst development, implantation, clinical pregnancy, or live birth. Clinical comparisons were secondary and limited by transfer cancellations. ([PubMed][10])                                                                                                        |
| **Huong et al., 2026**                      | RCT; 119 IVF couples, microfluidic sorting vs density-gradient centrifugation           | Microfluidics reduced sperm DNA fragmentation more effectively, especially when baseline DFI was ≥15%, but there were **no significant differences in fertilization, clinical pregnancy, ongoing pregnancy, or live birth**. ([PubMed][11])                                                                            |

**Clinical signal:** Microfluidics consistently appears capable of reducing measured DNA fragmentation, but improvement in the laboratory measurement has not reliably translated into live birth. The two principal clinical RCTs reporting exact live-birth results are discordant.

## 4. Magnetic-activated cell sorting: MACS/Annexin V selection

| Primary study                | Design and population                                                                          | Main reproductive findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Troya and Zorrilla, 2015** | Prospective randomized study; 136 patients assigned to conventional ICSI, PICSI, or MACS       | Clinical pregnancy was **58.1% with MACS, 40.4% with PICSI, and 27.3% with conventional ICSI**, *p*=0.019. Fertilization and embryo-development measures were similar. Live birth was not reported. Small three-arm study with unusually large differences. ([PubMed][12])                                          |
| **Gil Juliá et al., 2020**   | Retrospective comparative study; 724 cycles with high sperm DNA fragmentation, MACS+DGC vs DGC | In autologous cycles, live birth was **40.9% vs 24.6%** and miscarriage 11.3% vs 25.5%. In donor-oocyte cycles, live birth was **51.8% vs 29.4%**. Because treatment was not randomized, residual selection and confounding are important concerns. ([MDPI][13])                                                    |
| **Azadi et al., 2022**       | Prospective single-blind clinical trial; 206 couples with severe teratozoospermia              | Implantation was higher with MACS+DGC: **18.1% vs 10.4%**. Clinical pregnancy was 30.8% vs 22.2%, but not statistically significant. The MACS arm produced 24 pregnancies resulting in liveborn children versus 16 in the control arm, although live birth was not analyzed as the primary endpoint. ([PubMed][14]) |
| **Mei et al., 2022**         | Comparative study; approximately 80 first-cycle IVF/ICSI patients with DFI ≥30%                | First-transfer live birth was **63.2% vs 53.9%**, and cumulative live birth 79.5% vs 70.7%; these were trends rather than statistically significant improvements. Fewer transfers were required in the MACS group. ([PubMed][15])                                                                                   |

**Clinical signal:** MACS may be more plausible in patients selected for high sperm DNA fragmentation, but the strongest positive live-birth findings are observational. Randomized/prospective studies are small and frequently report implantation or clinical pregnancy rather than adequately powered cumulative live birth.

## 5. Other advanced selection approaches

| Technique and study                                                          | Primary-study result                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Zeta-potential selection — Kheirollahi-Kouhestani et al., 2016**           | Double-blind randomized trial of 203 male-factor ICSI cycles. DGC plus Zeta selection produced more top-quality embryos and higher pregnancy rates than DGC alone. It remains a single-center result without robust independent replication. The study also reported an altered offspring sex ratio, emphasizing the need for further safety and reproducibility work. ([PubMed][16]) |
| **Sperm-head birefringence — Gianaroli et al., 2008**                        | Prospective randomized study; 112 birefringence-selected cases and 119 conventional ICSI controls. No overall fertilization or cleavage benefit; clinical and ongoing pregnancy and implantation were higher only in the most severe male-factor subgroups. ([PubMed][17])                                                                                                            |
| **Flow-cytometric removal of DNA-fragmented sperm — de Geyter et al., 2019** | Double-blind RCT; 104 couples. Live birth was **42.3% with FACS vs 34.6% with swim-up**, but the prespecified efficacy target was not reached and the study stopped early. The authors concluded that ICSI outcome was not significantly improved. ([PubMed][18])                                                                                                                     |
| **Cumulus-cell-column selection — Yousefi et al., 2025**                     | Double-blind RCT; 88 ICSI cycles. Reported live birth was **72.7% vs 25.0%**, with large improvements in implantation and clinical pregnancy. This exceptionally large effect comes from a small, single-center study with multiple outcomes and requires independent replication before clinical adoption. ([PubMed][19])                                                            |
| **Zona-pellucida adhesion — 2024 primary study**                             | Sperm adhering to solubilized zona pellucida were compared with conventional selection in euploid single-embryo-transfer cycles. Live birth was **38.0% vs 25.9%**, clinical pregnancy 43.8% vs 33.3%, and miscarriage 11.3% vs 22.2%. The study appears nonrandomized and should be considered hypothesis-generating. ([PubMed][20])                                                 |

## Practical evidence hierarchy

For patient counseling or protocol decisions, I would weigh the evidence in this order:

1. **HABSelect/PICSI:** adequately powered live-birth evidence; no significant live-birth advantage, possible miscarriage reduction.
2. **IMSI and microfluidics:** multiple comparative trials, but clinically important results conflict and may depend on patient selection.
3. **MACS:** potentially useful signal in high-DFI populations, but live-birth evidence is predominantly observational or underpowered.
4. **Zeta, birefringence, FACS, zona adhesion, and cumulus-cell columns:** interesting individual studies, but insufficient replication.

One IMSI RCT published by La Sala et al. in 2015 should **not** be used as efficacy evidence because the article was subsequently retracted. ([PMC][21])
