## Evidence overview

My synthesis of the comparative primary studies is that **no advanced sperm-selection technique has yet produced a consistent, reproducible improvement in live birth over standard density-gradient, swim-up, or conventional morphology-based selection**.

The most informative evidence is:

* **Hyaluronan/PICSI:** the large HABSelect trial found no statistically significant improvement in term live birth, although miscarriage was reduced.
* **Microfluidic selection:** larger pragmatic trials generally found similar pregnancy and live-birth outcomes; one smaller male-factor trial reported substantially better outcomes.
* **IMSI:** several small or selected-population trials were positive, but larger and more recent trials were null.
* **MACS:** the better-controlled donor-oocyte trial was null; smaller trials and high–DNA-fragmentation cohorts are mixed.
* **Zeta potential, flow cytometry, birefringence and electrophoresis:** evidence is sparse, generally single-center and not independently replicated.

The studies below are primary comparative investigations; reproductive outcomes are prioritized over improvements in sperm parameters alone.

## 1. Hyaluronan-binding selection: PICSI or HA-ICSI

| Primary study                                     | Design and population                                                                         | Main comparative findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Miller et al., HABSelect, 2019**; PMID 30712901 | Multicenter parallel RCT; 2,772 couples at 16 UK centers; PICSI versus conventional ICSI      | Term live birth: **27.4% versus 25.2%**, OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.95–1.34, *p*=0.18. Clinical pregnancy was also similar. Miscarriage following clinical pregnancy was lower with PICSI: approximately **4.3% versus 7.0%**, OR 0.61. | **Worrilow et al., 2013**; PMID 23203216          | Multicenter, double-blind RCT; 802 couples; HA-bound sperm selection versus conventional ICSI | No clear overall clinical-pregnancy advantage. A post-randomization/subgroup analysis among samples with lower HA-binding scores, generally ≤65%, reported less pregnancy loss with HA-selected sperm. The subgroup-dependent finding requires caution. ([PMC][2])                                                                                                                       |
| **Majumdar and Majumdar, 2013**; PMID 24085466    | Randomized trial; 156 couples with unexplained infertility and normal semen parameters        | Fertilization: **65.7% versus 64.7%**; top-quality embryos: 45.8% versus 43.6%; clinical pregnancy: **35.0% versus 35.2%**. Pregnancy loss was numerically different but not significant. No effectiveness advantage was demonstrated. ([PubMed][3])                                                                                                                                     |
| **Alegre et al., 2025**; PMID 40426931            | Prospective, triple-blind randomized study; 277 donor-oocyte recipients                       | Clinical outcomes, including live birth, were broadly comparable. Reported pregnancy was 74.0% with PICSI and 70.9% with ICSI; fertilization per injected oocyte was lower in the PICSI group. A higher cumulative pregnancy estimate after repeated transfers was reported, but this was not accompanied by a convincing live-birth improvement. ([PubMed][4])                          |

**Interpretation:** PICSI has the largest evidence base. It may affect miscarriage, but the largest trial did **not** demonstrate improved term live birth.

Residual confounding and treatment-selection bias are major concerns in both directions. ([PubMed][20])

## Practical evidence-synthesis conclusions

1. **Live birth should remain the decisive outcome.** Many studies demonstrate lower sperm DNA fragmentation, better motility or better morphological embryo grading without a corresponding improvement in live birth.

2. **PICSI has the most precise evidence.** Its estimated live-birth effect is small and compatible with no benefit, while the miscarriage signal is more consistent and merits separate assessment rather than being interpreted as proof of improved overall effectiveness. ([PubMed][1])

3. **Microfluidic selection is biologically promising but clinically inconsistent.** The positive 128-couple male-factor trial conflicts with several null randomized studies, including a pragmatic trial and the 2026 RCT. ([PubMed][7])

4. **IMSI effects are unstable.** Small selected-population studies report large improvements, while multicenter, first-cycle and recent severe-teratozoospermia RCTs do not establish a live-birth advantage. ([PubMed][10])

5. **Potential effect modifiers are not yet validated treatment indications.** Frequently proposed modifiers include high sperm DNA fragmentation, severe teratozoospermia, repeated implantation failure, previous miscarriage and low HA-binding scores. Most favorable findings in these groups come from underpowered subgroup analyses, small trials or nonrandomized cohorts.

For extraction purposes, sibling-oocyte studies should be kept separate from couple-randomized trials: they provide strong within-cycle embryology comparisons but generally cannot estimate treatment-specific pregnancy, miscarriage or live-birth effects.
