## Summary

Weight-loss medications generally lower blood pressure alongside weight loss, and the newer GLP-1-based agents (semaglutide, tirzepatide) have also shown reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events in trials enrolling patients with high cardiovascular risk (most of whom had hypertension). Older agents (sibutramine, and to a lesser degree naltrexone-bupropion) raised cardiovascular safety concerns despite producing weight loss, underscoring that weight loss itself doesn't guarantee better cardiovascular health — the drug's other pharmacologic effects matter.

- **GLP-1/GIP-based agents (semaglutide, tirzepatide):** Consistently reduce systolic and diastolic BP (roughly 4–9 mmHg systolic in the STEP program; ~6 mmHg with tirzepatide in SURMOUNT-1). In SELECT, a dedicated cardiovascular outcomes trial in people with obesity, established cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 20% versus placebo, with more modest BP reduction (likely because participants were already on antihypertensive therapy).
- **Phentermine/topiramate:** Reduces both office and ambulatory BP in placebo-controlled trials (CONQUER/SEQUEL), with a specific ambulatory-BP-monitoring RCT confirming sustained reductions.
- **Orlistat (XENDOS):** Modest weight loss with modest improvements in BP and reduced diabetes incidence over 4 years.
- **Sibutramine (SCOUT):** Despite producing weight loss, increased nonfatal MI and stroke in patients with preexisting cardiovascular disease — leading to market withdrawal.
- **Naltrexone-bupropion (LIGHT):** A cardiovascular safety trial terminated early after a data-confidentiality breach; interim results were reassuring but the trial could not establish cardiovascular safety with statistical confidence.
