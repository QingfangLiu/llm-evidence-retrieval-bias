## Bottom line

In adults with overweight/obesity and hypertension, several weight-loss medicines improve **weight and intermediate cardiovascular risk factors**, including blood pressure. The largest blood-pressure reductions in contemporary randomized evidence have been reported with **tirzepatide and semaglutide**, although antihypertensive medicines may need adjustment as weight and BP fall. ([PubMed][1])

Evidence that a medicine prevents actual heart attacks, strokes, or cardiovascular death is much narrower. **Semaglutide 2.4 mg is the only currently used obesity medication with a large randomized obesity trial demonstrating fewer major cardiovascular events**: in SELECT, events occurred in 6.5% with semaglutide versus 8.0% with placebo, HR 0.80. However, SELECT enrolled people who already had cardiovascular disease; it does not establish the same benefit in someone whose only cardiovascular condition is hypertension. Approximately 81.7% of SELECT participants had hypertension. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

This was a focused search for original clinical studies through July 18, 2026, not a formal systematic review. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, opinions, and editorials.

## Findings by medication

### Semaglutide 2.4 mg

**Hard cardiovascular outcomes:** SELECT randomized 17,604 adults with overweight/obesity, established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes. Over approximately 40 months, cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke occurred in 6.5% receiving semaglutide and 8.0% receiving placebo. Treatment discontinuation was more frequent with semaglutide, 16.6% versus 8.2%, largely reflecting tolerability. ([PubMed][3])

**Blood pressure:** Original exploratory analyses from STEP 1 and STEP 4 found greater SBP and DBP reductions with semaglutide than placebo, including among participants with controlled or uncontrolled hypertension at baseline. Participants receiving semaglutide were also more likely to reduce or discontinue antihypertensive medication. When semaglutide was withdrawn in STEP 4, BP and other cardiometabolic improvements deteriorated, indicating that continued treatment was generally needed to maintain benefit. ([PMC][4])

**Interpretation:** This is the strongest option studied if a person also has established cardiovascular disease. For uncomplicated hypertension without prior cardiovascular disease, evidence supports BP improvement but not yet proven prevention of cardiovascular events.

### Tirzepatide

A prespecified/post-hoc analysis of the randomized SURMOUNT-1 trial found a placebo-adjusted reduction at 72 weeks of **6.8 mm Hg systolic and 4.2 mm Hg diastolic**. Normal BP was present in 58.0% of tirzepatide participants versus 35.2% of placebo participants. Approximately 68% of the systolic and 71% of the diastolic effect was statistically mediated by weight loss. Hypotension-related adverse events were uncommon but more frequent with tirzepatide. ([PubMed][1])

Tirzepatide produced substantial weight loss in the original SURMOUNT-1 trial, but its dedicated morbidity-and-mortality obesity trial, SURMOUNT-MMO, remains active with no posted results. Thus, BP improvement is well supported, while prevention of heart attack or stroke in the general obesity population remains unproven by a completed outcomes RCT. ([PubMed][5])

### Liraglutide 3.0 mg

SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes randomized 3,731 adults; eligibility explicitly included people with treated or untreated hypertension. At 56 weeks, mean weight loss was 8.4 kg with liraglutide versus 2.8 kg with placebo. Systolic and diastolic BP decreased more with liraglutide, although resting pulse increased and gastrointestinal adverse effects were common. The trial was not powered to determine whether liraglutide prevented cardiovascular events. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

**Interpretation:** Liraglutide improves weight and BP, but it has less weight-loss efficacy and less direct obesity-dose cardiovascular-outcome evidence than semaglutide.

### Phentermine/topiramate extended release

In a randomized 24-hour ambulatory BP trial of 565 adults, approximately two-thirds had hypertension. At eight weeks, mean 24-hour SBP changed by −3.3 mm Hg with phentermine/topiramate, −0.1 mm Hg with placebo, and +1.4 mm Hg with phentermine alone. The placebo-adjusted SBP difference was −3.2 mm Hg. Heart rate nevertheless increased by approximately 3.6 beats/min relative to placebo. Participants with screening BP above 140/90 mm Hg were excluded, limiting applicability to uncontrolled hypertension. ([PubMed][7])

In an original analysis of hypertensive participants from CONQUER, greater medication-associated weight loss accompanied greater BP improvement; participants losing at least 5% of weight had SBP reductions of approximately 7.5–11.8 mm Hg. This analysis does not prove that the entire reduction was a direct drug effect because it stratified participants according to achieved weight loss. ([PubMed][8])

**Interpretation:** Average BP can decline despite the phentermine component, but heart rate may rise and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality have not been established.

### Orlistat

A pragmatic randomized trial enrolled 204 overweight/obese patients specifically with hypertension. At 12 weeks, weight fell by 3.7 kg with orlistat plus diet versus 2.0 kg with diet alone. DBP decreased by 11.4 versus 5.2 mm Hg, a significant additional reduction; the additional SBP reduction was not statistically significant. Glucose and cholesterol also improved more with orlistat. ([PubMed][9])

A separate six-month randomized trial included a prespecified hypertension stratum of 614 participants. Weight decreased by 6.2% with orlistat versus 1.9% with placebo; participants with early weight loss had the largest DBP improvements. Gastrointestinal adverse effects were more frequent. ([PubMed][10])

**Interpretation:** Orlistat provides modest weight and BP benefit and does not have stimulant effects, but there is no definitive trial showing fewer cardiovascular events.

### Naltrexone/bupropion

COR-I enrolled adults with obesity or overweight accompanied by hypertension or dyslipidemia. Naltrexone/bupropion produced greater weight loss, but BP initially increased by approximately 1.5 mm Hg before subsequently falling to only about 1 mm Hg below baseline. Thus, weight loss did not translate into the consistent BP reductions seen with incretin-based medicines. ([PubMed][11])

The 8,910-participant LIGHT cardiovascular-outcomes trial was terminated prematurely after confidential interim results were publicly released. At the time of termination, the data could not establish the prespecified level of cardiovascular safety; the investigators concluded that cardiovascular safety remained uncertain. ([PubMed][12])

**Interpretation:** This is generally a less compelling choice when improving hypertension is a major treatment objective, particularly if BP is difficult to control.

### Historical safety lesson: sibutramine

SCOUT randomized 9,804 high-risk adults after a lead-in period. Sibutramine caused additional weight loss, but BP remained approximately 1.2/1.4 mm Hg higher than with placebo. Major cardiovascular events occurred in 11.4% versus 10.0%, HR 1.16, including significantly more nonfatal myocardial infarctions and strokes. Sibutramine is no longer used, but the study demonstrates that weight loss alone does not guarantee cardiovascular benefit. ([New England Journal of Medicine][13])

## How to interpret this for hypertension

The evidence supports three separate conclusions:

1. **Blood-pressure improvement:** Tirzepatide, semaglutide, liraglutide, phentermine/topiramate, and orlistat can lower BP in people who lose weight. The average additional reduction ranges from modest to approximately 5–7 mm Hg systolic in the stronger modern trials. ([PubMed][1])
2. **Cardiovascular-event prevention:** Proven only for semaglutide 2.4 mg in people who already had cardiovascular disease, most of whom also had hypertension. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])
3. **Not every medication is favorable for BP:** Naltrexone/bupropion can raise BP initially, phentermine-containing treatment can increase heart rate, and historical sibutramine increased cardiovascular events. ([PubMed][11])

Most trials enrolled people with treated or reasonably controlled BP. These results should not be assumed to apply unchanged to severe or uncontrolled hypertension. Weight-loss medication also does not replace standard antihypertensive therapy; BP should be followed because antihypertensive doses sometimes need reduction as weight falls.
