## Bottom line

**Weight-loss medicines often reduce weight and modestly improve blood pressure, but improvement in clinical health outcomes is strongly drug-specific.**

In trials that specifically enrolled adults with hypertension, orlistat, topiramate, and phentermine/topiramate generally lowered diastolic or ambulatory systolic blood pressure in addition to producing weight loss. By contrast, sibutramine often increased pulse or ambulatory blood pressure despite weight loss and subsequently increased nonfatal cardiovascular events. ([PubMed][1])

Among newer incretin-based treatments, semaglutide and tirzepatide produce larger weight loss and placebo-adjusted blood-pressure reductions of approximately 4–7 mmHg systolic in broader obesity populations. However, relatively few of these trials required hypertension for enrollment, so their blood-pressure results are supportive rather than wholly hypertension-specific. ([PubMed][2])

For actual cardiovascular events, **SELECT provides the clearest beneficial evidence identified**: semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke from 8.0% to 6.5%, HR 0.80. Although SELECT was not restricted to hypertension, 81.7% of participants had a hypertension diagnosis, making it highly relevant to this population. ([PubMed][3])

### Interpretation of the hypertension-specific trials

The direct evidence supports a modest BP benefit from weight reduction with orlistat, topiramate, and phentermine/topiramate. Nevertheless, these studies were generally short, often included controlled rather than uncontrolled hypertension, and were powered for weight or BP rather than stroke, myocardial infarction, kidney disease, or mortality.

The sibutramine findings show why weight loss alone is not sufficient evidence of improved health: sympathomimetic cardiovascular effects can offset or reverse the expected benefit of losing weight.

## Broader obesity trials relevant to hypertension

### GLP-1 and GIP/GLP-1 medicines

**Liraglutide – SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes.** Among 3,731 adults with obesity, or overweight plus a condition such as hypertension or dyslipidemia, liraglutide produced a mean 8.4-kg loss versus 2.8 kg with placebo at 56 weeks and improved cardiometabolic risk factors. The trial was not a hypertension-only study. ([PubMed][10])

**Semaglutide – STEP 4.** After a semaglutide run-in, continued treatment rather than withdrawal produced an additional −7.9% versus a 6.9% weight regain. The placebo-adjusted change in systolic pressure was −3.9 mmHg, and physical-function scores also improved. Because only participants who tolerated and reached the maintenance dose entered randomization, this is an enriched randomized-withdrawal population. ([PubMed][2])

**Tirzepatide – SURMOUNT-1 BP analysis.** At 72 weeks, pooled tirzepatide treatment produced net placebo-adjusted reductions of 6.8 mmHg systolic and 4.2 mmHg diastolic. Normal BP at week 72 was observed in 58.0% versus 35.2% with placebo. Mediation analysis estimated that weight loss explained 68% of the SBP and 71% of the DBP effect. This was a post hoc analysis of individual randomized-trial data, not a meta-analysis. ([PubMed][11])

### Other agents

**Phentermine/topiramate – CONQUER and SEQUEL.** In adults with overweight/obesity and at least two comorbidities, including hypertension, the combination produced approximately 8–10% weight loss at one year, sustained to two years, along with improvements in BP and other cardiometabolic variables. These trials were not designed to establish cardiovascular-event benefit. ([PubMed][12])

**Naltrexone/bupropion – COR-I and COR-II.** The combination produced approximately 5–6% mean weight loss but had less favorable BP effects than medicines such as semaglutide or tirzepatide; small early increases or attenuation of expected decreases in BP were observed. These studies therefore support weight efficacy more clearly than hypertension benefit. ([PubMed][13])

## Clinical health outcomes rather than BP alone

### Evidence of benefit

**Semaglutide – SELECT.** In 17,604 adults with overweight/obesity, established cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes, MACE occurred in 6.5% with semaglutide versus 8.0% with placebo, HR 0.80, over a mean follow-up of 39.8 months. Hypertension was present in 81.7%. Treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was more common with semaglutide, 16.6% versus 8.2%. This is the strongest evidence that a medicine prescribed for weight management can improve major cardiovascular outcomes in a population highly enriched for hypertension. It does not, however, establish the treatment effect specifically within a separately randomized hypertension subgroup. ([PubMed][3])

**Orlistat – XENDOS.** In 3,305 adults with obesity, four years of orlistat plus lifestyle treatment reduced new type 2 diabetes from 9.0% to 6.2% and produced greater sustained weight loss. The diabetes benefit was concentrated in participants with impaired glucose tolerance. This is a clinically relevant metabolic outcome, but the population and result were not hypertension-specific. ([PubMed][14])

**Liraglutide – three-year SCALE analysis.** Among 2,254 adults with obesity and prediabetes, diabetes was diagnosed during treatment in 2% with liraglutide and 6% with placebo; time to diabetes was longer with liraglutide, HR 0.21. Withdrawal was substantial, and participants who withdrew were not followed after discontinuation. ([PubMed][15])

### Neutral, harmful, or unresolved cardiovascular findings

**Sibutramine – SCOUT.** In 9,804 randomized participants at high cardiovascular risk, sibutramine produced modest additional weight loss but less BP reduction than placebo. The primary cardiovascular outcome occurred in 11.4% versus 10.0%, HR 1.16, driven by increases in nonfatal myocardial infarction and nonfatal stroke. ([New England Journal of Medicine][16])

**Lorcaserin – CAMELLIA–TIMI 61.** Among 12,000 adults with overweight/obesity and cardiovascular disease or multiple risk factors, lorcaserin increased the proportion achieving at least 5% weight loss but did not reduce MACE: annual rates were 2.0% versus 2.1%, HR 0.99. Thus, favorable weight and risk-factor changes did not translate into cardiovascular superiority. ([PubMed][17])

**Naltrexone/bupropion – LIGHT.** The cardiovascular trial enrolled 8,910 high-risk participants but was stopped prematurely after inappropriate public release of interim results. The final available data could not establish the prespecified cardiovascular safety margin reliably, leaving the cardiovascular effect unresolved. ([PubMed][18])

**Rimonabant – CRESCENDO.** In 18,695 people with vascular disease or increased vascular risk, rimonabant did not reduce cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or stroke and caused more serious psychiatric adverse events. ([PubMed][19])

## Overall evidence-synthesis interpretation

1. **Blood pressure:** Weight-loss pharmacotherapy frequently lowers BP, particularly when it produces substantial weight loss. In modern trials, placebo-adjusted systolic reductions are commonly around 3–7 mmHg, although the effect varies by medication and population.

2. **Hard outcomes:** Reduced weight or BP should not be used as a surrogate for cardiovascular benefit across all drugs. SELECT showed cardiovascular benefit with semaglutide, whereas SCOUT showed cardiovascular harm with sibutramine and CAMELLIA showed cardiovascular neutrality with lorcaserin.

3. **Directness:** The most hypertension-specific RCTs are relatively small, short, and largely involve older agents. The strongest modern outcome evidence comes from broader obesity populations in which hypertension was common rather than mandatory.

4. **Likely synthesis strategy:** Separate studies into:

   * hypertension-required or hypertension-enriched trials;
   * mixed obesity populations reporting BP;
   * cardiovascular or metabolic outcome trials;
   * currently relevant versus historical agents.
