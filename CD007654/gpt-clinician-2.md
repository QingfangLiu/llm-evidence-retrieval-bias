## Bottom line

Weight-loss medicines can improve health in people with hypertension, but the strength and type of evidence differ substantially by drug:

* **Semaglutide and tirzepatide have the strongest evidence for lowering blood pressure**, commonly by about 4–10 mmHg systolic versus placebo, with some participants needing less antihypertensive medication.
* **Patient-important cardiovascular benefit has been demonstrated only in selected high-risk populations:** semaglutide in people with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and tirzepatide in people with obesity-related HFpEF.
* For patients with **uncomplicated hypertension without ASCVD or HFpEF**, trials mainly establish improvements in weight, blood pressure and metabolic risk factors—not yet fewer myocardial infarctions, strokes or deaths specifically among hypertensive patients.
* Effects should not be assumed to be a class effect. Naltrexone/bupropion has less favorable BP evidence, phentermine alone may increase BP and pulse, and the older drug sibutramine increased cardiovascular events.

I limited the evidence below to **individual randomized trials and analyses of individual trials**. I did not use systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines or opinion articles.

## Blood-pressure and antihypertensive-medication outcomes

### Semaglutide

An exploratory analysis of the individual STEP 1 and STEP 4 trials found that semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced BP in participants with both controlled and uncontrolled hypertension. In STEP 1:

* Controlled hypertension: SBP changed by approximately −6.4 mmHg with semaglutide versus −1.5 mmHg with placebo.
* Uncontrolled hypertension: approximately −4.7 versus +1.6 mmHg.
* DBP also decreased more with semaglutide.
* Antihypertensive treatment was more often reduced or discontinued with semaglutide.
* In STEP 4, BP and other cardiometabolic improvements deteriorated after participants were switched from semaglutide to placebo, indicating that benefit generally depends on continued treatment. ([PubMed][1])

These were subgroup and exploratory outcomes; participants were not separately randomized according to hypertension status.

### Tirzepatide

In a post hoc analysis of the individual SURMOUNT-1 RCT, pooled tirzepatide doses produced placebo-adjusted reductions at 72 weeks of:

* **SBP: −6.8 mmHg**
* **DBP: −4.2 mmHg**

Normal BP was present in 58.0% of tirzepatide-treated participants versus 35.2% with placebo. Approximately 68% of the SBP effect and 71% of the DBP effect was statistically mediated by weight loss. Hypotension-related adverse events were uncommon, although more frequent with tirzepatide. Effects were generally consistent across baseline BP and hypertension categories. ([PubMed][2])

A prospectively planned SURMOUNT-1 ABPM substudy provides stronger measurement evidence. At 36 weeks, placebo-adjusted 24-hour SBP reductions were:

* 5 mg: −7.4 mmHg
* 10 mg: −10.6 mmHg
* 15 mg: −8.0 mmHg

The effects did not appear to differ materially according to hypertension diagnosis or antihypertensive use. Heart rate increased modestly, particularly at 15 mg. The mean baseline ambulatory SBP was only about 125 mmHg, so applicability to severe or uncontrolled hypertension is less certain. ([PubMed][3])

### Phentermine/topiramate versus phentermine alone

A 2024 randomized ABPM trial enrolled 565 participants; approximately 68% had diagnosed or treated hypertension, but people with screening BP above 140/90 mmHg were excluded. At eight weeks:

* Placebo: 24-hour SBP −0.1 mmHg
* Phentermine 30 mg: **+1.4 mmHg**
* Phentermine/topiramate ER: **−3.3 mmHg**

The combination reduced SBP by 3.2 mmHg versus placebo and by 4.7 mmHg versus phentermine alone. Thus, topiramate-containing combination treatment may lower BP through weight loss, whereas phentermine alone did not show a BP benefit in this trial. The short duration and exclusion of uncontrolled hypertension are important limitations. ([PubMed][4])

In the 56-week CONQUER trial’s hypertension analysis, phentermine/topiramate-associated weight loss was accompanied by reductions in BP and cardiovascular risk factors among participants with baseline hypertension. However, some analyses categorized participants by achieved weight loss, which weakens causal interpretation because weight-loss achievement was not randomized. ([PubMed][5])

### Orlistat

A pragmatic RCT enrolled 204 patients specifically with hypertension and overweight or obesity. After 12 weeks:

* Weight loss: −3.7 kg with orlistat plus diet versus −2.0 kg with diet alone.
* SBP: −15.3 versus −11.6 mmHg; the between-group difference was not significant.
* DBP: −11.4 versus −5.2 mmHg; the additional DBP reduction was significant.
* Glucose and total cholesterol also improved more with orlistat.

The large BP fall in both groups suggests a substantial dietary, adherence or regression-to-the-mean effect; the incremental medication effect was clearer for DBP than SBP. ([PubMed][6])

### Naltrexone/bupropion

COR-I enrolled adults with obesity or overweight plus dyslipidemia or hypertension. It produced greater weight loss than placebo, but mean SBP and DBP initially increased by approximately 1.5 mmHg before falling to about 1 mmHg below baseline. This does not establish a reliable BP-lowering effect, particularly because the clinically important comparison is with placebo rather than baseline. ([PubMed][7])

The LIGHT cardiovascular-outcomes trial enrolled 8,910 high-risk participants but was stopped early after confidential interim results were publicly disclosed. At 50% of the planned events, the MACE hazard ratio was 0.88, but the wide confidence interval did not exclude the prespecified safety margin. The investigators concluded that cardiovascular safety remained uncertain because the trial was terminated prematurely. ([PubMed][8])

## Cardiovascular, heart-failure and functional outcomes

### Semaglutide in established ASCVD: SELECT

SELECT randomized 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity, established cardiovascular disease and no diabetes. Over a mean 39.8 months, cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI or nonfatal stroke occurred in:

* Semaglutide: 6.5%
* Placebo: 8.0%
* HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.72–0.90

This represents an absolute risk reduction of 1.5 percentage points, or an approximate NNT of 67 over 3.3 years. Treatment discontinuation because of adverse events was more common with semaglutide, 16.6% versus 8.2%. ([PubMed][9])

**Applicability:** This is the strongest evidence that an anti-obesity medicine can prevent major cardiovascular events in people without diabetes. However, it applies to patients who already have ASCVD—not to hypertension alone—and the publication does not establish a separate randomized treatment effect specifically among hypertensive participants.

### Semaglutide in obesity-related HFpEF: STEP-HFpEF

Among 529 participants with obesity and HFpEF, semaglutide improved:

* KCCQ clinical summary score by 7.8 points more than placebo.
* Six-minute walk distance by 20.3 metres more than placebo.
* Body weight by 10.7 percentage points more than placebo.

Serious adverse events occurred in 13.3% versus 26.7%. The trial was not powered primarily for mortality or HF hospitalization, but it established meaningful symptom and functional improvement in a condition strongly associated with hypertension and obesity. ([PubMed][10])

A parallel trial in 616 patients with HFpEF, obesity and type 2 diabetes also found improved KCCQ scores, weight and walking distance with semaglutide. ([PubMed][11])

### Tirzepatide in obesity-related HFpEF: SUMMIT

SUMMIT randomized 731 patients with obesity and HFpEF. Over a median 104 weeks, cardiovascular death or worsening HF occurred in:

* Tirzepatide: 9.9%
* Placebo: 15.3%
* HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41–0.95

The result was principally driven by fewer worsening-HF events; cardiovascular deaths were few and were not lower with tirzepatide. KCCQ health status improved by 6.9 points more than placebo. Gastrointestinal adverse events leading to discontinuation occurred in 6.3% versus 1.4%. ([PubMed][12])

This is convincing event and health-status evidence for patients with **obesity-related HFpEF**, but it should not be extrapolated directly to patients who have hypertension without heart failure.

## Practical interpretation for a hypertensive patient

For a patient whose main comorbid condition is hypertension, **semaglutide and tirzepatide provide the most consistent evidence for clinically meaningful BP improvement**, with tirzepatide having especially strong ABPM data. BP should be monitored as weight falls because antihypertensive doses may need reduction, particularly with diuretics or multidrug regimens.

When additional disease is present:

* **Established ASCVD:** semaglutide has direct evidence of fewer major cardiovascular events.
* **Obesity-related HFpEF:** tirzepatide has evidence of fewer worsening-HF events and improved health status; semaglutide has strong symptom and functional evidence.
* **No ASCVD or HFpEF:** expected benefit is currently supported mainly by weight loss, BP reduction, improved metabolic risk factors and possibly less antihypertensive medication—not by a hypertension-specific cardiovascular-outcomes trial.

Phentermine/topiramate may reduce BP when hypertension is controlled, but phentermine alone can increase ambulatory SBP and heart rate. Naltrexone/bupropion offers less reassuring BP evidence, and its definitive cardiovascular safety trial was inconclusive.
