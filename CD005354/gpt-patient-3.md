## Bottom line

Across individual randomized IVF studies, **standard daily recombinant FSH—usually follitropin alfa or beta—has not shown a consistent live-birth advantage over urinary FSH or highly purified hMG/menotropins**. The preparations can produce somewhat different ovarian responses:

* Compared with older urinary FSH, rFSH sometimes produces **more oocytes with fewer total international units or fewer stimulation days**, although later studies using highly purified urinary FSH found very similar oocyte and live-birth outcomes.
* Compared with highly purified hMG, rFSH often produces **slightly more oocytes**, while hMG has LH activity and in some trials produced a higher proportion of top-quality embryos. Pregnancy and live-birth rates were generally similar.
* Adding recombinant LH to rFSH **does not consistently improve outcomes for all patients**, including broadly defined poor responders. A possible benefit has appeared in selected women who unexpectedly respond inadequately to rFSH, but this has not been reliably translated into higher live-birth rates.
* Long-acting recombinant FSH analogues such as corifollitropin alfa can replace the first seven daily injections, with broadly similar pregnancy outcomes in the populations studied.

These findings should not be interpreted as showing that all preparations are interchangeable for every patient. Age, AMH, antral follicle count, previous stimulation response, PCOS/high-response risk and whether the clinic uses an agonist or antagonist protocol can materially change the choice.

## What the direct studies found

### 1. rFSH versus urinary FSH

In a large 1995 randomized trial of 981 women, follitropin beta produced more retrieved oocytes than urinary FSH—10.84 versus 8.95—and required a lower total FSH dose and a slightly shorter treatment period. Ongoing pregnancy rates were numerically higher with rFSH but were not statistically different. ([PubMed][1])

Later trials using **highly purified** urinary FSH showed less difference. Lenton and colleagues reported similar oocyte numbers, pregnancy outcomes and OHSS rates; live birth was 33.8% with rFSH versus 26.7% with urinary FSH, but the study was too small for this difference to establish superiority. ([PubMed][2])

In Baker and colleagues’ 152-patient trial, mean oocyte yield was 16.3 with highly purified urinary FSH and 17.1 with rFSH, while the live-birth rate was exactly 38.2% in both groups. ([PubMed][3])

**Interpretation:** Compared with older urinary products, rFSH appeared more potent per administered unit. With modern highly purified urinary FSH, meaningful pregnancy or live-birth differences have not been demonstrated.

### 2. rFSH versus hMG/menotropins

Highly purified hMG contains FSH plus LH activity, with much of the measured LH activity in contemporary products being hCG-derived.

In a 727-treated-patient phase III trial, ongoing pregnancy was 25% with highly purified hMG and 22% with rFSH; OHSS, miscarriage and tolerability outcomes were similar. The trial established hMG as noninferior, not superior, to rFSH. ([PubMed][4])

The 731-patient MERIT trial found that rFSH produced more oocytes—11.8 versus 10.0—but hMG produced a higher proportion of top-quality embryos—11.3% versus 9.0%. Ongoing pregnancy was 27% with hMG and 22% with rFSH; the difference was not sufficient to establish superiority, although noninferiority was established. ([OUP Academic][5])

The 749-patient MEGASET trial used a GnRH-antagonist protocol and compulsory single-blastocyst transfer. Fresh-cycle ongoing pregnancy was 29% with hMG and 27% with rFSH in the intention-to-treat population. Including frozen transfers within one year, cumulative live birth was 40% versus 38%. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** rFSH may recruit somewhat more oocytes, but that has not consistently produced more live births. hMG and rFSH have generally produced comparable ongoing-pregnancy and cumulative live-birth outcomes.

### 3. rFSH alone versus rFSH plus recombinant LH

In 431 generally normal-responding women, Marrs and colleagues found virtually identical mature-oocyte numbers with rFSH plus rLH and rFSH alone—10.3 versus 10.4. The study did not demonstrate a broad ovarian-response benefit from routine LH supplementation. ([PubMed][7])

A more favorable result appeared in a selected group of 130 women who had an unexpectedly inadequate initial response to rFSH. Adding 150 IU rLH produced more retrieved oocyte complexes than increasing the rFSH dose—9.0 versus 6.1. This supports LH as a possible rescue strategy for a specific hypo-response phenotype, but the study did not establish a live-birth advantage. ([PubMed][8])

Conversely, an 84-patient randomized trial in poor responders found no improvement in ovarian-response, implantation or ongoing-pregnancy outcomes from adding rLH. ([PubMed][9])

The much larger ESPART trial randomized 939 poor responders to combined rFSH/rLH or rFSH alone. Mean retrieved oocytes were 3.3 versus 3.6, and live-birth rates were 10.6% versus 11.7%, respectively. The combined treatment did not meet its superiority endpoint. A post hoc suggestion of benefit in moderate or severe poor responders remains hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. ([OUP Academic][10])

**Interpretation:** Routine LH supplementation has not been shown to improve live birth in unselected women or broadly defined poor responders. It may be considered in selected unexpected hypo-responders, but the evidence is less secure than for the basic FSH-versus-hMG comparisons.

### 4. Daily rFSH versus long-acting FSH

Corifollitropin alfa is a **long-acting recombinant FSH analogue**, rather than a different reproductive hormone. Its practical advantage is replacing seven daily FSH injections with one injection.

In the 1,506-patient ENGAGE trial, ongoing pregnancy was 38.9% after one corifollitropin injection versus 38.1% with daily rFSH. The long-acting treatment recruited slightly more follicles but was noninferior for ongoing pregnancy. ([PubMed][11])

In the 1,390-patient PURSUE trial of women aged 35–42, live birth was 21.3% with corifollitropin and 23.4% with daily rFSH; mean oocyte numbers and OHSS rates were also similar. ([PubMed][12])

A 2026 Chinese phase III trial in 474 women with normal ovarian reserve found 12.1 versus 10.9 oocytes with corifollitropin alfa N02 and daily rFSH. Ongoing pregnancy was 38.8% versus 44.3%, and OHSS occurred in 3.8% versus 2.5%; these differences did not establish pregnancy superiority for either treatment. Women with PCOS, previous OHSS or very high antral follicle counts were excluded, limiting application to high responders. ([ScienceDirect][13])

**Interpretation:** Corifollitropin can substantially reduce injection burden with broadly similar efficacy in appropriately selected patients. Because its first-week dose cannot be reduced after injection, clinics may be more cautious in women likely to overrespond.

### 5. Individualized rFSH formulations

Follitropin delta is itself an rFSH, but it is produced in a human cell line and ordinarily dosed using AMH and body weight.

In the 1,329-patient ESTHER-1 trial, individualized follitropin delta and conventional follitropin alfa produced similar live-birth rates—29.8% versus 30.7%—and similar mean oocyte numbers. Delta dosing produced more women in the target oocyte range, fewer excessive responses and fewer OHSS-prevention interventions. ([ScienceDirect][14])

A separate 1,009-patient Asian trial found live birth of 31.3% with individualized follitropin delta versus 24.7% with follitropin alfa, together with less early OHSS or preventive intervention. Because the difference was seen in one geographic trial and dosing strategy was inseparable from the drug comparison, it should not be assumed to apply universally. ([PubMed][15])

## Practical meaning for an IVF patient

The available direct trials suggest that **the choice of gonadotropin usually changes ovarian-response characteristics more reliably than it changes the chance of live birth**. Questions worth discussing with the reproductive endocrinologist include:

* Whether you are expected to be a low, normal or high responder based on AMH, antral follicle count, age and previous cycles.
* Whether the goal is maximizing oocyte yield, avoiding an excessive response, reducing injections or adding LH activity.
* Your individualized OHSS risk and the planned trigger, embryo-transfer and freeze-all strategy.
* Whether a proposed LH-containing regimen is based on a demonstrated previous hypo-response rather than being added routinely.

Most studies evaluated one stimulation cycle and frequently used ongoing pregnancy or oocyte number as the primary endpoint. Noninferiority or a nonsignificant result does not prove that treatments are exactly equivalent, particularly for uncommon safety outcomes or specific patient subgroups.
