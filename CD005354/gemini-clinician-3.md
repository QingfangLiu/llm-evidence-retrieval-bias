It is a nuanced clinical choice. When deciding between recombinant FSH (rFSH) and human menopausal gonadotropin (hMG or highly purified HP-hMG) for controlled ovarian stimulation, the tradeoff usually centers on oocyte quantity versus embryo quality.

Here is a breakdown of how they compare based on primary clinical studies.

## Clinical Comparisons in Ovarian Stimulation

### Oocyte Yield and Maturity

A consistent finding across trials is that rFSH drives a more robust follicular response than its urinary-derived counterparts. This generally translates to a higher total number of retrieved oocytes and a greater number of mature (MII) oocytes compared to hMG and HP-hMG (Andersen et al., 2006; Israeli et al., 2024). The relatively lower oocyte yield seen with hMG is often attributed to the presence of luteinizing hormone (LH) activity in its composition, which plays a role in the follicular atresia process during stimulation (Chapon et al., 2020).

### Embryo Quality and Endometrial Receptivity

While rFSH yields more eggs, treatments utilizing hMG or HP-hMG frequently result in a higher proportion of top-quality embryos. Beyond embryo grading, the choice of gonadotropin also influences the endocrine environment leading up to retrieval. Stimulation with HP-hMG is associated with lower late-follicular estradiol levels and a reduced incidence of premature progesterone elevation. This hormonal profile drives a more favorable, hyperechogenic endometrial pattern, which can positively impact receptivity for fresh transfers (Andersen et al., 2006).

### Clinical Pregnancy Rates

Because the higher oocyte yield from rFSH does not necessarily translate to a higher number of viable, top-quality embryos, the final pregnancy outcomes between the two gonadotropins remain remarkably similar. The landmark MERiT trial established that HP-hMG is clinically non-inferior to rFSH, noting an ongoing pregnancy rate of 27% for HP-hMG versus 22% for rFSH (Andersen et al., 2006). Subsequent randomized controlled trials utilizing different stimulation protocols, such as GnRH antagonist cycles, have continued to support that clinical pregnancy rates do not significantly differ between the two drugs (Chapon et al., 2020).
