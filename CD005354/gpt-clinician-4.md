## Scope

I restricted the main comparison to **human IVF/ICSI studies directly comparing rFSH with another gonadotropin regimen**:

* Recombinant FSH versus urinary or highly purified urinary FSH.
* Recombinant FSH versus hMG/highly purified hMG, which provides FSH plus LH bioactivity.
* Recombinant FSH alone versus rFSH supplemented with recombinant LH or hMG.

I excluded IUI-only studies, simple ovulation-induction studies, reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions, and editorials. This is a clinically oriented primary-study map rather than a formal systematic review.

## Clinical bottom line

**There is no consistently demonstrated live-birth advantage for rFSH over urinary FSH or HP-hMG.** Earlier trials often found that rFSH generated more follicles or oocytes, required fewer IU, or shortened stimulation, but pregnancy and live-birth differences were usually nonsignificant. ([PubMed][1])

Against **HP-hMG**, rFSH generally produces more oocytes, while HP-hMG produces a different steroidogenic and embryo-development profile. Large trials found HP-hMG noninferior for ongoing pregnancy and cumulative live birth. ([OUP Academic][2])

The most clinically notable safety signal comes from **predicted high responders**. In MEGASET-HR, women with AMH ≥5 ng/mL had similar cumulative live-birth rates with HP-hMG and rFSH, but OHSS was lower with HP-hMG: 9.7% versus 21.4%. This finding should not automatically be generalized to normal or poor responders. ([ScienceDirect][3])

Adding **recombinant LH** to rFSH has not shown a dependable benefit in unselected IVF patients or in all older/poor-responder populations. Some trials suggest possible usefulness in women with an unexpected inadequate response to rFSH or selected women aged approximately 35–39, but confirmatory live-birth evidence is inconsistent. ([PubMed][4])

## Findings by comparator

### 1. rFSH versus urinary or highly purified urinary FSH

Several older multicenter trials showed greater ovarian response with rFSH:

* **Out et al., 1995:** 981 women; follitropin beta produced more oocytes, with lower total FSH exposure and shorter stimulation. Ongoing pregnancy after the fresh attempt was not significantly different; pregnancy including frozen transfers was borderline higher with rFSH. ([PubMed][1])
* **Bergh et al., 1997:** 235 women; oocytes averaged 12.2 with rFSH versus 7.6 with HP-uFSH, with fewer treatment days and ampoules. Clinical pregnancy per started cycle was 45% versus 36%, not statistically significant. 
* **Frydman et al., 2000:** 278 women; rFSH produced more oocytes and embryos and required fewer treatment days and ampoules. Live-born children numbered 36 versus 33, not significantly different. ([OUP Academic][5])
* **Schats et al., 2000:** 496 women; rFSH shortened treatment and increased oocyte yield, but pregnancy and moderate/severe OHSS did not differ significantly. ([PubMed][6])

Other RCTs found little or no clinically important difference:

* **Lenton et al., 2000:** similar oocyte yield; live birth was 33.8% with rFSH and 26.7% with HP-uFSH, but the difference was not significant. ([PubMed][7])
* **Baker et al., 2009:** 152 women; no significant difference in oocytes, clinical pregnancy, or live birth. Live birth was exactly 38.2% in both groups. ([PubMed][8])
* **Sohrabvand et al., 2012, PCOS:** similar oocytes, embryo measures, clinical pregnancy, and OHSS. ([PubMed][9])

Subgroup results in older women were not consistent with the earlier “greater potency” pattern. A randomized study of women older than 39 found that urinary FSH required less total FSH and less FSH per oocyte, without differences in the other assessed outcomes. ([PubMed][10])

**Interpretation:** rFSH’s reproducible advantage is pharmacodynamic—often more oocytes per administered IU or a shorter stimulation—not clearly a greater probability of live birth.

### 2. rFSH versus hMG or highly purified hMG

The major trials consistently show different ovarian responses without a decisive efficacy winner:

* **Strehler et al., 2001:** 578 patients; rFSH retrieved more oocytes, but pregnancy after embryo transfer was 30.1% with rFSH and 32.3% with hMG. ([PubMed][11])
* **European and Israeli Study Group, 2002:** 781 enrolled; ongoing pregnancy was 25% with HP-hMG and 22% with rFSH in the per-protocol analysis. Safety, miscarriage, and OHSS were similar. ([PubMed][12])
* **Kilani et al., 2003:** 100 ICSI patients; HP-hMG required fewer treatment days and less gonadotropin. This was a relatively small single-study comparison. ([PubMed][13])
* **MERIT, Andersen et al., 2006:** 731 women undergoing IVF. rFSH produced more oocytes, 11.8 versus 10.0; HP-hMG produced a higher proportion of top-quality embryos in the local assessment. Ongoing pregnancy was 27% versus 22%, but HP-hMG superiority was not established; noninferiority was. ([OUP Academic][2])
* **MEGASET, Devroey et al., 2012:** 749 women using a GnRH-antagonist protocol and compulsory single-blastocyst transfer. Fresh ongoing pregnancy was approximately 29% with HP-hMG and 27% with rFSH; cumulative live birth was 40% versus 38%. HP-hMG was noninferior. ([PubMed][14])
* **MEGASET-HR, Witz et al., 2020:** 620 predicted high responders. Cumulative live birth was 50.6% with HP-hMG and 51.5% with rFSH. HP-hMG produced fewer oocytes but was associated with less OHSS and less early pregnancy loss. ([ScienceDirect][3])

**Interpretation:** For most IVF patients, choosing rFSH versus HP-hMG is more likely to change the **number of oocytes, endocrine profile, cost, injection characteristics, and excessive-response risk** than the ultimate cumulative live-birth probability.

### 3. rFSH alone versus LH-containing supplementation

Results depend strongly on the population:

* In a 431-patient ICSI trial, adding 150 IU recombinant LH from stimulation day 6 did not improve the number of MII oocytes or overall implantation. Subgroup observations in women ≥35 were hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. ([PubMed][4])
* In women with an initially inadequate response to 225 IU rFSH, adding rLH produced more cumulus–oocyte complexes than increasing the rFSH dose by another 150 IU. ([PubMed][15])
* A poor-ovarian-reserve RCT did not demonstrate improved top-quality embryo yield with rLH supplementation. ([PubMed][16])
* Among 240 women aged ≥35 using an antagonist protocol, live birth was 16.7% with rFSH/rLH and 17.5% with rFSH alone. ([PubMed][17])
* A smaller trial in women aged 35–39 reported higher implantation and live birth with mid-follicular rLH, but the sample included only 131 women and the finding has not been consistently reproduced. ([PubMed][18])
* In 610 normal responders, adding HP-hMG to rFSH improved fertilization and the proportion reaching fresh transfer, but did not improve implantation, clinical pregnancy, or OHSS. ([PubMed][19])

**Interpretation:** Routine LH supplementation for everyone is not supported by these individual trials. A possible role remains in carefully defined hyporesponders, but “poor responder,” older age, and low LH are not interchangeable indications.

## Important applicability limitations

Many of the influential studies used long GnRH-agonist suppression, cleavage-stage transfer, and transfer of two or three embryos. Those designs differ from contemporary antagonist protocols, GnRH-agonist triggering, routine single-blastocyst transfer, freeze-all strategies, and cumulative live-birth assessment. The later MEGASET and MEGASET-HR trials are therefore more applicable to current practice than many 1990s comparisons. ([PubMed][1])

Several pivotal studies also had substantial manufacturer involvement, including trials involving Organon/MSD or Ferring products. That does not invalidate the results, but it should be considered when interpreting noninferiority margins, selected outcomes, and subgroup analyses. ([PubMed][1])
