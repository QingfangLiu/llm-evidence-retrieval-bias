# Primary comparative studies of rFSH for IVF ovarian stimulation

## Scope

I conducted a targeted primary-study search through **July 26, 2026**, prioritizing randomized comparative trials in women undergoing IVF or ICSI.

Included comparisons were:

* Recombinant FSH versus urinary or highly purified urinary FSH.
* Recombinant FSH versus human menopausal gonadotropin/highly purified hMG.
* Recombinant FSH alone versus recombinant FSH plus recombinant LH or hMG.
* Separate, supplementary comparisons between conventional rFSH and newer recombinant FSH preparations.

I excluded ovulation-induction or IUI studies, trigger-agent comparisons, pharmacokinetic-only studies, systematic or narrative reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee opinions, and editorials. This is a **targeted evidence map rather than a formally exhaustive systematic review**.

## Main findings

### rFSH versus urinary FSH

Several of the large early randomized trials found that rFSH produced **more retrieved oocytes with a lower total FSH dose or shorter stimulation**, but generally did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in ongoing pregnancy. ([PubMed][1])

That pattern was not universal. Later studies found:

* No material difference in oocyte yield, clinical pregnancy, or live birth.
* Better drug efficiency with urinary FSH in some populations, including older women.
* Similar pregnancy outcomes in PCOS populations.

For example, Baker et al. reported an identical live-birth rate of **38.2% in both groups**, while Mohamed et al. found lower gonadotropin consumption per oocyte with urinary FSH among women older than 39 years. ([PubMed][2])

**Interpretation:** rFSH may have greater apparent biologic potency than older urinary preparations, particularly in the early product-development trials, but the individual studies do not establish a consistent live-birth advantage.

### rFSH versus hMG or HP-hMG

The large HP-hMG trials generally established **noninferiority or similar effectiveness for ongoing pregnancy and live birth**.

* The 2002 European–Israeli trial found ongoing pregnancy rates of 25% with HP-hMG and 22% with rFSH. ([PubMed][3])
* MERIT found more oocytes with rFSH—11.8 versus 10.0—but more top-quality embryos per retrieved oocyte with HP-hMG; ongoing pregnancy and live birth were not significantly different. ([OUP Academic][4])
* MEGASET found cumulative live-birth rates of 40% with HP-hMG and 38% with rFSH following fresh and eligible frozen transfers. ([PubMed][5])
* In predicted high responders, MEGASET-HR found similar cumulative live birth—50.6% versus 51.5%—but substantially less OHSS with HP-hMG: 9.7% versus 21.4% with rFSH. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** rFSH frequently produces more oocytes, but this does not consistently translate into more live births. HP-hMG may offer a safety advantage in high responders, although this finding comes predominantly from one large, manufacturer-sponsored trial in an AMH-selected population.

### Adding LH activity to rFSH

Trials of recombinant LH supplementation have not established a general live-birth benefit:

* Marrs et al. found no significant overall improvement in metaphase-II oocytes or implantation with rFSH plus rLH. ([PubMed][7])
* In women aged 35 years or older, Vuong et al. found live-birth rates of 16.7% with rFSH/rLH and 17.5% with rFSH alone. ([PubMed][8])
* In 939 poor responders, ESPART found similar oocyte numbers and live-birth rates: 10.6% with rFSH/rLH and 11.7% with rFSH. Apparent treatment interactions by poor-response severity were post hoc and should be treated as hypothesis-generating. ([OUP Academic][9])

**Interpretation:** Routine LH supplementation is not supported by a clear benefit in the direct randomized studies. Possible benefits in narrowly defined severe poor responders remain uncertain.

### Comparisons between recombinant FSH preparations

These are within-class comparisons rather than rFSH-versus-another-hormone comparisons, but they are relevant when interpreting contemporary stimulation practice.

Individualized follitropin-delta regimens generally produced similar pregnancy or live-birth outcomes with fewer excessive responses and fewer OHSS-related interventions than conventional follitropin-alfa dosing. However, those trials compared both a different molecule **and a different AMH/bodyweight dosing algorithm**. ([ScienceDirect][10])

ADAPT-1 reduced this confounding by using conventional starting doses for both products. It found the same mean oocyte yield—9.9 oocytes in each group—and similar clinical pregnancy and OHSS rates. ([PubMed][11])

## Issues important for evidence synthesis

**Outcome hierarchy matters.** Oocyte yield, gonadotropin consumption, estradiol concentration, fertilization, and embryo morphology are intermediate outcomes. A product can improve one or more of these without improving cumulative live birth.

**Older studies have limited relevance to current practice.** Many used long GnRH-agonist down-regulation, cleavage-stage transfer, multiple-embryo transfer, and fresh-cycle pregnancy endpoints rather than antagonist protocols, blastocyst transfer, freeze-all strategies, and cumulative live birth.

**Patient populations should not be combined indiscriminately.** High responders, PCOS populations, poor responders, women older than 39 years, oocyte donors, and conventionally responding IVF patients address materially different clinical questions.

**Some comparisons confound the molecule with the dosing strategy.** This is especially important for follitropin delta, where individualized AMH/bodyweight dosing is part of the intervention.

**Industry involvement should be extracted explicitly.** Several pivotal product-development trials reported manufacturer funding, company-employed investigators, or both, including the major HP-hMG and follitropin-delta programs. This does not invalidate the studies, but it belongs in risk-of-bias and applicability assessments. ([PubMed][6])
