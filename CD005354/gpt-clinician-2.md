# Primary-study evidence map

**Scope:** Direct comparative primary studies of controlled ovarian stimulation for IVF/ICSI, emphasizing randomized trials. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, committee opinions, editorials, IUI studies, and studies limited to non-IVF ovulation induction.

Abbreviations: **uFSH**, urinary FSH; **HP-uFSH**, highly purified urinary FSH; **hMG/HP-hMG**, menotropins; **rLH**, recombinant luteinizing hormone.

## Main conclusions

### 1. rFSH versus urinary FSH

Early randomized trials frequently found that rFSH produced **more retrieved oocytes with fewer ampoules or a shorter stimulation duration** than uFSH or HP-uFSH. However, the differences in clinical pregnancy, ongoing pregnancy, and live birth were usually small or statistically inconclusive. Later trials sometimes found essentially equivalent ovarian-response and live-birth outcomes. ([PubMed][1])

Examples include:

* **Out et al., 1995:** rFSH yielded more oocytes than uFSH—10.84 versus 8.95—and required less total FSH. Ongoing pregnancy was 22.2% versus 18.2%, a nonsignificant difference in the fresh cycle. ([PubMed][1])
* **Bergh et al., 1997:** rFSH yielded substantially more oocytes than HP-uFSH—12.2 versus 7.6—but clinical pregnancy per transfer was almost identical, 48% versus 47%. ([OUP Academic][2])
* **Frydman et al., 2000:** rFSH resulted in more oocytes and embryos than HP-uFSH, but the numbers of liveborn children were not significantly different. ([OUP Academic][3])
* **Schats et al., 2000:** rFSH produced more oocytes—13.1 versus 11.4—and required fewer ampoules; pregnancy per initiated cycle was 25.1% versus 20.1%, without a significant difference. ([PubMed][4])
* **Baker et al., 2009:** HP-uFSH and rFSH produced similar oocyte numbers and clinical pregnancy rates; live birth was 38.2% in both groups. ([PubMed][5])

**Interpretation:** The most reproducible advantage of rFSH over older urinary FSH preparations is stimulation efficiency or oocyte yield, not a demonstrated improvement in live birth.

### 2. rFSH versus hMG or HP-hMG

The direct trials generally show a trade-off rather than clear superiority:

* rFSH often produces **more oocytes**.
* HP-hMG produces a different endocrine response and, in some studies, favorable embryo-development or early-pregnancy findings.
* The better-powered trials generally show **similar ongoing pregnancy or cumulative live-birth rates**. ([PubMed][6])

Important trials include:

* **Strehler et al., 2001:** Among 578 randomized IVF/ICSI patients, rFSH produced more oocytes, but pregnancy rates after embryo transfer were similar—30.1% with rFSH and 32.3% with hMG. ([PubMed][6])
* **European and Israeli Study Group, 2002:** HP-hMG and rFSH had comparable ongoing-pregnancy and safety outcomes in 727 treated patients. ([PubMed][7])
* **Kilani et al., 2003:** In 100 ICSI patients, pregnancy rates per initiated cycle were 30% with HP-hMG and 28% with rFSH; pregnancy per transfer was 35% in both groups. ([PubMed][8])
* **MERIT, 2006:** Ongoing pregnancy was 27% with HP-hMG and 22% with rFSH. An associated embryo analysis found a higher proportion of top-quality embryos with HP-hMG under local assessment, although centralized assessment did not show the same clear difference. ([OUP Academic][9])
* **Bosch et al., 2008:** In an antagonist protocol, ongoing pregnancy was similar—35.0% with HP-hMG and 32.1% with rFSH—while rFSH yielded more oocytes, 14.4 versus 11.3. ([OUP Academic][10])
* **MEGASET, 2012:** With single-blastocyst transfer, fresh ongoing pregnancy was 29% with HP-hMG and 27% with rFSH; cumulative live birth was 40% versus 38%, meeting the trial’s noninferiority criterion. ([PubMed][11])
* **MEGASET-HR, 2020:** In women with AMH ≥5 ng/mL, cumulative live birth was almost identical—50.6% with HP-hMG and 51.5% with rFSH. OHSS occurred less often with HP-hMG, approximately 9.7% versus 21.4%, and early pregnancy loss was also lower. ([PubMed][12])

**Interpretation:** Across these primary trials, retrieving more oocytes with rFSH did not consistently translate into better cumulative live birth. In predicted high responders, the MEGASET-HR trial provides evidence that HP-hMG can produce a similar cumulative live-birth rate with less OHSS, although that conclusion is based principally on one large trial in a specifically selected high-AMH population.

### 3. Adding rLH to rFSH

Routine addition of rLH to rFSH has not consistently improved IVF outcomes in unselected women, women selected only by age, or broadly defined poor responders. ([PubMed][13])

* **Marrs et al., 2004:** Among 431 patients, rLH supplementation did not increase mature-oocyte yield or implantation overall. A subgroup aged ≥35 showed only a nonsignificant favorable trend. ([PubMed][13])
* **Humaidan et al., 2004:** Overall pregnancy did not differ in 231 randomized cycles. A post hoc age subgroup ≥35 had higher implantation and lower FSH consumption with rLH, but this was not the overall trial result. ([PubMed][14])
* **De Placido et al., 2005:** In women demonstrating an inadequate response during stimulation, adding rLH produced more oocytes than simply increasing the rFSH dose—9.0 versus 6.1. This is one of the stronger positive signals for rLH in a specifically defined hypo-response population. ([PubMed][15])
* **Barrenetxea et al., 2008:** Among 84 poor responders aged ≥40 or with elevated basal FSH, adding rLH did not improve ongoing pregnancy, implantation, ovarian response, or the other measured outcomes. ([PubMed][16])
* **Musters et al., 2012:** In women with expected poor ovarian reserve, rLH did not significantly improve the proportion of top-quality embryos or ongoing pregnancy; ongoing pregnancy was 13% versus 12%. ([PubMed][17])
* **Vuong et al., 2015:** In 240 women aged ≥35 treated with an antagonist protocol, live birth was 16.7% with rFSH plus rLH and 17.5% with rFSH alone. Oocyte yield, implantation, clinical pregnancy, and miscarriage were also similar. ([PubMed][18])

**Interpretation:** Age ≥35 or poor ovarian reserve alone has not reliably identified a population that benefits from rLH. The most plausible positive indication in the primary studies is an **unexpected inadequate follicular response during ongoing rFSH stimulation**, but that finding requires confirmation in trials powered for live birth rather than oocyte count.

## Important limitations

Many older trials used long GnRH-agonist suppression, fixed gonadotropin dosing, cleavage-stage or multiple-embryo transfer, and fresh-cycle pregnancy as the principal outcome. Their applicability to modern antagonist protocols, freeze-all strategies, blastocyst culture, PGT-A, and elective single-embryo transfer is therefore limited. ([PubMed][1])

Several studies were designed primarily around oocyte yield, gonadotropin consumption, embryo characteristics, or fresh ongoing pregnancy rather than cumulative live birth. Consequently, statistically significant differences in oocyte number should not automatically be interpreted as differences in the probability of ultimately having a liveborn infant. ([OUP Academic][2])
