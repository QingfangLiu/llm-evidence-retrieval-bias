## Summary of findings across primary studies

The overall picture from individual RCTs is a **modest, generally short-term benefit** for pain, with function less consistently improved:

- In an early Dutch trial, supervised exercise therapy from a physiotherapist (added to GP care) reduced pain and observed disability compared with GP care alone in patients with hip or knee OA over a 12-week program.
- A German RCT (THüKo) found that twelve weeks of exercise therapy in hip OA reduced pain and improved physical function, with pain reduction significantly greater in the intervention than control group, though there was no significant improvement in general health-related quality of life. A follow-up secondary analysis of the same cohort found that small-to-medium beneficial long-term effects persisted at 12 months in the group that received exercise first, though effects were less prominent when exercise was delayed and non-randomized, with differences partly explained by exercise adherence.
- A trial testing hydrotherapy and Tai Chi classes against a waiting-list control in 152 people with chronic hip or knee OA found mean improvements in pain and physical function at 12 weeks, concluding that access to either intervention produced large and sustained improvements in physical function for many older, sedentary participants. (Tai Chi is a land-based intervention; hydrotherapy is not.)
- The EMPART trial (hip OA specifically) compared exercise therapy alone, exercise plus manual therapy, and a waitlist control, using WOMAC function, pain, and quality-of-life outcomes.
- The more recent PHOENIX trial found that although both a resistance-only exercise program and a combined aerobic-plus-resistance program improved pain and function, adding moderate-intensity aerobic activity did not produce superior outcomes over resistance exercise alone.
- A single-blind Norwegian/Scandinavian trial (Fernandes et al.) compared supervised exercise plus patient education against education alone, specifically in people with hip OA.

Together, these individual trials generally support a small-to-moderate short-term reduction in pain from land-based exercise in hip OA, with effects on self-reported physical function being more variable and often smaller or non-significant — consistent with what your search strategy is likely aiming to characterize at the primary-study level before any pooling.
