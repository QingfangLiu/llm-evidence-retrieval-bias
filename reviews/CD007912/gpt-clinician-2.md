## Bottom line

**Land-based exercise can produce small improvements in pain and physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but results vary substantially among trials.** The clearest pattern is:

* Functional improvement is somewhat more consistent than pain reduction.
* Benefits are usually modest and may not reach a patient-important threshold.
* Exercise-only trials generally look more favorable than multimodal physiotherapy trials using credible sham controls.
* Evidence from studies combining hip and knee OA is less directly applicable to hip OA.

## Key primary-study findings

### Hip-OA-specific randomized trials

**Krauß et al., 2014 — THüKo trial**

In 209 adults, 12 weeks of hip-specific exercise was compared with no treatment and placebo ultrasound. Exercise improved WOMAC pain versus no treatment by 7.4 points and versus placebo by 5.1 points; comparable improvements occurred in WOMAC function. General SF-36 outcomes were largely unchanged. This is one of the more supportive hip-specific trials, although the absolute improvements were modest. ([PubMed][1])

**Tak et al., 2005**

This trial randomized 109 independently living adults aged 55 years or older to an eight-week strengthening/lifestyle program or control. Exercise produced a moderate short-term effect on pain, a small improvement in hip function, and small later improvements in self-reported disability and Timed Up and Go performance. Several other functional measures and quality of life did not improve. ([PubMed][2])

**Fernandes et al., 2010**

Among 109 adults with mild-to-moderate radiographic and symptomatic hip OA, supervised strengthening, functional and flexibility exercise was added to patient education and compared with education alone. There was **no significant improvement in WOMAC pain over 16 months**, but WOMAC physical function favored exercise, particularly at 10 and 16 months. ([ScienceDirect][3])

**Juhakoski et al., 2011**

This two-year RCT included 120 adults and compared exercise plus usual general-practitioner care with usual care alone. Exercise did not reduce WOMAC hip pain, but WOMAC function was better at six months by 7.5 points and at 18 months by 7.9 points. Performance tests and RAND-36 physical function did not differ significantly. ([PubMed][4])

**French et al., 2013 — EMPART**

This 131-person trial compared exercise, exercise plus manual therapy, and an initial waiting-list control. At nine weeks, the exercise groups improved in WOMAC outcomes, hip range of motion and patient-perceived change compared with waiting list. Adding manual therapy did not improve function or pain beyond exercise alone. The investigators questioned whether the magnitude of improvement was clinically meaningful. ([PubMed][5])

**Bennell et al., 2014**

In 102 adults with painful radiographic hip OA, a multimodal physical-therapy package—education, manual therapy, home exercise and gait advice—was compared with sham ultrasound and inert gel. It did **not** produce greater improvement in pain or function than sham treatment. Mild adverse effects were more frequent with active treatment. Because exercise was bundled with other treatments, this trial does not isolate exercise alone, but it is important as a rigorous placebo-controlled test of a typical physiotherapy program. ([PubMed][6])

**Thompson et al., 2020**

This pilot RCT randomized 31 adults with radiographic hip OA to a three-month resistance/aerobic program or wait list. Six-minute walk distance improved by a median 49 m with exercise versus 22 m with control, but the between-group difference was not statistically significant. The study was designed mainly to establish feasibility and was underpowered for definitive efficacy conclusions. ([PubMed][7])

**Wainwright et al., 2025 — CLEAT trial**

In 221 adults, an eight-week group cycling and education program was compared with usual individual physiotherapy. HOOS activities-of-daily-living scores improved 6.9 points more with cycling and education. This was statistically significant but narrowly below the prespecified 7.4-point minimum clinically important difference. No treatment-related serious adverse events occurred. Because the comparator often included exercise or physiotherapy, this evaluates one exercise-delivery model rather than exercise versus no exercise. ([PubMed][8])

### Trials with mixed hip and knee OA populations

**van Baar et al., 1998**

This primary-care RCT randomized 201 patients with hip or knee OA to usual medical management with or without 12 weeks of physiotherapist-delivered exercise. Exercise reduced pain in the previous week by 17 points and produced a small improvement in observed disability. Because hip-specific results were not the sole focus, applicability to hip OA alone is less certain. ([PubMed][9])

**Foley et al., 2003**

In 105 adults with hip or knee OA, participants received gym-based resistance exercise, hydrotherapy or no exercise for six weeks. The gym arm represents land-based strengthening and showed improvements in strength and aspects of physical function compared with control, although the mixed-joint population limits hip-specific inference. ([PubMed][10])

**Abbott et al., 2013 — MOA trial**

This factorial trial included 206 adults with hip or knee OA. At one year, exercise physiotherapy did not significantly improve the total WOMAC score compared with usual care—the adjusted difference was 16.4 points with a confidence interval crossing zero—but physical-performance outcomes favored exercise. Manual therapy performed better for the WOMAC outcome, while combining manual therapy with exercise did not add benefit. ([PubMed][11])

## Clinical interpretation

For an adult with symptomatic hip OA, a reasonable expectation is that a structured land-based program—usually combining progressive strengthening, functional exercise and aerobic activity—**may modestly improve function and may reduce pain**, particularly over the first two to four months. However, patients should not be told that substantial pain relief is assured.

The evidence does not identify one clearly superior regimen. Programs in the positive studies were generally supervised initially, progressive and continued at home. Cycling appears to be a reasonable low-impact option, but its advantage over good-quality usual physiotherapy was slightly below the trial’s patient-important threshold. ([PubMed][8])
