## Bottom line

**Land-based exercise can reduce pain and improve physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the average effects are generally modest and inconsistent.** Benefits are most apparent immediately after supervised programs lasting roughly 8–16 weeks. Functional outcomes—self-reported function, walking ability, strength, and timed performance—have been somewhat more consistently favorable than pain outcomes.

Several hip-specific trials reported clinically relevant or statistically significant short-term improvements, whereas other well-conducted trials found little or no advantage over education, usual care, or sham treatment. Longer-term benefits commonly attenuated after supervised treatment stopped. ([PubMed][1])

I treated WOMAC/HOOS physical function, Harris Hip Score, walking tests, timed performance, strength, and activities-of-daily-living measures as joint-function outcomes. Protocol-only publications and secondary analyses without relevant pain or function results were excluded.

### Interpretation of the hip-specific evidence

The positive trials support a **small short-term effect**, particularly for structured strengthening, functional exercise and supervised programs. However:

* Fernandes and Juhakoski found no convincing pain benefit.
* Teirlinck found only small, transient improvements.
* Bennell’s sham-controlled trial was negative for both pain and function.
* The positive long-term Svege report concerns function and time to joint replacement rather than a persistent pain effect and comes from the same participants as Fernandes.

Thus, the primary studies do not support a large or universally reproducible analgesic effect.

## Recent active-comparator studies

These studies help identify the preferred exercise format or place exercise in context, but **do not estimate exercise versus no exercise**, because every nonsurgical group received an exercise program.

* **Kjeldsen et al., 2024—PROHIP:** progressive resistance training versus neuromuscular exercise. This is principally an exercise-type comparison. ([PubMed][17])
* **Hall et al., 2025—PHOENIX:** combined aerobic and resistance training versus resistance training alone. It evaluates the additional value of aerobic exercise rather than exercise efficacy itself. ([PubMed][18])
* **Hall et al., 2025:** very-low-calorie diet plus exercise versus exercise alone in adults with overweight or obesity; adding diet did not significantly improve the principal hip-pain outcome. ([PubMed][19])
* **Frydendal et al., 2024:** total hip replacement versus resistance training in severe hip OA. Surgery produced much greater symptomatic improvement, although resistance training remained a feasible nonsurgical comparator. ([PubMed][20])
* **Talonpoika et al., 2026:** total hip arthroplasty plus rehabilitation versus self-directed exercise in moderate-to-severe OA. Exercise produced modest improvement on some measures, but surgery was substantially more effective; 38% of the exercise group crossed over to arthroplasty. ([PubMed][21])

## Overall evidence judgment

Based only on the individual primary studies:

**Pain:** Exercise probably provides a **small short-term reduction** for some adults with hip OA, but several trials found no significant benefit and durable pain relief is uncertain.

**Function:** Exercise probably provides a **small-to-moderate short-term improvement** in self-reported function or physical performance. This signal is somewhat more consistent than the pain signal.

**Durability:** Effects often diminish after supervision stops. Booster sessions, adherence and continued exercise may matter, but the evidence is not definitive.

**Disease severity:** People with moderate disease may obtain useful symptom and function improvements. In advanced OA eligible for arthroplasty, exercise is considerably less effective than total hip replacement, though it can remain an appropriate conservative treatment or preoperative strategy.
