The evidence from primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) regarding land-based exercise for hip osteoarthritis (OA) is mixed. While some trials demonstrate significant clinical benefits in reducing pain and improving physical function, other rigorous studies have found that exercise therapy performs no better than a placebo.

## Evidence Supporting Exercise Therapy

Several primary trials indicate that land-based exercise yields improvements over usual care or waitlist controls:

* **The EMPART Trial:** This multicenter RCT evaluated an 8-week exercise program for patients with hip OA. The results showed that exercise therapy significantly improved self-reported physical function, hip range of motion, and patient-perceived changes when compared to waitlist controls (French et al., 2013). Interestingly, adding manual therapy to the exercise regimen provided no additional benefit to these clinical outcomes.
* **The MOA Trial:** Evaluating both hip and knee OA patients, this factorial RCT found that multi-modal, supervised exercise physiotherapy provided significant physical performance benefits over usual care at a one-year follow-up (Abbott et al., 2013). Like the EMPART trial, combining exercise with manual therapy resulted in an antagonistic interaction, offering no added clinical benefit.

## Evidence Showing No Benefit Over Placebo

When land-based exercise is compared directly against a robust sham intervention, the specific effects of the exercise become less clear.

* A well-known trial investigated a 12-week multimodal physical therapy program—which included strengthening, stretching, and functional balance home exercises—against a sham treatment of inactive ultrasound and inert gel (Bennell et al., 2014). The trial found no significant between-group differences for improvements in pain or physical function, suggesting that the benefits of physical therapy interventions may be largely driven by the placebo effect or contextual factors of care.
