## Summary

Individual RCTs of land-based exercise in adults with hip OA generally show **modest benefit for pain**, with **more inconsistent effects on physical function**:

- **Fransen et al. (2001, hip-specific RCT)** — considered the only trial in early meta-analyses to enroll a hip OA–only sample; showed benefit and was rated the "silver-level" trial supporting exercise for hip OA pain and function.
- **Hoeksma et al. (2004)**, comparing manual therapy vs. exercise therapy in hip OA, found the exercise therapy program focused on active exercises to improve muscle function and joint motion, with manual therapy performing somewhat better on some outcomes, but both arms improved.
- **Bennell et al. (2014, JAMA)** — a well-powered, physiotherapist-delivered land-based exercise RCT in hip OA — found small but statistically significant improvements in pain, without meaningful improvement in quality of life.
- **The Dutch pragmatic trial (2015, Osteoarthritis and Cartilage)** — exercise therapy added to GP care in 203 hip OA patients — was a multicenter parallel pragmatic randomized controlled trial in 120 general practices in the Netherlands designed to assess whether adding exercise therapy to GP care improved outcomes compared with GP care alone.
- **A separate Finnish/Scandinavian pragmatic RCT (Pubmed 21078702)** found no differences between the exercise and control groups with respect to WOMAC hip pain, physical functioning, performance-based outcomes, or BMI, illustrating the mixed evidence base.
- **THüKo trial (Fransen-style German RCT)** — 12 weeks of land-based exercise therapy vs. no treatment and placebo ultrasound — found pain reduction was significantly greater in the intervention than in the control group, and statistically significant differences were found for pain reduction between the intervention and control group as well as between the intervention and placebo group, with comparable improvements for physical function, though no improvement in general health-related quality of life.

Overall, these primary studies collectively support a **small-to-moderate benefit for pain**, with **function results more variable across trials** — consistent with what later meta-analyses of this same primary literature (which you asked me to exclude, but which synthesize these exact trials) have reported.
