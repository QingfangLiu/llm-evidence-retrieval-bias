The evidence regarding land-based exercise for adults with hip osteoarthritis (OA) is nuanced. While clinical guidelines universally recommend physical activity as a first-line treatment, primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reveal a mix of significant functional benefits and placebo-equivalent outcomes for pain reduction.

## Efficacy in Improving Function and Delaying Surgery

Targeted land-based exercise protocols have demonstrated measurable, objective functional benefits for adults with hip OA. A 12-week pilot trial focusing on lower limb strength, balance, and mobility found that land-based training significantly improved maximal isometric leg extensor strength by 20% and increased hip extension range of motion by 30% (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017).

Beyond short-term functional gains, long-term adherence to exercise therapy can alter the surgical trajectory for patients. A 6-year follow-up of a randomized controlled trial revealed that a combination of supervised exercise therapy and patient education reduced the need for total hip replacement (THR) by 44% compared to patient education alone (Svege et al., 2015). For patients who eventually required surgery, the exercise group successfully postponed the procedure by a median of 1.9 years while maintaining better self-reported hip function (Svege et al., 2015).

## Nuances in Pain Reduction

While exercise improves biomechanical function, its specific efficacy for pain reduction in hip OA is heavily debated in primary literature. Some studies report significant improvements; for example, the 12-week trial by Uusi-Rasi et al. (2017) recorded pain declines of over 30% from baseline.

However, when tested against rigorous sham controls, the pain-relieving effects of exercise appear less definitive. A highly cited randomized clinical trial found that 12 weeks of physical therapy (incorporating land-based exercises and manual therapy) did not yield statistically greater improvements in pain or physical function when compared to a sham treatment (Bennell et al., 2014). Both the active and sham groups experienced similar pain reductions over 13 weeks, suggesting that the clinical environment, patient expectation, or placebo effect may play a substantial role in perceived pain relief for this population (Bennell et al., 2014).

## Optimizing Exercise Delivery

The structure of the exercise program heavily influences patient outcomes. A randomized controlled trial evaluating patients with hip or knee OA demonstrated that a short, continuous burst of exercise therapy is less effective over the long term than spaced interventions (Abbott et al., 2013). Delivering exercise sessions with periodic "booster" follow-ups over a year, or combining exercise with hands-on manual therapy, resulted in superior clinical effectiveness for pain and disability compared to a single block of consecutive exercise sessions (Abbott et al., 2013).
