Yes, the primary literature strongly supports land-based exercise as an effective intervention for adults with hip osteoarthritis. Individual randomized controlled trials (RCTs) show that structured land-based exercise programs provide meaningful short-term improvements in both pain and joint function, and can significantly delay the need for surgical intervention in the long term.

Here is a breakdown of the findings from key primary studies, focusing exclusively on clinical outcomes rather than systematic reviews or guidelines.

## Short-Term Benefits on Pain and Function

Supervised land-based exercise programs—typically spanning 8 to 12 weeks and focusing on muscle strengthening, aerobic conditioning, and active range of motion—yield statistically and clinically significant improvements in the short term.

* **Improved Physical Function and Range of Motion:** The EMPART trial (French et al., 2013) evaluated an 8-week structured exercise therapy program and found significant improvements in self-reported physical function, hip range of motion, and patient-perceived overall improvement. Notably, this trial also tested the addition of manual therapy but found that while it increased patient satisfaction, it did not provide additional functional benefits over exercise alone.
* **Pain Reduction and Objective Strength:** A 12-week pilot RCT (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017) focusing on strength and mobility training demonstrated that exercise safely reduced joint pain by over 30% from baseline. Objective functional assessments in the same study showed that the exercise group achieved a 20% improvement in maximal isometric leg extensor strength and a 30% improvement in hip extension range of motion.
* **Disease-Specific Pain Scores:** The THüKo trial (Krauß et al., 2014) compared a 12-week exercise intervention (combining group classes and home exercises) against a control group and a placebo ultrasound group. The exercise cohort achieved significantly greater pain reduction and functional improvement on the WOMAC osteoarthritis index compared to both control arms, confirming the specific efficacy of the physical loading and mechanical joint benefits of the exercise program.

## Long-Term Outcomes and Joint Preservation

While osteoarthritis is a progressive disease and pain scores often slowly baseline over several years regardless of conservative intervention, long-term follow-ups of primary trials show that early exercise therapy significantly alters the surgical trajectory.

* **Postponement of Total Hip Replacement (THR):** A landmark 6-year follow-up of a randomized trial (Svege et al., 2015) investigated the survival of the native hip in patients who underwent exercise therapy combined with patient education versus education alone. The study found that the addition of exercise therapy **reduced the need for total hip replacement by 44%** over the 6-year period. While self-reported pain eventually equalized between the two groups as the disease progressed, the exercise group maintained significantly better self-reported hip function right up to the point of surgery or the end of the study.
