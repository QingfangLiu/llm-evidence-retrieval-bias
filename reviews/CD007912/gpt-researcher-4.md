## Bottom line

Taken study by study, **land-based exercise can reduce pain and improve physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the effects are generally modest, heterogeneous, and more consistent immediately after treatment than over longer follow-up**.

The most directly applicable hip-only randomized trials show:

* **Positive pain and function findings:** Tak 2005, French 2013, Krauß 2014, and the short-term assessment in Teirlinck 2016.
* **Function improvement without clear pain improvement:** Fernandes 2010 and Juhakoski 2011.
* **No superiority over a credible sham intervention:** Bennell 2014.
* **Inconclusive pilot evidence:** Thompson 2020.

Thus, exercise is not uniformly effective in every trial. Self-reported function appears somewhat more likely to improve than pain, while sustained effects beyond the supervised treatment period are uncertain. In these studies, “joint function” generally means self-reported activity limitations—usually WOMAC or HOOS function/ADL—and performance tests, rather than structural modification of the hip joint.

## Scope

I prioritized randomized controlled trials enrolling adults with clinically or radiographically diagnosed hip OA and evaluating structured land-based exercise. I separated:

1. Hip-specific exercise versus nonexercise, education, usual-care, wait-list, or sham comparators.
2. Preoperative studies in advanced hip OA.
3. Mixed hip-and-knee OA trials, which provide less direct evidence when hip results were not reported separately.
4. Exercise-versus-exercise trials, which inform program selection but cannot establish whether exercise is better than no exercise.

Aquatic-only treatment, postoperative-only rehabilitation, protocols, reviews, guidelines, opinions, and editorials were excluded. The search was updated through **July 26, 2026**.

### Interpretation of the hip-specific evidence

The direct evidence is **not uniformly positive**. Exercise performed better than no treatment or wait-list in several trials, but effects were smaller or absent against education, active usual care, or a credible sham intervention. This comparator pattern suggests that attention, reassurance, education, treatment expectations, and nonspecific activity changes may account for part of the observed improvement.

The best-supported conclusion is therefore:

> **Structured land-based exercise probably produces a modest short-term improvement in physical function and may reduce pain for some adults with hip OA, but pain effects are inconsistent and durable superiority over credible control treatment has not been established consistently.**

## 3. Mixed hip-and-knee OA trials

These are relevant primary studies, but their applicability is limited when outcomes are pooled across joints.

* **van Baar 1998:** In 201 participants, 12 weeks of physiotherapist-directed exercise plus GP care reduced pain and improved observed disability compared with GP care. Hip-specific estimates were not reported separately. ([PubMed][14])
* **Hopman-Rock and Westhoff 2000:** A six-week education-and-exercise program improved pain, quality of life, strength, and some activity outcomes in 105 older adults, with attenuated effects at six months. Hip-specific results were not isolated. ([Amsterdam UMC][15])
* **Foley 2003:** Among 105 participants, gym-based strengthening improved quadriceps strength, walking speed, and self-efficacy compared with control, but did not demonstrate a clear WOMAC pain or function advantage. ([PubMed][16])
* **Fransen 2007:** Tai Chi improved WOMAC physical function compared with wait-list; the pain estimate favored Tai Chi but its confidence interval included no effect. The sample included both hip and knee OA. ([PubMed][17])
* **Abbott 2013:** Exercise therapy improved physical performance in a mixed hip/knee population, but the adjusted WOMAC estimate versus usual care was imprecise. Combining exercise and manual therapy did not produce an additive benefit. ([PubMed][18])
* **Saw 2016:** Exercise, education, and relaxation improved pain in adults awaiting hip or knee arthroplasty, without a joint-specific analysis. ([PMC][12])

For a hip-OA-only synthesis, these trials should be coded as **indirect evidence** unless investigators provide usable hip-subgroup data.

---

## 4. Trials comparing different land-based exercise approaches

These studies address **which program is preferable**, not whether exercise itself works compared with nonexercise care.

* **Bieler 2017:** Nordic walking, strength training, and unsupervised home exercise all involved active exercise. Nordic walking generally produced better performance outcomes than the other programs, although not every primary comparison differed. ([PubMed][19])
* **Fukumoto 2013/2017:** High- versus low-velocity resistance training produced some differing performance or gait changes, but no consistent superiority across pain and functional outcomes. ([PubMed][20])
* **Østerås 2017:** In a 33-person pilot trial, high-dose medical exercise was not superior to lower-dose exercise, although both groups improved from baseline. ([PubMed][21])
* **Kjeldsen 2024:** In 160 adults, 12 weeks of progressive resistance training was not superior to neuromuscular exercise for chair stands, HOOS pain, or other main outcomes. ([PubMed][22])
* **PHOENIX 2025:** Adding aerobic physical activity to resistance exercise did not further improve pain or function. Both groups improved substantially, but without a nonexercise comparator those changes cannot be attributed solely to exercise. ([PubMed][23])
* **CLEAT 2025:** Group cycling plus education improved HOOS ADL more than usual physiotherapy by 6.9 points, 95% CI 2.5–11.2. This was just below the trial’s prespecified 7.4-point clinically important difference. ([PubMed][24])
* **Kjeldsen 2025 booster trial:** Four exercise booster sessions after an initial supervised program did not clearly establish one initial exercise type as superior; this report principally informs maintenance strategies. ([PubMed][25])

Overall, there is no convincing indication that one conventional format—resistance, neuromuscular, aerobic-plus-resistance, or high-dose exercise—is consistently best for both pain and function.

## Evidence-synthesis considerations

* **Do not double-count companion publications.** The Krauß 2014 and Steinhilber 2017 reports arise from the same THüKo trial; the Fukumoto publications are companion analyses; and the Kjeldsen 2024 and 2025 reports concern linked phases of the same trial program.
* **Separate bundled interventions.** Bennell, Saw, CLEAT, and some other trials combine exercise with education, manual therapy, relaxation, or other care. Their results estimate the package, not exercise alone.
* **Comparator choice matters.** Wait-list and no-treatment comparisons generally produced more favorable results than education, usual physiotherapy, or sham comparisons.
* **Separate perioperative populations.** Advanced OA awaiting arthroplasty differs substantially in severity, prognosis, and potential treatment ceiling.
* **Avoid treating within-group improvement as efficacy.** This is particularly important in exercise-versus-exercise trials in which both groups improved.
* **Clinical importance is uncertain.** Several statistically significant differences were small, and not all met study-specific thresholds for meaningful improvement.
