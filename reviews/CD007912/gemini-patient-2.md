Based on individual primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating land-based exercise for hip osteoarthritis, the evidence shows a divergence between functional outcomes and pain relief. While structured exercise programs consistently improve physical function and joint mobility, their ability to provide significant, long-term pain reduction is more mixed when looking strictly at individual trials.

## Impact on Physical Function and Mobility

Land-based exercise—including aerobic and strength training—demonstrates clear, measurable benefits for improving how well the hip joint functions.

* **Short-term improvements:** An 8-week multicenter RCT found that participants undergoing an exercise therapy program experienced notable improvements in self-reported physical function and hip range of motion compared to a waitlist control group (French et al., 2013).
* **Long-term improvements:** A two-year trial evaluating a combination of supervised and home-based exercise showed statistically significant improvements in physical functioning (measured via the WOMAC index) at both 6-month and 18-month check-ins (Juhakoski et al., 2010).
* **Trend toward better mobility:** A pilot study tracking a 3-month aerobic and strengthening program noted that the exercise group doubled their distance in a 6-minute walk test and their physical function scores compared to controls. While small sample sizes prevented this from reaching statistical significance, the clinical trend strongly favored exercise for functional gains (Thompson et al., 2019).

## Impact on Pain Reduction

While systematic reviews often aggregate data to show pain benefits, individual primary trials highlight that isolated pain reduction is harder to achieve or sustain than functional improvements.

* **Slight but non-significant reductions:** In the 3-month pilot RCT, pain scores decreased slightly for those doing aerobic and strength exercises, but the reduction was not statistically different from the control group (Thompson et al., 2019).
* **Lack of long-term pain relief:** The two-year longitudinal study found that despite the documented improvements in physical function, the exercise program did not result in significantly reduced hip pain over the extended follow-up period compared to standard general practitioner care alone (Juhakoski et al., 2010).

*(Note: Even when isolated pain scores do not drop significantly, the simultaneous improvements in function and range of motion often lead to better overall patient-perceived outcomes and satisfaction with the therapy (French et al., 2013).)*
