# Clinical bottom line

**Land-based exercise can reduce pain and improve function in some adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the effects in individual trials are inconsistent and usually modest.** Trials comparing exercise with no treatment or a waiting list are more often positive; trials using education, usual physiotherapy, or a credible sham comparator are more mixed. Function appears somewhat more consistently responsive than pain, although neither outcome improves uniformly.

For counseling, an **8–12-week individualized exercise trial** is reasonable, with expectations framed around possible modest improvements in walking, activities of daily living, strength, or confidence rather than assured pain relief. The studies assess symptoms and clinical function—not structural modification of the hip joint.

### Longer-term finding from the Fernandes trial

At approximately six years, fewer participants randomized to exercise plus education had undergone total hip replacement than those receiving education alone: 22 versus 31 patients; native-hip survival was 41% versus 25%, hazard ratio 0.56. Function favored exercise before surgery or final follow-up, but pain and stiffness did not. This was an extended outcome from the same 109-person trial and should not be interpreted as independent confirmation. ([ScienceDirect][10])

## Does exercise type matter?

The comparative trials do not identify one consistently superior program:

* High-velocity resistance exercise produced a small additional Timed Up and Go improvement compared with slow-velocity exercise, but most pain and function outcomes did not differ. ([PubMed][11])
* Nordic walking generally improved performance more than supervised strength training or unsupervised home exercise, although chair-stand improvement was initially similar across groups. ([PubMed][12])
* A small high-dose-versus-low-dose trial found no between-group difference in pain or WOMAC function. ([PubMed][13])
* Progressive resistance training and neuromuscular exercise produced nearly identical chair-stand and HOOS pain improvements at 12 weeks. ([PubMed][14])
* Adding moderate-intensity aerobic activity to resistance exercise did not improve pain or function more than resistance exercise alone; both groups improved by approximately 2.2–2.4/10 in pain and 7–9 WOMAC function points. ([PubMed][15])

Thus, **patient preference, access, comorbidities, baseline capacity and likelihood of continued participation are reasonable determinants of exercise selection**. Current primary trials do not justify insisting that strengthening, neuromuscular exercise, walking, or cycling is universally best.

Mixed hip-and-knee trials provide supportive but less directly applicable evidence. Van Baar found less pain and observed disability overall with exercise in a combined hip/knee population, whereas Abbott’s factorial hip/knee trial did not establish a statistically significant WOMAC benefit for exercise alone over usual care. Hip-specific effects cannot be confidently extracted from their pooled results. ([PubMed][16])

## Practical interpretation

A reasonable way to present the evidence is:

> “Exercise has a meaningful chance of improving your ability to walk and perform daily activities and may reduce pain, but the average benefit is modest and some people experience little change. We will reassess after approximately 8–12 weeks and modify the program if it repeatedly aggravates symptoms.”

Pain during or after exercise does not automatically mean joint injury, but repeated or sustained exacerbation warrants adjustment of load, range, frequency or exercise type. Mild symptom flares occurred in some trials, while treatment-related serious events were uncommon in the more recent studies that reported them. ([JAMA Network][6])
