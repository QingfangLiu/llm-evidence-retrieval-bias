## Bottom line

Based only on individual primary studies, **land-based exercise can improve symptoms in some adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the average benefit is generally small and inconsistent**.

* **Pain:** Results are mixed. Several trials found little or no statistically significant pain benefit, particularly at longer follow-up or when exercise-based physiotherapy was compared with a credible sham intervention.
* **Physical function:** The signal is somewhat more favorable. Several trials reported small improvements in self-reported function, but benefits were sometimes limited to early or selected follow-up points and were not always accompanied by better objective performance.
* **Comparator matters:** Benefits appear more consistently when exercise is compared with waiting-list, no treatment, or usual care. Sham-controlled trials conflict: one found modest benefits, while another well-conducted trial found no advantage for a multimodal physiotherapy program.
* **Clinical expectation:** Exercise is reasonable to try, but patients should not be promised large or durable pain relief. A meaningful individual response is more important than the small average treatment effect seen across trials.

### Longer-term follow-up

Svege and colleagues followed the Fernandes trial cohort. Education plus exercise produced a better overall WOMAC function trajectory, but not a statistically significant pain difference. Over approximately six years, fewer participants underwent total hip replacement in the exercise group, although arthroplasty was a secondary long-term outcome and the study was not blinded. ([PMC][9])

## What newer active-comparator trials add

These studies help with exercise selection but **cannot establish whether exercise works better than no exercise**, because both groups received active treatment.

* **CLEAT, 2025; n=221:** A cycling-and-education program produced a 6.9-point greater improvement in HOOS activities of daily living than usual physiotherapy. This was statistically significant but slightly below the trial’s prespecified 7.4-point threshold for a clinically important difference. ([ScienceDirect][10])
* **PHOENIX, 2025; n=196:** Adding aerobic physical activity to resistance exercise did not improve pain or function more than resistance exercise alone. Both groups improved, but the absence of a nonexercise control prevents attributing the improvement specifically to exercise. ([PubMed][11])

## Interpretation for clinical practice

The most defensible conclusion is:

> **Land-based exercise may provide a small improvement in function and, less consistently, pain. Large analgesic effects are unlikely at the group level, and benefits are not reliably sustained after treatment.**

An evidence-aligned clinical trial of exercise would generally last about 8–12 weeks and include progressive strengthening, functional exercise and possibly aerobic, balance or neuromuscular components. Assess pain and function before and after the trial—using the same pain scale and a measure such as HOOS or WOMAC—and continue or adapt the program according to the patient’s actual response.

The evidence does **not** clearly identify a single superior land-based program. The PHOENIX trial suggests that adding aerobic exercise to an adequate resistance program does not necessarily produce additional pain or function benefit, while CLEAT suggests that a structured group cycling-and-education format may be somewhat more effective than usual physiotherapy for activities of daily living. ([PubMed][11])
