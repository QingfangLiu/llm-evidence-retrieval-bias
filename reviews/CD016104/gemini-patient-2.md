Navigating a lung cancer diagnosis and weighing your surgical and treatment options is a lot to handle, but gathering the facts from primary research is a great step.

When treating localized (resectable) non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), oncologists now frequently use **immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)**—drugs like nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, and durvalumab that help your immune system recognize and attack cancer cells.

Depending on the treatment plan, these can be given:

* **Neoadjuvant:** Before surgery (usually alongside chemotherapy).
* **Adjuvant:** After surgery (often following standard chemotherapy).
* **Perioperative:** Both before *and* after surgery.

Based on the primary Phase 3 clinical trials—which are the large, randomized studies that establish current medical standards—here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms of using ICIs compared to just using standard chemotherapy or a placebo.

## Benefits of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

**1. Higher Chance of Wiping Out the Tumor Before Surgery**
When ICIs are given *before* surgery (neoadjuvant or perioperative), they significantly increase the likelihood of a "pathologic complete response." This means that when the surgeon removes the lung tissue, the pathologist finds zero active cancer cells left. For example, adding the ICI nivolumab to standard chemotherapy before surgery resulted in a 24% pathologic complete response rate, compared to just 2.2% with chemotherapy alone (Forde et al., 2022). Similar high responses have been shown with durvalumab (Heymach et al., 2023) and other ICIs (Cascone et al., 2024).

**2. Delaying or Preventing Cancer Recurrence**
The primary goal of adding ICIs is to prevent the cancer from coming back. The trials consistently show that adding an ICI extends "event-free survival" or "disease-free survival":

* **After surgery (Adjuvant):** Giving atezolizumab or pembrolizumab after surgery and standard chemotherapy significantly reduces the risk of the cancer returning compared to supportive care or a placebo (Felip et al., 2021; O’Brien et al., 2022).
* **Before/Around surgery (Perioperative):** Using ICIs both before and after surgery also significantly extends the time patients live without the disease progressing or returning (Cascone et al., 2024; Heymach et al., 2023).

**3. Overall Survival**
While data on total lifespan is still maturing for some of the newer trials, early results strongly suggest that adding ICIs helps patients live longer overall. For instance, the KEYNOTE-671 trial found a significant overall survival benefit for patients who received perioperative pembrolizumab compared to those who only received chemotherapy and surgery (Spicer et al., 2024).

## Harms and Risks

**1. Immune-Related Side Effects (irAEs)**
Because ICIs work by taking the brakes off your immune system, they can sometimes cause the immune system to mistakenly attack healthy organs. This can lead to inflammation in the lungs (pneumonitis), colon (colitis), skin (rash), or disrupt the thyroid gland. Across the trials, severe (Grade 3 or 4) treatment-related adverse events occurred in roughly 30% to 45% of patients taking ICIs, and severe immune-related specific events occurred in a smaller fraction (Felip et al., 2021; Forde et al., 2022; O’Brien et al., 2022).

**2. General Toxicity and Fatigue**
Adding ICIs to chemotherapy can compound general side effects like fatigue, nausea, and changes in blood counts. As an older adult, this is a crucial consideration. While these trials successfully treated older patients, severe fatigue and toxicity can sometimes hit older adults harder, temporarily impacting daily energy and quality of life more heavily than in younger patients.

**3. Surgical Delays or Complications**
A major historical fear of giving treatments *before* surgery was that side effects might make the patient too sick to operate on, or that the surgery would become more difficult. Fortunately, the neoadjuvant trials proved this fear largely unfounded. Adding nivolumab to chemotherapy did not increase the incidence of surgery-related adverse events or impede the feasibility of the surgery itself (Forde et al., 2022). The vast majority of patients in the immunotherapy arms successfully made it to the operating room (Heymach et al., 2023).
