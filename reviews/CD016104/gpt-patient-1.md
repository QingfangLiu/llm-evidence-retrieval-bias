## Bottom line

For **resectable NSCLC**, the most consistent randomized evidence supports giving an immune checkpoint inhibitor **with platinum chemotherapy before surgery**. This increases the chance of finding no viable cancer at surgery and delays recurrence. Three trials have now demonstrated an overall-survival benefit: neoadjuvant-only CheckMate 816 and the perioperative KEYNOTE-671 and RATIONALE-315 trials. ([PubMed][1])

Evidence for an immune checkpoint inhibitor **only after surgery** is less consistent. Atezolizumab and pembrolizumab improved disease-free survival in IMpower010 and KEYNOTE-091, but durvalumab and nivolumab did not do so in BR.31 and ANVIL. None of the adjuvant-only trials has established an overall-survival benefit for the overall trial population. ([PubMed][2])

For an older adult, the major uncertainty is **not whether people over 65 were enrolled—they were—but whether the results apply to someone over 75, frail, or living with substantial heart, lung, kidney, neurologic, or autoimmune disease**. Participants were generally fit, with performance status 0–1. TD-FOREKNOW excluded anyone older than 70; NEOTORCH and RATIONALE-315 had median ages of 62; ANVIL had the oldest typical population, with median ages of 66 and 67. Age-specific analyses were exploratory rather than designed to determine benefit in frail or very old adults. ([JAMA Network][3])

An HR of 0.60 means an approximately 40% lower **relative rate** of the measured event during follow-up. It does not mean that 40% more patients are cured.

## What harms matter most?

The high-grade-event percentages include many chemotherapy toxicities—especially neutropenia, anemia, infection, neuropathy, kidney injury, hearing effects, fatigue and nausea—so they do not measure checkpoint-inhibitor toxicity alone.

Checkpoint-specific problems included pneumonitis, thyroid dysfunction, diarrhea or colitis, myocarditis and other inflammatory organ damage. Most are manageable, but some require stopping treatment, prolonged corticosteroids or long-term medical treatment. Rare treatment-related deaths occurred in several large trials, including KEYNOTE-091, IMpower010 and ANVIL. ([PubMed][11])

Preoperative treatment creates an additional concern: toxicity or progression could delay or prevent surgery. Overall, the randomized trials did **not** show a major reduction in surgery rates from adding checkpoint inhibition; several showed numerically more surgery or more complete resections. Nevertheless, an individual patient can still have a serious adverse event or lose the opportunity for surgery. ([PubMed][4])

## How directly does this apply to an older adult?

The evidence applies best when all of the following resemble the trial populations:

* The cancer is **stage II or III, or a stage IB tumor at least 4 cm**. Small, node-negative stage I cancers were generally not represented.
* Surgery is considered feasible after multidisciplinary review.
* Performance status is 0–1, with sufficient pulmonary and organ reserve for platinum chemotherapy and lung resection.
* There is no active autoimmune disease requiring substantial immunosuppression and no organ transplant.
* For adenocarcinoma, broad molecular testing has addressed **EGFR and ALK**. Many perioperative trials excluded these alterations or excluded them from efficacy analyses.
* The expected benefit is large enough to justify both chemotherapy toxicity and the possibility of immune toxicity.

Someone aged 65–74 who is active and physiologically fit may resemble many participants. Evidence is substantially less certain for a person over 75–80, someone with frailty or impaired daily function, or someone who would not tolerate standard platinum-doublet chemotherapy.

The treatment decision therefore depends more on **physiologic fitness, exact TNM stage, lymph-node involvement, planned extent of lung resection, EGFR/ALK results and PD-L1 level** than on chronological age alone.
