## What the primary trials show

For **fit adults with resectable stage II–III NSCLC**, adding a PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor to chemotherapy before surgery consistently increases the chance that no viable cancer is found at surgery and reduces recurrence or progression. Overall-survival improvement has now been demonstrated in several trials, although follow-up remains immature for some regimens. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

The evidence is less consistent for **immunotherapy given only after surgery**: atezolizumab and pembrolizumab improved disease-free survival in certain populations, but two large trials of durvalumab and nivolumab found no meaningful benefit. ([ASCO Publications][2])

## How directly does this apply to an older adult?

The trial participants were generally unusually fit: most had an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1, adequate organ function and median ages in the early-to-mid 60s. Several studies included patients into their 80s, but some smaller trials capped eligibility at 70. Very few results distinguish ages 65–74 from age 75 or older, and the trials did not incorporate frailty or formal geriatric-assessment measures. ([PMC][5])

Age-subgroup estimates generally do not demonstrate that checkpoint inhibitors stop working at 65. However, those analyses are underpowered. In KEYNOTE-671, the overall-survival point estimate was less clearly favorable in participants 65 or older, but its confidence interval overlapped the overall trial result; CheckMate 816 reported survival consistency across most subgroups. These findings cannot establish the benefit specifically for a frail person or someone over 75. ([PMC][15])

## Practical interpretation

**The most convincing approach is checkpoint inhibition combined with chemotherapy before surgery** for an appropriate stage II–III tumor. It has consistently produced large improvements in pathologic response and event-free survival, and some trials now demonstrate longer overall survival.

Several uncertainties remain:

* Perioperative trials changed both the preoperative and postoperative treatment, so they cannot determine how much benefit comes from the additional year of immunotherapy after surgery.
* A pCR is encouraging but is not the same thing as guaranteed cure.
* Adjuvant-only immunotherapy has mixed results.
* Most neoadjuvant/perioperative evidence concerns **stage II–III, tumors at least 4 cm, or node-positive disease**. It is much less applicable to a small, node-negative stage I tumor.
* Many trials excluded sensitizing **EGFR or ALK alterations**. Tumor molecular testing, PD-L1, lymph-node status, surgical risk, lung function, autoimmune disease and the ability to tolerate platinum chemotherapy materially change the benefit–harm calculation. ([PubMed][3])
