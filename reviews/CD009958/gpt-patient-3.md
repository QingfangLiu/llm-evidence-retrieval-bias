## Bottom line

The individual trials do **not** establish one universally best repositioning interval or position.

The most defensible interpretation is:

* **Every 2 hours has not consistently prevented more pressure injuries than every 3 or 4 hours** when adults were on appropriate pressure-redistributing mattresses.
* The strongest positive result for body position was **a 30° lateral tilt every 3 hours overnight**, but it was compared with **90° lateral positioning every 6 hours**. Because both the angle and frequency changed, the study cannot determine which component produced the benefit.
* A full **90° side-lying position has no demonstrated advantage** over a 30° tilt and concentrates pressure over the trochanter.
* The effectiveness of repositioning depends heavily on the **mattress/support surface, actual adherence, duration of pressure relief, mobility, perfusion and individual risk**.
* Longer intervals should not automatically be applied to people at very high or severe risk, people with existing skin changes, or those using an ordinary mattress.

Every study cited below is an **individual primary study**, not a review or guideline.

### Study addressing whether the turns are actually performed

**Pickham et al., 2018 — LS-HAPI trial:** 1,312 ICU patients were randomized to usual turning practices or real-time wearable-sensor feedback. Hospital-acquired pressure injuries occurred in **0.7% with sensor feedback versus 2.3% with usual care**; turning compliance was 67% versus 54%. This trial did not compare different prescribed intervals, but it suggests that reliable delivery of effective turns may matter as much as selecting a nominal schedule. ([PubMed][7])

### Special setting: prolonged surgery

Two recent randomized trials tested small intraoperative positional changes rather than conventional bedside turning:

* **Jin et al., 2024:** Among 200 adults having prolonged surgery in the supine position, tilting the operating table **15° left and right for 5 minutes every hour**, in addition to sacral foam dressing, reduced sacrococcygeal pressure injuries from **10% to 2%**. ([PubMed][11])
* **Micro-movement trial in posterior lumbar surgery, 2024:** Among 277 overweight adults, regular operating-table adjustments produced an injury incidence of **2.9%**, compared with **8.6%** using silicone foam dressings. These findings apply primarily to anesthetized surgical patients rather than routine bed care. ([PMC][12])

## Practical interpretation of these primary studies

For an at-risk adult lying in bed, the evidence supports a **planned, individualized rotation**, commonly incorporating:

**30° left lateral tilt → back/supine with pressure properly redistributed → 30° right lateral tilt**, rather than repeatedly placing the person directly at 90° on the hip. Heels and other vulnerable sites require separate off-loading; simply changing the torso position may not unload every threatened area.

However, the studies do **not** prove that this sequence—or any fixed interval—is best for everyone. Intervals of **3–4 hours appear potentially reasonable for selected adults using effective pressure-redistributing surfaces**, but the evidence does not justify independently extending an existing schedule. People with severe immobility, circulatory instability, loss of sensation, malnutrition, moisture exposure, existing non-blanchable redness or an unsuitable mattress may need more frequent or site-specific repositioning.

A useful care-plan question for the treating nurse or wound clinician is: **“Given my mattress, skin findings, mobility and risk score, what maximum interval can I safely tolerate, and how will we verify that each turn actually unloads my sacrum, hips and heels?”**
