## Clinical bottom line

The primary comparative studies do **not establish one universally optimal repositioning interval**. In adults using effective pressure-redistributing mattresses, several trials found that **every 3–4 hours produced similar short-term pressure-injury rates to every 2 hours**. These results are most applicable to nursing-home residents without an existing injury and to selected ICU patients on advanced support surfaces—not necessarily to patients with profound hemodynamic instability, very severe risk, or rapidly changing skin findings.

For body position, the most promising conventional regimen is a **30° lateral tilt rather than full 90° lateral positioning**, but results conflict: one reasonably sized nursing-home trial favored the 30° tilt, whereas a small hospital trial found no advantage and substantial difficulty maintaining the position. Prone positioning shifts pressure to the face and anterior body and generally increases pressure-injury burden. Small intraoperative trials suggest that brief, regular **micromovements** may be beneficial.

### What the frequency studies support

For appropriately selected adults on a functioning high-specification foam or alternating-pressure surface, these trials do **not demonstrate that routine 2-hour turning is superior to 3- or 4-hour turning**. The strongest direct nursing-home evidence permits intervals up to 4 hours, provided that repositioning actually occurs, the mattress is appropriate, and skin condition is monitored. ([PMC][3])

They do **not** establish that every 4 hours is adequate for every patient. Very severely at-risk residents were excluded from TEAM-UP, follow-up was generally brief, and event numbers were low.

## Practical interpretation of the primary evidence

A defensible evidence-based interpretation is:

* **Interval:** Every **3–4 hours may be reasonable for selected adults on an effective pressure-redistributing mattress**, particularly stable nursing-home residents without a current injury. The evidence does not show a consistent benefit from automatically shortening the interval to every 2 hours.
* **Lateral positioning:** Use of a **supported 30° tilt** is biologically and clinically plausible and was beneficial in one nursing-home trial, but it is not conclusively superior to other rotations and may be difficult to maintain.
* **Avoid prolonged full 90° lateral loading** when a smaller supported tilt can achieve off-loading, while recognizing that the direct trial evidence is limited.
* **Prone positioning:** Use it when clinically indicated for pulmonary or surgical reasons, but do not regard it as protective for the skin. It changes the sites at risk and requires deliberate facial, thoracic, iliac, knee, and device-related off-loading.
* **Operating room:** Brief scheduled micromovements—rather than leaving the patient completely fixed—have produced relatively consistent benefit in recent randomized trials, although these results are specific to operative settings.

The studies were generally short, unblinded, and underpowered for uncommon stage 3–4 injuries. Mattress technology, turning fidelity, spontaneous movement, continence care, heel off-loading, and device management were often inseparable from the assigned repositioning regimen.
