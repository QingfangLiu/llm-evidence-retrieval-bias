Here are the key primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating repositioning frequency and positioning for pressure injury prevention in adults, excluding reviews and consensus guidelines.

## Repositioning Frequency

The traditional clinical standard has been 2-hour turning, but primary RCTs show that optimal frequency is highly dependent on the type of support surface (mattress) used.

* **Defloor et al. (2005)** evaluated 838 geriatric nursing home patients. The study compared turning every 2 or 3 hours on standard mattresses against turning every 4 or 6 hours on viscoelastic foam mattresses. They found that turning every 4 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress resulted in a significant reduction in pressure ulcer lesions (3.0% incidence) compared to the other groups (which ranged from 14.3% to 24.1%).
* **The TURN Study (Bergstrom et al., 2013)** was a multisite trial evaluating 942 nursing home residents using high-density foam mattresses. Residents were randomized to 2-, 3-, or 4-hour turning intervals. The results showed no statistically significant difference in pressure ulcer incidence across the three intervals.

> **Key insight:** When high-density or viscoelastic foam mattresses are used, turning intervals can safely be extended to 3 or 4 hours without increasing pressure injury risk. This reduces staff burden and protects patient sleep cycles.

## Optimal Body Positions

Research on positioning primarily compares the **30° tilt** to the standard **90° lateral** or supine positions, balancing pressure relief with patient tolerability.

* **Moore et al. (2011)** conducted a cluster RCT in long-term care settings, comparing 3-hourly repositioning using a 30° tilt at night against routine 6-hourly 90° lateral rotation. The 30° tilt group saw a significantly lower incidence of pressure ulcers (3%) compared to the control group (11%).
* **Young (2004)** compared the 30° tilt to the 90° lateral and supine positions in an acute hospital inpatient population. While incidence rates did not significantly differ between the groups, 78% of the patients had difficulty adopting and maintaining the 30° tilt.

> **Key insight:** While the 30° tilt is highly effective at reducing interface pressure and preventing ulcers in long-term care, it may be difficult to maintain for acutely ill hospital populations due to comfort and feasibility issues.
