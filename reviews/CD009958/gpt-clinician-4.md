I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, consensus statements, opinions, and editorials. Every study cited below is an individual primary study.

## Clinical takeaways

**Repositioning frequency:** Comparative trials have not demonstrated that routine **every-2-hour turning is superior to every 3 or 4 hours** in selected adults using high-density foam or alternating-pressure mattresses. This finding is most applicable to nursing-home residents without severe instability and mechanically ventilated ICU patients already receiving advanced support surfaces. ([PMC][1])

Evidence for extending the interval to **6 hours is less reassuring**: one nursing-home trial favored every 4 hours over every 6 hours on viscoelastic foam, although event definitions and methodological limitations reduce confidence. ([PubMed][2])

**Position:** A **30° lateral tilt** may be preferable to a full **90° lateral position**, but the evidence is inconsistent. The positive trial combined 30° tilt with more frequent turning, so the effect of angle cannot be separated from frequency. ([PubMed][3])

**Prone positioning** increases pressure-injury burden compared with supine positioning in severe ARDS. This does not mean avoiding medically indicated proning; it means proned patients require especially intensive anterior-surface, facial, device, and head-position management. ([ResearchGate][4])

## Practical interpretation of these studies

For adults on an effective pressure-redistributing surface, the trial evidence supports considering a **2- to 4-hour range**, rather than assuming that every 2 hours is universally necessary. Every-3- or every-4-hour schedules are best supported in stable long-term-care residents and in ICU patients using alternating-pressure surfaces. Evidence is insufficient to extend that conclusion to patients with severe shock, markedly impaired perfusion, rapidly changing skin findings, or surfaces that do not adequately redistribute pressure.

For lateral positioning, a **maintainable 30° tilt** is the most promising studied position, but it has not been definitively proven superior to other positions. The evidence does not support prolonged full 90° lateral loading as a clearly preferable strategy. For prone patients, risk shifts to the face, chest, iliac areas, knees, and devices; proning should be treated as a particularly high-risk pressure-injury state rather than as a preventive body position.
