As an evidence-synthesis researcher, you know that practice guidelines and systematic reviews often rely heavily on historical precedent or underpowered data. By stripping away the commentary and looking strictly at the primary literature, a few major randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies challenge the traditional "2-hour turning" rule and highlight specific angles for optimal offloading.

Here is the synthesis of the highest-impact primary studies addressing repositioning frequency and body position in adults.

## 1. How Often Should Patients Be Repositioned?

Historically, the 2-hour turning schedule was an unquestioned standard, but large-scale RCTs have demonstrated that interval effectiveness depends heavily on the support surface (mattress) being used.

* **Bergstrom et al. (2013) — The TURN-IN Study:** This landmark multisite RCT evaluated 942 nursing home residents at risk for pressure injuries, all of whom were placed on high-density foam mattresses. They compared turning intervals of **2 hours, 3 hours, and 4 hours**.
* *Finding:* There was **no statistically significant difference** in pressure injury incidence among the three groups. Repositioning every 4 hours on a high-density foam mattress is equally effective as every 2 hours, saving significant nursing time and reducing patient sleep disruption.


* **Defloor et al. (2005):** This trial tested 838 geriatric patients to compare frequency in tandem with mattress type. The groups included turning every 2 hours or 3 hours on a standard institutional mattress, and turning every 4 hours or 6 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress.
* *Finding:* Turning **every 4 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress** resulted in a significantly lower incidence of pressure injuries (3.0%) compared to 2-hour (14.3%) or 3-hour turning on standard mattresses.



## 2. Which Body Positions Work Best?

When patients are turned laterally, the angle of the turn dictates where the pressure is distributed. The primary literature strongly supports a shallower turn over a full lateral rotation.

* **Moore, Cowman, & Conroy (2011):** This cluster RCT directly compared a **30° lateral tilt** (repositioned every 3 hours at night) against the standard **90° lateral rotation** (repositioned every 6 hours at night).
* *Finding:* The 30° tilt significantly reduced the incidence of pressure ulcers. A 90° turn places the patient's entire body weight directly over the greater trochanter (the hip bone), creating a high-pressure focal point. The 30° tilt shifts the weight off the sacrum and the trochanter, distributing it across the larger, fleshier gluteal mass.


* **Kapp et al. (2019):** This observational study focused on whether patients actually *stay* in the optimal position. The researchers measured patients placed in a 30° lateral tilt at baseline, 1 hour, and 2 hours, comparing standard hospital pillows against purpose-designed fluidised positioners.
* *Finding:* Patients propped with standard pillows degraded from an average 26.7° angle at baseline to 16.6° at 2 hours, losing the protective offloading benefit. Purpose-designed devices maintained the angle significantly better (degrading only from 30.7° to 26.8° over 2 hours).
