## Bottom line

The primary studies do **not establish one universally optimal repositioning interval or body position** for all at-risk adults. The most defensible conclusions are:

* In **selected nursing-home residents using high-density foam mattresses**, repositioning every **3–4 hours** produced similarly low pressure-injury rates to every 2 hours. These findings should not be extrapolated automatically to people with severe risk, unstable perfusion, existing deep tissue injury, or inadequate support surfaces.
* In mechanically ventilated ICU patients using **alternating-pressure air mattresses**, every-2-hour turning was not superior to every 4 hours and increased workload and device-related adverse events.
* Evidence favoring a **30° lateral tilt** over a 90° lateral position is suggestive but inconsistent. The strongest positive study also changed the turning frequency, making it impossible to isolate the effect of angle.
* Prone positioning increases anterior pressure-injury exposure. That is a reason for intensified preventive care—not to withhold clinically indicated prone ventilation.
* Small perioperative trials suggest that brief, repeated **micromovements or table tilts** can reduce intraoperative injuries.

### Frequency interpretation

Taken together, these trials support **considering 3- or 4-hour repositioning rather than automatically prescribing every 2 hours** when all of the following resemble the study conditions:

* The patient is on an effective pressure-redistributing surface.
* Skin and tissue are reassessed regularly.
* The patient is not at exceptionally high risk or showing early tissue deterioration.
* Repositioning adherence can be monitored.
* The schedule is shortened when skin response, perfusion, device pressure, moisture, or clinical deterioration warrants it.

That is a synthesis rather than a proven universal threshold. The trials are generally short, have low event rates, and often combine the repositioning schedule with a particular mattress or monitoring system. ([PubMed][1])

## What the primary evidence says about “best” positions

**30° lateral tilt:** Reasonable as a pressure-offloading strategy, but not definitively proven superior. One small trial was negative; a larger positive trial compared 30° every 3 hours with 90° every 6 hours, so angle and frequency remain inseparable. ([PubMed][7])

**90° lateral position:** There is no strong trial establishing that it should never be used, but the available comparative trial does not favor prolonged 90° lateral positioning. Where feasible, a shallower tilt that avoids direct loading of the greater trochanter is more consistent with the positive trial, though the evidence is not conclusive. ([PubMed][8])

**Head-of-bed angle:** The identified randomized trial provides no useful evidence that 30° or 45° is superior for pressure-injury prevention because no injuries occurred during its brief follow-up. ([ResearchGate][9])

**Prone position:** Expect increased anterior pressure-injury risk and use a dedicated preventive approach involving facial/head repositioning, careful padding, device surveillance, and repeated skin assessment. The tested ESCARD bundle was not clearly effective, so no single prone-position protocol can yet be called optimal. ([Springer][10])

**Brief micromovements:** These are promising during long operations when full repositioning is impossible, but the evidence is currently confined largely to small perioperative trials. ([PubMed][12])

Overall, the primary evidence favors a **responsive, surface-dependent repositioning strategy** rather than an inflexible universal 2-hour rule. A 3- or 4-hour schedule has direct trial support in selected adults on effective support surfaces, while higher-risk or clinically deteriorating patients may require more frequent offloading based on repeated skin and tissue response.
