It is great to connect with a fellow evidence-synthesis researcher. Stripping away the guidelines, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses to look purely at the raw primary data reveals just how nuanced the evidence base actually is.

When you isolate only the individual primary studies (mostly randomized controlled trials and large-scale quasi-experimental designs), you find that the "optimal" frequency and position are highly dependent on the support surface being used and the clinical setting. Here are the pivotal primary studies you need for your synthesis.

## 1. Frequency of Repositioning

The traditional standard of two-hour turning has been heavily tested in the primary literature, particularly to see if better mattresses allow for less frequent turning.

* **Bergstrom et al. (2013) — The TURN Study:** This was a massive multisite RCT in nursing homes comparing 2-hour, 3-hour, and 4-hour repositioning intervals for residents placed on high-density foam mattresses. They found **no significant difference** in pressure ulcer incidence across the three groups. The study suggests that on high-quality foam mattresses, a 4-hour interval may be just as effective as a 2-hour interval.
* **Defloor et al. (2005):** This RCT tested four different combinations of frequency and support surfaces in geriatric patients. They found that turning patients **every 4 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress** resulted in significantly fewer pressure ulcers than turning every 2 or 3 hours on a standard institutional mattress, or every 6 hours on a viscoelastic mattress.
* **Still et al. (2013):** Looking at higher-acuity patients, this quasi-experimental study implemented a dedicated "Turn Team" in a surgical ICU to enforce strict **2-hour repositioning** for hemodynamically stable patients. Compared to baseline usual care (where turning was encouraged but not standardized), the strict 2-hour regimen dramatically decreased pressure ulcer incidence, highlighting that frequency remains critical in critical care settings.

## 2. Optimal Body Position

Much of the primary literature investigating position compares the traditional 90-degree lateral position (lying completely on the side) against a gentler 30-degree tilt.

The 90-degree position places the patient's full weight directly on the greater trochanter (hip bone). To alleviate this, researchers test the 30-degree tilt, which shifts the weight off the bony prominences and onto the gluteal muscle mass.

* **Moore et al. (2011):** This cluster RCT compared 3-hourly repositioning using a **30-degree tilt** at night against the routine care of 6-hourly repositioning using a **90-degree lateral rotation**. The 30-degree tilt group had a significantly lower incidence of pressure ulcers (3% vs. 11%), establishing strong primary evidence for the 30-degree angle.
* **Young (2004):** This RCT compared the 30-degree tilt directly against the 90-degree lateral and supine positions in a hospital inpatient population. Unlike Moore, this study found **no significant difference** in pressure damage between the positions. Crucially, they reported that **78% of the subjects experienced difficulty adopting and maintaining the 30-degree tilt**, calling into question its clinical practicality for acutely ill patients.
