### Frequency synthesis

The controlled studies do **not identify a universally optimal interval**.

In adults using modern pressure-redistributing mattresses:

* Two-hourly turning has not consistently reduced injuries compared with 3- or 4-hourly turning.
* In the ICU trial using alternating-pressure mattresses, 2-hourly turning increased workload and adverse events without a detectable injury benefit.
* The Defloor trial suggests that the support surface modifies the appropriate interval: 4-hourly turning on viscoelastic foam performed better than more frequent turning on a standard mattress, but this factorial confounding prevents attribution to frequency alone.
* The sensor trials suggest that **whether repositioning is actually delivered** may be as important as the nominal interval.

These results should not be generalized automatically to adults at extremely high risk, those on standard non-redistributing mattresses, or patients whose tissue response or clinical condition demands more frequent off-loading.

## Position synthesis

**Thirty-degree lateral tilt:** The clinical evidence is inconclusive. One very small trial found no benefit; one larger cluster trial favored a 30°-tilt package, but also doubled the repositioning frequency relative to control. Thus, there is no clean clinical-outcome estimate for 30° versus 90° lateral positioning at the same interval.

**Prone versus supine:** Prolonged prone ventilation increases exposure to pressure injury, particularly at anterior and device-contact sites. It should be considered a high-risk position requiring targeted off-loading, rather than interpreted as an inferior position that should be avoided when proning is otherwise clinically indicated.

**Head-of-bed angle:** The available short clinical trial does not establish an optimal 30° versus 45° angle for pressure-injury prevention. Healthy-volunteer experiments indicate increasing sacral interface pressure as head elevation rises, but those are surrogate studies rather than injury-outcome trials. ([PubMed][14])

**Micromovements:** The most consistent recent positional signal comes from operating-room RCTs. Small, scheduled table tilts or micromovements reduced injuries during prolonged supine and prone procedures, although external validity to ward and ICU patients remains uncertain.

## Overall evidence interpretation

The primary studies support four conclusions:

1. **There is no demonstrated universal “every 2 hours” optimum.** In selected adults on high-specification foam or alternating-pressure mattresses, 3- or 4-hour schedules generally did not produce more injuries than 2-hour schedules.

2. **Frequency and support surface should not be treated as independent interventions.** The strongest apparent frequency effect came from a trial in which mattress type changed simultaneously.

3. **No lateral position is conclusively best.** Evidence favoring a 30° tilt is promising but confounded and inconsistent.

4. **Position execution matters.** Sensor-cued adherence and small pressure-relieving movements have reduced injuries in individual trials even when the formal position or schedule was otherwise unchanged.

Important sources of heterogeneity include setting, baseline risk, mattress type, stage-1 inclusion, outcome threshold—nonblanchable erythema versus stage ≥2—follow-up duration, position fidelity and adherence. Consequently, these trials provide evidence for **context-dependent, individualized repositioning**, rather than a single interval or body position that is optimal for all at-risk adults.
