The tradition of the rigid 2-hourly turning rule has been significantly challenged by primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs). These studies demonstrate that repositioning frequency is highly dependent on the support surface being used, and that extending intervals does not necessarily increase pressure injury risk.

Here is a synthesis of the key primary studies addressing both frequency and positioning, strictly excluding secondary literature.

## Repositioning Frequency: How Often?

For decades, the standard of care was to reposition at-risk adults every 2 hours. However, multiple primary clinical trials indicate that when modern, high-density support surfaces are used, repositioning intervals can safely be extended to 3 or 4 hours.

**The Role of Support Surfaces**
In a foundational multicenter trial by Defloor et al. (2005), researchers investigated how different turning intervals interacted with mattress types in 838 nursing home residents. The study found that turning patients every 4 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress resulted in a significantly lower incidence of grade II+ pressure ulcers (3.0%) compared to turning every 2 hours (14.3%) or 3 hours on standard institutional mattresses.

**Equivalence of 2-, 3-, and 4-Hour Intervals**
The TURN (Turning for Ulcer ReductioN) trial directly compared extended intervals among 942 residents on high-density foam mattresses (Bergstrom et al., 2013). Participants were randomized to 2-, 3-, or 4-hour turning schedules. The researchers found no statistically significant difference in pressure ulcer incidence across the three groups (2.5% for 2-hour, 0.6% for 3-hour, and 3.1% for 4-hour intervals).

This was later corroborated by the large-scale, pragmatic TEAM-UP cluster RCT (Yap et al., 2022). Evaluating 992 nursing home residents on high-density foam mattresses, the trial randomly assigned facilities to 2-, 3-, or 4-hour protocols. No new pressure injuries developed during the intervention across any group, but staff compliance fidelity was significantly higher for the 4-hour interval (95%) compared to the 2-hour interval (80%).

## Body Position: Which Works Best?

When determining *how* to position an adult to minimize pressure, primary evidence points away from steep lateral turns (which place heavy pressure on the trochanter) and strongly favors gentler tilt angles.

**The 30° Tilt vs. 90° Lateral Position**
Moore et al. (2011) conducted a cluster-randomized controlled trial with 213 older adults to evaluate position mechanics. The experimental group was positioned using a 30° tilt every 3 hours overnight, while the control group received standard 90° lateral rotations every 6 hours.

The trial found that the 30° tilt regimen resulted in significantly fewer pressure ulcers (3%) compared to the 90° lateral rotation group (11%). The 30° position better distributes weight across larger, well-padded surface areas of the body, effectively avoiding the concentrated pressure on bony prominences that occurs in a full 90° side-lying position (Moore et al., 2011).
