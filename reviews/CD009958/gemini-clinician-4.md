Managing pressure injury risk requires balancing tissue offloading with patient comfort, sleep, and practical nursing workflows. While a strict 2-hour turning schedule has historically been a clinical baseline, a review of primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) provides a more nuanced look at how frequency and specific body angles impact patient outcomes.

Here is what the primary literature reveals about repositioning frequency and body position.

## Optimal Repositioning Frequency

The necessary interval for turning is heavily dependent on the type of support surface the patient is resting on.

* **Frequency and Mattress Type:** A cornerstone multicenter RCT by Defloor et al. (2005) investigated four different turning schedules across 838 geriatric patients. They compared turning every 2 or 3 hours on standard institutional mattresses against turning every 4 or 6 hours on viscoelastic foam mattresses. They found that a 4-hour turning interval on a viscoelastic foam mattress resulted in a significantly lower incidence (3.0%) of Grade II or higher pressure ulcers compared to the other groups (which ranged from 14.3% to 24.1%). This suggests that upgrading the support surface can safely extend turning intervals without compromising patient skin integrity.
* **Unequal Time Intervals:** Vanderwee et al. (2006) explored whether a schedule alternating 2 hours in a lateral position and 4 hours in a supine position was more effective than standard 4-hour turning. The incidence of pressure ulcers did not differ significantly between the two groups. Notably, the researchers observed that staff found it practically challenging to maintain patients in the lateral position between scheduled turns, as many patients naturally shifted back to a supine posture.

## The Efficacy of Specific Body Positions

When placing patients on their side, the angle of lateral rotation—specifically the 30° tilt versus the 90° lateral position—has been a primary focus of investigation.

* **Benefits of the 30° Tilt:** An RCT by Moore et al. (2011) assessed 213 older adults in long-term care settings. The experimental group was repositioned every 3 hours overnight using a 30° tilt, while the control group received standard care consisting of a 6-hour 90° lateral rotation. The 30° tilt regimen resulted in a statistically significant reduction in pressure ulcer incidence compared to the standard care group.
* **Challenges and Tolerability:** Conversely, an earlier RCT by Young (2004) evaluating the 30° tilt versus 90° lateral and supine positioning in an acutely ill hospital population found no significant reduction in pressure damage with the 30° tilt. More importantly, this study highlighted substantial tolerability issues: 78% of the patients had difficulty adopting and maintaining the 30° tilt position, which questions its universal practicality for sicker or uncooperative populations.

## Summary of Primary Trials

| Study | Focus | Key Clinical Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Defloor et al. (2005)** | Frequency & Mattress | 4-hourly turning on viscoelastic foam mattresses yielded the lowest incidence of Grade II+ pressure ulcers. |
| **Vanderwee et al. (2006)** | Unequal Turning Intervals | Alternating 2-hr lateral and 4-hr supine positions showed no significant benefit over a standard 4-hour schedule. |
| **Moore et al. (2011)** | 30° Tilt vs. 90° Rotation | 30° tilt every 3 hours at night significantly reduced pressure ulcer incidence compared to 6-hourly 90° rotation. |
| **Young (2004)** | 30° Tilt Tolerability | The 30° tilt failed to reduce ulcer incidence, and 78% of subjects found the position difficult to maintain. |
