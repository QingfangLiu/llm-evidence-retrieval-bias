Managing the risk of pressure injuries (bedsores) requires balancing the need to relieve pressure on vulnerable areas with the need for comfort and uninterrupted rest. Historically, a strict "every 2 hours" turning schedule was the universal standard, but modern primary clinical trials have tested these assumptions, especially when using modern mattresses.

Here is a breakdown of what individual primary studies—specifically clinical trials and observational studies—say about repositioning frequency and body positions.

---

## 1. How Often Should You Be Repositioned?

Recent clinical trials suggest that the traditional 2-hour repositioning schedule can often be safely extended to 3 or 4 hours, provided the patient is on a high-quality, pressure-redistributing mattress.

* **Extending to 4 Hours on Modern Mattresses:** A major randomized controlled trial (Defloor et al., 2005) involving 838 patients found that turning patients every 4 hours on a viscoelastic foam mattress resulted in a significantly lower incidence of pressure ulcers (3.0%) compared to turning them every 2 hours on a standard institutional mattress (up to 24.1%).
* **The TEAM-UP Trial:** A recent large-scale clinical trial (Yap et al., 2022) tested 2-, 3-, and 4-hour repositioning intervals on nearly 1,000 nursing home residents resting on high-density foam mattresses. The researchers found that the intervals could be safely extended to 3 or 4 hours without increasing the incidence of pressure injuries. Furthermore, nursing staff were significantly more compliant with the 4-hour schedule (95%) than the 2-hour schedule (80%), meaning the turns were performed more consistently.
* **Risks of Over-Repositioning:** A trial focusing on intensive care patients on mechanical ventilation (Manzano et al., 2014) compared 2-hour versus 4-hour repositioning on alternating pressure air mattresses. The study found that 2-hour repositioning did not reduce pressure ulcers better than the 4-hour schedule. In fact, the more frequent 2-hour turning increased the risk of medical device-related adverse events, such as breathing tube obstructions.

---

## 2. Which Body Positions Work Best?

When you lie entirely flat on your back (**supine**) or completely flat on your side (**90-degree lateral rotation**), your body weight drives intensely into a few bony prominences, such as the tailbone (sacrum) or the hip bone (trochanter). To combat this, primary clinical literature frequently focuses on the **30-degree lateral tilt**.

In a 30-degree tilt, you are rolled slightly onto your side and propped up with wedges or pillows. This shifts the weight off the bony hip and tailbone, distributing it across the fleshier parts of your back and buttocks.

* **Superiority Over 90-Degree Positioning:** A randomized controlled trial of older adults (Moore, Cowman, & Conroy, 2011) compared patients repositioned using a 30-degree tilt every 3 hours at night versus patients receiving routine care (a 90-degree lateral turn every 6 hours). Only 3% of the patients using the 30-degree tilt developed pressure ulcers, compared to 11% in the routine care group.
* **The Importance of Proper Wedges:** Maintaining the exact 30-degree angle is crucial but difficult. An observational study (Kapp et al., 2019) tracked patients placed in the 30-degree tilt. They found that when standard pillows were used, the body naturally flattened out over a two-hour period (from 30 degrees down to roughly 26 degrees), putting pressure back onto vulnerable areas. Purpose-designed fluidised positioners and wedges were much more effective at holding the correct angle.
* **Comfort and Feasibility:** While highly effective on paper, the 30-degree tilt is not right for everyone. A hospital-based clinical trial (Young, 2004) found that the 30-degree tilt did not prevent pressure damage better than the 90-degree or supine positions in their specific patient group. The researchers noted a major practical barrier: 78% of the acutely ill patients in the study found it difficult to comfortably adopt and maintain the 30-degree position.
