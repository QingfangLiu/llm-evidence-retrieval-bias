## Scope

I found the following **comparative primary studies in adults that reported incident pressure injuries or non-blanchable erythema**. I separated conventional bed repositioning from perioperative positioning because the latter is not directly generalizable to ward or long-term-care turning. Reviews, guidelines, opinions, and commentaries are not included as evidence below.

This was a targeted primary-study search rather than a database-grade systematic search, so it should not be treated as proof that no additional unpublished or non-indexed studies exist.

### Frequency conclusion

The direct trials do **not support a universal every-2-hour rule**. Their more defensible finding is conditional:

* In nursing-home residents at moderate or high—but generally not extreme—risk, **3- or 4-hour intervals performed similarly to 2-hour intervals when high-density or viscoelastic foam mattresses and organized prevention care were used**.
* In mechanically ventilated ICU patients on alternating-pressure air mattresses, **2-hourly turning was not significantly better than 4-hourly turning** and created additional burden and adverse events.
* These studies do not establish that 4-hour schedules are safe for every patient, particularly those with rapidly changing perfusion, severe instability, existing tissue changes, very high risk, or inadequate support surfaces.

Moore’s trial supports a **package of 30° tilt plus 3-hourly nighttime repositioning**, but Young did not find the 30° tilt superior when frequencies were similar. There is no reliable direct evidence that one head-of-bed angle prevents more PIs than another.

### Position conclusion

The most promising position-specific findings are setting dependent:

* **Long-term care:** A 30° lateral tilt combined with **3-hourly nighttime repositioning** reduced stage 1–2 injuries compared with 90° lateral positioning every 6 hours, but angle and frequency cannot be disentangled.
* **Routine medical inpatients:** The small Young trial did not show that a 30° tilt was better than supine/90° lateral positioning and found maintenance difficult.
* **Prolonged supine surgery:** A **curvilinear supine configuration** and brief alternating **15° micromovements** reduced intraoperative PI incidence in several single-centre trials.
* **Prone surgery:** Alternating 15° table micromovements were beneficial in one overweight lumbar-surgery population.
* **Prone ARDS ventilation:** A 15° inclination/cushion/head-turning bundle did not significantly reduce the very high rate of anterior PIs.

## 3. Relevant implementation trials that do not determine the optimal interval

Two additional primary studies help establish that **delivering the prescribed repositioning regimen reliably** matters, although they do not directly compare candidate schedules.

**Pickham et al., LS-HAPI, 2018**, randomized 1,312 ICU patients to standard turn reminders or a wearable position sensor providing real-time feedback. HAPI occurred in **5/659 (0.7%) versus 15/653 (2.3%)**, OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.12–0.90; measured turning compliance was 67% versus 54%. This supports sensor-assisted adherence but does not identify the optimal turning interval or angle. ([PubMed][13])

**De Meyer et al., PROTECT, 2019**, was a three-arm cluster RCT involving 227 at-risk patients on 29 hospital wards. Tailored schedules and a turning aid increased adherence and the proportion correctly positioned at a 30°–45° side-lying angle, but too few PIs occurred to establish comparative clinical effectiveness. ([PubMed][14])

## Important eligibility distinction

I would exclude **Vanderwee et al., 2007** from a strict *primary-prevention* synthesis because participants already had non-blanchable erythema/stage-1 injury at enrollment; it principally addresses progression or resolution of existing early tissue damage rather than first PI occurrence. ([PubMed][15])

I also excluded studies reporting only interface pressure, subepidermal moisture, skin perfusion or other surrogate outcomes from the main tables.

## Overall evidence-synthesis conclusion

The primary studies do not identify one universally optimal repositioning frequency or body position. The intervention should be conceptualized as a combination of:

**repositioning interval + angle/movement magnitude + support surface + adherence + patient tissue tolerance and physiologic stability.**

Across the most directly applicable trials, **3- to 4-hour schedules on effective pressure-redistributing mattresses did not produce more injuries than 2-hour schedules**, but low event rates, brief follow-up and selective populations limit certainty. Evidence that a **30° lateral tilt** is intrinsically superior is unresolved. The clearest recent positive results are for **small, periodic operating-table movements during prolonged surgery**, which should not be extrapolated directly to routine inpatient bed care.
