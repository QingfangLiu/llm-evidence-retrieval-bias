## Scope

Below is a focused evidence map of **comparative primary studies in adults reporting clinical pressure-injury outcomes**. The search was updated through **July 17, 2026**. Reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee statements, editorials, and comments are excluded from the study set. Studies measuring only interface pressure, blood flow, or other surrogate outcomes are also excluded from the main synthesis.

## 1. Studies directly comparing repositioning frequencies

### Defloor et al., 2005

**Citation:** Defloor T, De Bacquer D, Grypdonck MHF. *The effect of various combinations of turning and pressure reducing devices on the incidence of pressure ulcers.* Int J Nurs Stud. 2005;42(1):37–46. doi:10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2004.05.013.

* **Design/population:** Cluster trial involving 838 Belgian nursing-home residents.
* **Regimens:** Every 2 or 3 hours on a standard mattress; every 4 or 6 hours on a viscoelastic-foam mattress; plus usual-care residents.
* **Results:** Stage II or worse ulcer incidence was approximately 3% with four-hourly turning plus viscoelastic foam, compared with roughly 14%–24% in the other protocol groups. Nonblanchable erythema did not differ materially.
* **Major limitation:** Turning frequency and mattress type were changed together. The result cannot establish that four-hourly turning itself was superior. ([PubMed][1])

### Bergstrom et al., 2013—the TURN trial

**Citation:** Bergstrom N, et al. *Turning for Ulcer ReductioN: A Multisite Randomized Clinical Trial in Nursing Homes.* J Am Geriatr Soc. 2013;61(10):1705–1713. doi:10.1111/jgs.12440.

* **Design/population:** Multisite randomized trial; 942 nursing-home residents analyzed, all at moderate or high risk and cared for on high-density foam mattresses.
* **Regimens:** Repositioning every 2, 3, or 4 hours for three weeks.
* **Results:** Pressure ulcers occurred in 8/321 residents assigned to two-hourly turning, 2/326 assigned to three-hourly turning, and 9/295 assigned to four-hourly turning—2.5%, 0.6%, and 3.1%, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference between schedules, and no full-thickness ulcers developed.
* **Applicability:** Supports the possibility of intervals up to four hours in this particular nursing-home population when high-density foam, reliable turning, and skin surveillance are present. ([AGSJournals][2])

### Manzano et al., 2014

**Citation:** Manzano F, et al. *Comparison of two repositioning schedules for the prevention of pressure ulcers in patients on mechanical ventilation with alternating pressure air mattresses.* Intensive Care Med. 2014;40(11):1679–1687. doi:10.1007/s00134-014-3424-3.

* **Design/population:** Pragmatic randomized trial; 329 mechanically ventilated ICU patients using alternating-pressure air mattresses.
* **Regimens:** Repositioning every 2 versus every 4 hours.
* **Results:** Stage II or worse pressure ulcers developed in 17/165 patients with two-hourly turning and 22/164 with four-hourly turning—10.3% versus 13.4%; HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.46–1.71.
* **Harms/workload:** Two-hourly turning increased the composite of device-related adverse events and approximately doubled repositioning workload: median 21 versus 11 minutes per patient per day.
* **Interpretation:** Two-hourly turning did not demonstrate superiority over four-hourly turning in ventilated patients on this support surface. ([PubMed][3])

### Yap et al., 2022—TEAM-UP

**Citation:** Yap TL, et al. *Effect of Varying Repositioning Frequency on Pressure Injury Prevention in Nursing Home Residents: TEAM-UP Trial Results.* Adv Skin Wound Care. 2022;35. doi:10.1097/01.ASW.0000817840.68588.04.

* **Design/population:** Pragmatic cluster-randomized study in nine nursing homes; effective sample 992 residents.
* **Regimens:** Facility-wide 2-, 3-, or 4-hour schedules for four weeks. Residents used high-density foam mattresses, and wearable sensors cued staff when turns were due.
* **Results:** No new pressure injuries occurred in any arm. Repositioning compliance was 80%, 90%, and 95% in the 2-, 3-, and 4-hour facilities, respectively.
* **Major limitation:** With zero outcome events, the relative effects of the three intervals cannot be estimated. The intervention also combined schedule assignment with sensor cueing and standardized surfaces. ([PMC][4])

### Vanderwee et al., 2007

**Citation:** Vanderwee K, Grypdonck MHF, De Bacquer D, Defloor T. *Effectiveness of turning with unequal time intervals on the incidence of pressure ulcer lesions.* J Adv Nurs. 2007;57(1):59–68. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2648.2006.04060.x.

* **Design/population:** Randomized trial in 16 Belgian elder-care nursing homes; 235 participants.
* **Important eligibility issue:** All participants already had nonblanchable erythema. This is principally a study of **preventing progression**, rather than pure primary prevention.
* **Regimens:** Two hours in a lateral position alternating with four hours supine versus repositioning every four hours.
* **Results:** Stage II–IV ulcers developed in 16.4% versus 21.2%, respectively; the difference was not statistically significant.
* **Interpretation:** Shortening the lateral exposure period did not clearly prevent progression. ([PubMed][5])

## 2. Studies comparing body positions or angles

### Young, 2004

**Citation:** Young T. *The 30 degree tilt position vs the 90 degree lateral and supine positions in reducing the incidence of non-blanching erythema in a hospital inpatient population: a randomised controlled trial.* J Tissue Viability. 2004;14(3):88, 90, 92–96. doi:10.1016/S0965-206X(04)43004-6.

* **Design/population:** Randomized trial; 46 hospital inpatients randomized and 39 completing approximately 24 hours.
* **Comparison:** A 30° lateral tilt versus a sequence using 90° lateral and supine positions; both used approximately 2- to 3-hour intervals.
* **Results:** Five participants developed nonblanchable erythema, with no significant difference between groups. No participant developed open skin loss.
* **Feasibility:** Seventy-eight percent had difficulty adopting or maintaining the 30° tilt.
* **Interpretation:** No demonstrated clinical advantage for 30° tilt in this small, very short study. ([PubMed][6])

### Moore et al., 2011

**Citation:** Moore Z, Cowman S, Conroy RM. *A randomised controlled clinical trial of repositioning, using the 30° tilt, for the prevention of pressure ulcers.* J Clin Nurs. 2011;20(17–18):2633–2644. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2702.2011.03736.x.

* **Design/population:** Pragmatic cluster-randomized trial at 12 sites; 213 older adults followed for four weeks.
* **Comparison:** Three-hourly repositioning overnight using a 30° tilt versus routine six-hourly repositioning using 90° lateral rotation.
* **Results:** Pressure ulcers developed in 3/99 participants—3%—in the intervention group and 13/114—11%—in the control group; p=.035. All ulcers were grade 1 or 2.
* **Major limitation:** Both the **angle and frequency** changed. The result cannot establish whether the benefit came from the 30° tilt, three-hourly turning, or their combination. ([PubMed][7])

### Ghezeljeh et al., 2017

**Citation:** Ghezeljeh TN, et al. *Comparison of the effect of the head of bed elevation to 30 and 45 degrees on the incidence of ventilator associated pneumonia and the risk for pressure ulcers.* Iran Red Crescent Med J. 2017;19(7):e14224. doi:10.5812/ircmj.14224.

* **Design/population:** Three-group randomized ICU trial; 120 mechanically ventilated adults.
* **Comparison:** Head-of-bed elevation of 30°, 45°, or routine care. All patients also received two-hourly repositioning and skin care.
* **Results:** No pressure injuries occurred in any group during three days.
* **Interpretation:** The study provides no usable comparative effect estimate for pressure-injury prevention because follow-up was short and there were no events. ([ResearchGate][8])

### Girard et al., 2014—ancillary PROSEVA analysis

**Citation:** Girard R, Baboi L, Ayzac L, Richard JC, Guérin C; PROSEVA Trial Group. *The impact of patient positioning on pressure ulcers in patients with severe ARDS: results from a multicentre randomised controlled trial on prone positioning.* Intensive Care Med. 2014;40(3):397–403. doi:10.1007/s00134-013-3188-1.

* **Design/population:** Pressure-ulcer analysis within a multicenter randomized trial; 237 severe-ARDS patients allocated to prolonged prone sessions and 229 to supine positioning.
* **Results:** New stage II or worse ulcer incidence was 20.80 versus 14.26 per 1,000 ventilator-days, p=.061, and 13.92 versus 7.72 per 1,000 ICU-days, p=.002, for prone versus supine positioning.
* **Interpretation:** Prolonged prone positioning increased pressure-injury burden. However, proning was a life-saving ARDS treatment rather than a pressure-injury prevention choice, so the finding concerns additional prevention needs during medically indicated proning. ([ResearchGate][9])

### Gay et al., 2025—ESCARD

**Citation:** Gay L, et al. *A Bundle of Interventions to Prevent Pressure Ulcers During Prone Position in Adult Patients With Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: Results of a French Stepped-Wedge Randomized Controlled Trial.* Nurs Crit Care. 2025;30(4):e70084. doi:10.1111/nicc.70084.

* **Design/population:** Multicenter stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial; 156 prone-positioned adults with moderate-to-severe ARDS analyzed.
* **Intervention:** A bundle incorporating a 15° body inclination, specialized cushions, eye protection, skin protection, and head rotation every four hours.
* **Results:** By day 7, a new pressure ulcer was identified in 63.9% during control periods and 54.8% during intervention periods; adjusted OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.39–2.18.
* **Limitations:** The intervention was a bundle, not an isolated position comparison, and disagreement between expert outcome assessors was substantial.
* **Interpretation:** The bundle did not significantly reduce pressure ulcers during prone ventilation. ([PMC][10])

## 3. Micromovement and position-delivery studies

These studies do not identify a conventional bedside turning interval, but they directly test whether **small periodic position changes** or better implementation prevent clinical pressure injuries.

### Jin et al., 2024

**Citation:** Jin YX, et al. *Effect of “micromovement” in preventing intraoperative acquired pressure injuries among patients undergoing surgery in supine position.* Int Wound J. 2024;21(2):e14408. doi:10.1111/iwj.14408.

* **Design/population:** Randomized trial; 200 adults undergoing elective surgery in the supine position.
* **Intervention:** In addition to a sacrococcygeal silicone-foam dressing, the operating table was tilted 15° alternately left and right every hour, maintained for five minutes.
* **Results:** Intraoperative-acquired pressure injuries occurred in 2% with micromovement versus 10% with dressing alone.
* **Interpretation:** A clinically important positive signal for brief hourly off-loading during surgery. ([PubMed][11])

### Zhang et al., 2024

**Citation:** Zhang J, et al. *The effect of micro-movement on prevention of intraoperative acquired pressure injury in overweight patients undergoing posterior lumbar surgery: a randomized controlled trial.* J Orthop Surg Res. 2024;19:823. doi:10.1186/s13018-024-05312-8.

* **Design/population:** Prospective randomized trial; 277 overweight adults undergoing posterior lumbar surgery in the prone position.
* **Comparison:** Operating-table micromovements versus silicone-foam dressings at pressure points.
* **Results:** Intraoperative pressure injuries occurred in 2.9% of the micromovement group and 8.6% of the dressing group.
* **Interpretation:** Supports periodic small position changes in a specific prolonged prone-surgical setting. ([PMC][12])

### Malekara et al., 2025

**Citation:** Malekara NJ, Larti N, Shirzadi A, Bozorgmehr R. *Comparing the effects of curvilinear position and micromovement on prevention of intraoperative acquired pressure injuries among patients undergoing surgery in the supine position: A randomized controlled trial.* Injury. 2025;56(6):112327. doi:10.1016/j.injury.2025.112327.

* **Design/population:** Three-arm randomized trial; 120 randomized and 114 analyzed after supine general surgery.
* **Comparison:** Micromovement, a curvilinear supine position, or control care.
* **Results:** Pressure-injury incidence differed significantly between the three groups, favoring micromovement and curvilinear positioning.
* **Reporting limitation:** The abstract does not provide the event counts for each arm, limiting independent effect-size calculation. ([ScienceDirect][13])

### Pickham et al., 2018—LS-HAPI

**Citation:** Pickham D, et al. *Effect of a wearable patient sensor on care delivery for preventing pressure injuries in acutely ill adults: A pragmatic randomized clinical trial.* Int J Nurs Stud. 2018;80:12–19. doi:10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2017.12.012.

* **Design/population:** Pragmatic randomized trial; 1,312 ICU patients.
* **Comparison:** Sensor-informed visual turning reminders versus traditional reminders and usual turning practice.
* **Results:** Hospital-acquired pressure injuries occurred in 5/659 intervention patients—0.7%—and 15/653 control patients—2.3%; OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.12–0.90. Time compliant with turning increased from 54% to 67%.
* **Interpretation:** Better delivery and monitoring of repositioning reduced injuries, but the study did not compare different prescribed intervals. ([PubMed][14])

### De Meyer et al., 2019—PROTECT

**Citation:** De Meyer D, Van Hecke A, Verhaeghe S, Beeckman D. *PROTECT-Trial: A cluster RCT to study the effectiveness of a repositioning aid and tailored repositioning to increase repositioning compliance.* J Adv Nurs. 2019;75(5):1085–1098. doi:10.1111/jan.13932.

* **Design/population:** Three-arm cluster-randomized pragmatic trial; 227 at-risk adults from 29 wards in 16 hospitals.
* **Interventions:** Tailored repositioning, with or without a commercial turning and repositioning system, compared with control care.
* **Results:** Interventions improved on-time repositioning and increased the proportion correctly placed in a 30°–45° tilted side-lying position. Few pressure ulcers occurred, preventing a reliable clinical-efficacy comparison.
* **Interpretation:** Useful implementation evidence, but not evidence establishing an optimal interval or angle. ([PubMed][15])

### Kapp et al., 2023

**Citation:** Kapp S, et al. *Clinical and cost effectiveness of a system for turning and positioning intensive care unit patients… A randomised controlled trial.* Int Wound J. 2023;20(9):3567–3579. doi:10.1111/iwj.14230.

* **Design/population:** Randomized ICU trial terminated early because of pandemic-related recruitment problems; 78 participants.
* **Comparison:** A dedicated turning-and-positioning system versus usual positioning devices.
* **Results:** Four pressure injuries occurred—two in each group.
* **Interpretation:** Too underpowered to determine effectiveness, although the system appeared feasible and safe. ([PubMed][16])

## Conclusions from the primary studies

### How frequently should adults be repositioned?

The studies do **not** demonstrate that two-hourly turning is universally superior.

* In nursing-home residents using high-density foam mattresses, the TURN and TEAM-UP trials found no higher observed pressure-injury incidence with three- or four-hour schedules than with two-hour schedules, although TEAM-UP had no events and therefore could not estimate comparative effects. ([AGSJournals][2])
* In mechanically ventilated ICU patients using alternating-pressure mattresses, two-hourly turning did not significantly reduce stage II or worse injury compared with four-hourly turning, while increasing device-related events and workload. ([PubMed][3])
* Evidence concerning **six-hour intervals** is sparse and less reassuring, but existing comparisons confound the interval with mattress type or position angle. ([PubMed][1])

Thus, the defensible evidence-synthesis conclusion is not “turn everyone every four hours,” but rather:

> **Intervals up to three or four hours have not produced worse outcomes than two-hourly turning in selected nursing-home and ICU populations when pressure-redistributing surfaces, reliable implementation, and skin surveillance were present. Generalization to all at-risk adults is not established.**

### Which positions work best?

No conventional bed position has been established as universally optimal.

* A **30° lateral tilt** has one favorable trial, but that trial also changed the overnight frequency from six to three hours. A smaller trial using similar frequencies found no benefit and substantial difficulty maintaining the position. ([PubMed][7])
* **Prone positioning** increases the locations and burden of pressure injury compared with supine care, although it may remain medically necessary and life-saving in severe ARDS. ([ResearchGate][9])
* **30° versus 45° head-of-bed elevation** has not been adequately tested for clinical pressure-injury incidence; the available randomized trial had no events during three days. ([ResearchGate][8])
* The most consistent favorable position-related signal is for **small, periodic off-loading movements**, particularly brief 15° operating-table tilts or related micromovements during prolonged surgery. These results are promising but presently setting-specific. ([PubMed][11])

A central synthesis issue is that repositioning is rarely a single intervention. The observed effect generally represents a package of **frequency × angle × support surface × adherence × patient risk**, and many trials do not isolate these components.
