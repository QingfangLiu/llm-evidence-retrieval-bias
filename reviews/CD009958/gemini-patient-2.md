## How Often to Reposition (Frequency)

Historically, you might have heard that "every two hours" is the golden rule. However, primary clinical trials show that the ideal frequency depends heavily on the **type of mattress** you are using.

* **Standard vs. Special Mattresses:** In a large randomized controlled trial, researchers tested turning patients every 2, 3, 4, or 6 hours across different mattress types. They found that turning patients every 4 hours while they were on a viscoelastic foam (pressure-reducing) mattress resulted in significantly fewer pressure injuries compared to the groups turned every 2 or 3 hours on standard hospital mattresses (Defloor et al., 2005).
* **Balancing Risks:** Another trial compared a strict 2-hour versus 4-hour turning schedule for critically ill patients resting on alternating pressure air mattresses. The study found no difference in pressure injury rates between the 2-hour and 4-hour groups. However, turning every 2 hours led to more adverse events (such as disrupting medical tubing) and sleep interference, suggesting that unnecessarily frequent turning on a high-quality mattress might cause unnecessary disruption (Manzano et al., 2014).

**The Takeaway:** If you are resting on a standard mattress, more frequent repositioning is crucial. But if you have a specialized pressure-reducing mattress, repositioning every 4 hours is generally highly effective and allows you to rest more comfortably.

## The Best Body Positions

When turning, the angle of your body plays a massive role in whether pressure is properly relieved or simply shifted to a new vulnerable spot.

* **The 30-Degree Tilt:** Research strongly supports the "30-degree tilt" over lying completely on your side. A clinical trial evaluating older adults at high risk for pressure injuries compared two methods: shifting patients into a 30-degree tilted position every 3 hours at night versus the traditional method of fully rolling them onto their side (a 90-degree turn) every 6 hours. The patients turned using the 30-degree tilt had a significantly lower rate of developing bedsores (Moore et al., 2011).
* **Why it works:** Lying completely flat on your side (90 degrees) places the full weight of your body directly on the trochanter (the prominent bone on the side of your hip), which can quickly cause a bedsore there. A 30-degree tilt—often supported by placing pillows tucked behind your back—shifts your weight away from the hip bone and onto the fleshy, muscular part of your buttocks, which handles pressure much better.
