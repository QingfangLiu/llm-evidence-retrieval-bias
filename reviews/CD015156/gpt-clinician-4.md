## Bottom line

The evidence is concentrated in **calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease)** and **Osgood–Schlatter disease (OSD)**. There is **no clearly superior nonsurgical treatment across lower-limb apophysitis**, and most studies are small, short-term, or lack an untreated comparator.

* **Calcaneal apophysitis:** Mechanical unloading with a heel cup, heel raise, orthosis, or barefoot-sport brace consistently improves symptoms. However, pragmatic trials generally show that differences between devices, physical therapy, and observation diminish by about three months. A simple, inexpensive heel cup or heel raise is therefore the most defensible initial device. One trial favored custom orthoses over heel lifts at 12 weeks, but that isolated result does not establish that custom orthoses are routinely necessary.
* **OSD:** Pain-guided activity modification with progressive knee/hip strengthening has the best balance of supporting evidence, low risk, and applicability. It improves many adolescents, but the latest randomized comparison did not demonstrate superiority over usual care. Injection studies—particularly dextrose—have contradictory results and do not establish injections as the best treatment.
* **Sinding–Larsen–Johansson, Iselin, iliac-crest and other less-common apophysitides:** Evidence consists principally of retrospective series and case reports. These cannot determine which treatment works best.

The search was updated through **July 25, 2026**. All evidence discussed below comes from individual primary studies or a primary trial registry.

## 1. Calcaneal apophysitis: the strongest evidence

### Heel cups, heel raises and orthoses

A crossover randomized study of 51 boys found substantially less sports-related pain with a **heel cup than with a heel wedge**; more than 75% preferred the heel cup. A related trial found that insoles reduced pain while children maintained their usual activity. These studies support mechanical unloading but were small and involved predominantly or exclusively boys. ([PubMed][1])

A pragmatic randomized trial assigned 101 children to:

1. Wait-and-see;
2. A heel raise; or
3. Supervised physical therapy with eccentric exercises.

There were some six-week differences in satisfaction and selected function scores, but **all three groups improved and there were no clinically relevant pain differences at three months**. This suggests that structured PT is not routinely superior to a heel raise or symptom-guided observation. ([PubMed][2])

Similarly, a 124-child factorial RCT comparing heel raises with prefabricated orthoses—and footwear replacement with continued usual footwear—found a small early physical-function advantage for heel raises, but **no meaningful treatment differences at six or 12 months**. Prescribing replacement footwear did not add a durable benefit. ([PubMed][3])

One larger 12-week RCT of 208 children found **greater improvement with custom polypropylene orthoses than with off-the-shelf heel lifts**, including larger changes in pain and pressure-pain threshold. This is evidence that custom orthoses can help selected patients, but the study had no minimal-treatment arm, short follow-up, and has not yet been strongly replicated. ([PMC][4])

### Barefoot-sport braces

In a randomized trial of barefoot athletes, 43 were enrolled and 32 completed follow-up. A heel-cup brace and an elastic heel strap produced similar improvements; at three months there were **no between-device differences in physical function, play, emotional function or pain**. Either device appears reasonable when an in-shoe heel cup cannot be worn. ([PMC][5])

### Kinesiology tape

A small RCT of 22 male soccer players compared kinesiology tape with sham tape. The kinesiology-tape group had a statistically better AOFAS score, but the final scores were very high in both groups—99.7 versus 97.4—and **pain did not differ significantly**. This does not support taping as a primary analgesic treatment. ([PubMed][6])

### Practical interpretation for Sever disease

The primary studies support:

* A **heel cup, heel raise or similar unloading device** as a reasonable low-cost initial intervention.
* Selection based on footwear, sport and comfort rather than an expectation that one standard device is decisively superior.
* **Custom orthoses** as an option for persistent symptoms or relevant foot mechanics, rather than a clearly necessary first-line treatment.
* Physical therapy when strength, flexibility, kinetic-chain or return-to-sport needs warrant it, but not because it has demonstrated consistent superiority over simpler care.
* Taping as an adjunct, not a treatment with established analgesic superiority.

## 2. Osgood–Schlatter disease

### Activity modification and progressive strengthening

A prospective cohort of 51 adolescents with symptoms averaging 21 months used pain monitoring, an activity ladder, progressive knee strengthening and graded return to sport. At 12 weeks, 80% considered themselves improved; this increased to 90% at 12 months. Only 16% had returned to sport by 12 weeks, however, increasing to 69% by one year. Strength, jump performance and several KOOS domains improved. Because there was no control group, the study demonstrates feasibility and improvement but not treatment superiority. ([PubMed][7])

A newer pragmatic RCT randomized 130 adolescents to a standardized self-management program or usual care. Both incorporated combinations of education, exercise and activity modification. The standardized program **was not superior on the primary KOOS-child Sport/Play outcome**, although both groups improved substantially. Only a peer-reviewed conference abstract was available, so complete methods and adverse-event data remain unavailable. ([Aalborg Universitets forskningsportal][8])

A retrospective academy-soccer cohort treated 28 players without immobilization using quadriceps-focused kinesiotherapy, physiotherapy and gradual loading. Mean treatment duration was approximately 27 days, although 36% experienced some discomfort when initially resuming training. Without a comparator, this study cannot distinguish treatment effect from the natural course or athlete-selection effects. ([PubMed][9])

**Interpretation:** Progressive loading, strength restoration and pain-guided sports modification are reasonable first-line care, but the current trials do not prove that a particular exercise protocol is superior to well-delivered usual care.

### Dextrose injections: inconsistent randomized evidence

Three randomized studies reached conflicting conclusions:

* **Topol et al.:** Among 54 adolescent athletes with 65 affected knees, monthly 12.5% dextrose injections produced more asymptomatic sports participation at three months than lidocaine injections or supervised usual care. After three months, crossover to dextrose was allowed, limiting long-term randomized comparisons. ([PubMed][10])
* **Nakase et al.:** In 38 adolescents with 49 affected knees, dextrose plus lidocaine was **not superior** to saline plus lidocaine on VISA scores; both groups improved. ([PubMed][11])
* **Wu et al.:** In 70 participants, ultrasound-guided 12.5% dextrose produced a large three-month VISA-P advantage over saline. Smaller between-group differences persisted at six and 12 months. ([PubMed][12])

Differences in injection formulation, use of anesthetic, ultrasound guidance, patient selection and outcome methods may explain some inconsistency. These data do **not** establish dextrose as reliably superior to noninvasive care.

### Dexamethasone iontophoresis

A triple-masked randomized trial compared:

* PT plus dexamethasone iontophoresis;
* PT plus placebo iontophoresis; and
* PT alone.

The study planned approximately 147 participants but was terminated for slow enrollment after 45. Consequently, it was underpowered to provide a dependable answer regarding return-to-sport time or functional superiority. Results are available principally through the trial registry rather than a full peer-reviewed report. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][13])

### Immobilization

A retrospective cohort evaluated four weeks of long-leg synthetic-resin immobilization in 30 adolescents. Median return to sport was 11 weeks and return to the same performance level was 16 weeks; 90% reported satisfaction. There was no rest-only, rehabilitation or nonimmobilization comparison, so it does not show that casting accelerates recovery. ([ScienceDirect][14])

### Shock-wave therapy

A small uncontrolled series treated 14 adolescents—16 knees—with radial extracorporeal shock-wave therapy for recalcitrant OSD. Long-term outcomes appeared favorable in many knees, but the absence of a control group and the very small sample make efficacy uncertain. ([PubMed][15])

### PRP versus dextrose

A 2025 retrospective study compared 20 adolescents receiving one PRP injection with 20 receiving three 10% dextrose injections. PRP was associated with better 12-week pain, Lysholm scores, satisfaction and earlier return to sport. Treatment was not randomized and depended on availability or preference, creating substantial selection and treatment-intensity bias. This is hypothesis-generating evidence, not proof that PRP is superior. ([PMC][16])

### Practical interpretation for OSD

The most defensible sequence based on the primary evidence is:

1. **Pain-guided load modification**, rather than necessarily complete sports prohibition.
2. Progressive quadriceps, hip and lower-extremity strengthening with a graded return-to-sport plan.
3. Consider short-term protection or immobilization only for unusually severe functional limitation; comparative benefit is unproven.
4. Treat dextrose, PRP, corticosteroid iontophoresis and shock-wave therapy as **uncertain or investigational options**, particularly in skeletally immature patients, because superiority and long-term safety have not been established adequately.

## 3. Other lower-limb apophysitis

### Iselin disease

A retrospective series of 27 athletes with fifth-metatarsal apophysitis used varying combinations of rest, footwear modification, a CAM boot or a postoperative shoe. All healed; average treatment duration was 24 days and average return to play was 38 days. Because treatment selection was not randomized or stratified, the study cannot show whether a boot, shoe modification or rest was best. ([PubMed][17])

### Sinding–Larsen–Johansson disease

Published treatment evidence is predominantly individual cases. For example, a 13-year-old soccer player recovered after temporary sports cessation, with complete clinical and ultrasound recovery reported at five months. A single case cannot separate the effect of treatment from natural resolution. ([PubMed][18])

### Pelvic apophyseal injury

A reported adolescent triathlete with bilateral iliac-crest apophyseal injury returned through a staged rehabilitation program over approximately four months and was pain-free at one year. Again, this is a case report and cannot identify the best intervention. ([PubMed][19])
