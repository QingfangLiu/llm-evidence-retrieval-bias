## Bottom line

The evidence base is small and concentrated in **calcaneal apophysitis (Sever disease)** and **tibial-tubercle apophysitis (Osgood–Schlatter disease, OSD)**. Most trials are modest in size, use heterogeneous outcomes, and compare active treatments rather than treatment with placebo or no treatment.

## Calcaneal apophysitis: heel cups or heel raises are the most practical evidence-based option

Three related Perhamre studies support cushioning or containment of the heel while allowing continued activity. In 38 boys, both randomized insole types substantially reduced activity-related pain compared with periods without insoles. In a subsequent 51-boy crossover study, a custom-molded heel cup reduced the odds of pain by approximately 80% compared with a heel wedge, and more than 75% preferred the heel cup. A third randomized mechanistic study found that the cup improved heel-pad thickness and pressure distribution, with changes associated with pain reduction. ([PubMed][1])

Larger pragmatic trials suggest, however, that the advantage of any particular device is modest or temporary:

* In 124 children, a heel raise had a small advantage over a prefabricated orthosis at one to two months, but there was no meaningful difference by six or 12 months. Replacing the child’s footwear did not improve outcomes. ([PubMed][2])
* In 101 children randomized to wait-and-see, a heel raise, or supervised eccentric exercise/physical therapy, all groups improved. There were some six-week satisfaction and functional differences, but no clinically relevant differences at three months. ([PubMed][3])
* One 208-participant trial reported considerably greater 12-week pain improvement with custom polypropylene orthoses than with off-the-shelf heel lifts. Because both groups received devices and the result conflicts somewhat with the larger factorial trial, this does not establish that custom orthoses are routinely necessary. ([PMC][4])

Other modalities have not demonstrated a clear advantage. Kinesiology tape improved one functional score but did not reduce pain more than sham tape. Two heel braces designed for barefoot sports produced similar three-month improvement, without evidence that either was superior. ([PubMed][5])

A 2026 single-cohort feasibility study found that daily Achilles loading, pain-guided activity modification and education were feasible and generally well accepted, but it had only 13 participants and no comparator, so it does not establish efficacy. ([PubMed][6])

### Practical interpretation for Sever disease

A reasonable evidence-aligned starting point is:

**Pain-guided activity modification plus a low-cost heel cup or heel raise**, chosen according to shoe fit, sport and patient comfort. Supervised rehabilitation is reasonable when weakness, limited calf capacity, movement deficits or difficulty progressing activity are present, but it has not been shown to produce better medium-term outcomes than simpler management.

Custom orthoses may be justified for comfort, substantial foot-mechanics concerns or failure of a simpler insert, but the evidence does not consistently show that they are worth routinely prescribing as first-line treatment.

## Osgood–Schlatter disease: active rehabilitation helps, but the optimal program remains uncertain

A prospective cohort of 51 adolescents used education, a pain-monitoring/activity ladder, progressive strengthening and graded return to sport. Eighty percent described the outcome as successful at 12 weeks and 90% at 12 months. Only 16% had returned to sport at 12 weeks, increasing to 69% at 12 months. The lack of a comparator means the improvement cannot be separated from natural recovery, regression to the mean or nonspecific care effects. ([PMC][7])

The more informative SOGOOD randomized trial enrolled 130 adolescents. A standardized self-management program incorporating exercise, education and activity modification was **not superior to usual care** on KOOS-Child Sport/Play at five months; the between-group difference in change was −4.8 points. Both groups improved by more than 20 points. The findings are currently available as a peer-reviewed conference abstract rather than a full results paper, which limits appraisal of adherence, missing data and secondary outcomes. ([BMJ Open Seminars][8])

A retrospective cohort of 28 academy soccer players treated without immobilization—using quadriceps flexibility work, physical modalities and gradual activity progression—reported a mean treatment duration of 27 days, although more than one-third had some discomfort when regular training resumed. There was no untreated or alternative-treatment group. ([PubMed][9])

### Rehabilitation interpretation

The studies support providing **active, individualized care rather than simply telling the adolescent to wait for skeletal maturity**, but they do not establish that one exercise dosage, pain threshold or progression algorithm is superior. “Usual care” containing sensible education, load adjustment, exercise and follow-up may perform as well as a highly standardized package.

## OSD injections and physical modalities

### Dextrose injection

The two blinded pediatric trials conflict:

* Topol et al. randomized 54 adolescent athletes with 65 affected knees to usual care, lidocaine injection, or lidocaine plus 12.5% dextrose. At three months, return to unaltered sport occurred in 21/21 dextrose-treated knees, 20/22 lidocaine-treated knees and 13/22 usual-care knees. Asymptomatic sport participation was also more common following dextrose. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][10])
* Nakase et al. randomized adolescents with refractory symptoms to 20% dextrose plus lidocaine or saline plus lidocaine. Both groups improved, but there was no significant between-group difference at any measured time point. ([PubMed][11])

Differences in injection composition, comparator care, patient selection and outcome definitions may explain some inconsistency. Overall, the evidence is insufficient to support dextrose as routine first-line treatment; it remains an uncertain option for selected recalcitrant cases.

### Iontophoresis, interferential therapy and osteopathic treatment

A US randomized trial of dexamethasone iontophoresis plus physical therapy versus saline iontophoresis plus physical therapy versus physical therapy alone planned to enroll 147 participants but was terminated for slow recruitment after enrolling 45. Registry results were posted, but no peer-reviewed full paper was identified; the small final groups preclude a firm effectiveness conclusion. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][12])

A 2025 Russian study randomized 90 adolescents to exercise alone, exercise plus calcium-phosphorus iontophoresis, or exercise plus iontophoresis and interferential therapy. The combined group reportedly had better 12-month pain, knee-flexion and ultrasound outcomes. The brief report provides limited information about concealment, blinding, attrition and numerical between-group changes, so replication is needed before adopting this treatment package. ([Vrach (The Doctor)][13])

Another 2025 study randomized 30 boys to rehabilitation with or without osteopathic treatment. The combined group had better one-month pain and step-down-test findings and fewer ultrasound inflammatory findings at three months. The very small sample, exclusively male population and limited reporting make this preliminary evidence. ([Hep Journal][14])

## Iselin disease

A 2026 prospective cohort compared removable bracing or strapping plus early physical therapy with strict activity cessation in 44 adolescents. The combined-treatment group returned to sport in a mean of 5.8 weeks versus 8.3 weeks and had lower four-week pain scores. Because allocation was not reported as randomized, treatment-selection and prognostic differences could account for some of the advantage. ([ScienceDirect][15])

An earlier retrospective series of 27 patients used varying combinations of rest, footwear modification, a CAM boot or postoperative shoe. All healed, with mean return to play at 38 days, but the study could not determine which intervention was best. ([PubMed][16])

## Overall clinical takeaway

For **Sever disease**, begin with the least burdensome supported intervention: a heel cup or heel raise, pain-guided activity adjustment and progressive return. No evidence supports routinely escalating directly to custom orthoses or prolonged complete rest.

For **OSD**, use education, individualized load management, lower-extremity strengthening and planned progression back to sport. Continued follow-up may matter more than selecting one branded protocol. Injections and electrotherapeutic modalities should be considered investigational or selectively used for refractory cases, not routine first-line care.

For **Iselin disease**, brief relative immobilization combined with early rehabilitation is promising but needs randomized confirmation.
