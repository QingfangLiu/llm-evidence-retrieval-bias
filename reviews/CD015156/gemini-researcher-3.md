When synthesizing the primary literature on nonsurgical management of lower-limb apophysitis, the evidence base leans heavily toward conservative management—primarily focusing on load modification, orthoses, and physical therapy. Below is a breakdown of the primary studies evaluating these interventions, categorized by condition.

## Calcaneal Apophysitis (Sever's Disease)

For calcaneal apophysitis, primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) indicate that most conservative treatments yield similar long-term outcomes, though short-term differences exist depending on the mechanical support provided.

* **Wait-and-See vs. Physical Therapy vs. Heel Raises:** A pragmatic RCT by Wiegerinck et al. (2016) compared a "wait-and-see" approach against physical therapy (eccentric exercises) and heel raise inlays. At three months, all three groups demonstrated significant and clinically relevant reductions in heel pain, with no single intervention proving statistically superior to the others. The authors concluded that treatment choice can largely be driven by patient and parent preference.
* **Orthoses and Footwear Modification:** James et al. (2016) conducted a factorial RCT examining heel raises versus prefabricated orthoses (with or without footwear replacement). At the two-month mark, heel raises offered a slight functional advantage over prefabricated orthoses. However, at 12 months, there was no longer any relative advantage among the treatments.
* **Heel Cups:** Evaluating the *shape* of the orthotic, a crossover RCT found that a heel cup (which fixates the heel pad) reduced pain odds by 80% compared to a traditional heel wedge, and more than 75% of the children preferred it (Perhamre et al., 2011).
* **Barefoot Athletes:** For gymnasts or martial artists who cannot wear standard orthotics in footwear, Sweeney et al. (2023) compared two specialized braces (Tuli's Cheetah heel cup vs. The X Brace). Over three months, both braces led to equivalent and significant improvements in pain and function, with no between-group differences.

## Tibial Tuberosity Apophysitis (Osgood-Schlatter Disease)

The literature surrounding Osgood-Schlatter disease (OSD) relies heavily on observational data regarding conservative care, though some trials exist for recalcitrant cases.

* **General Conservative Management:** A large retrospective cohort study mapping general practice data highlighted that OSD is primarily managed through **advice and education (55.1%)**, followed by **rest (21%)**, and **physiotherapy (13.4%)** (van Leeuwen et al., 2021). The reliance on education and load management tracks with the largely self-limiting nature of the condition.
* **Injection Therapies (Refractory cases):** When standard conservative therapy fails, clinicians sometimes explore injections. Nakase et al. (2019) conducted a double-blind RCT comparing 20% dextrose injections to saline placebo injections. While both groups saw significant improvements in their Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment (VISA) scores at one month, the dextrose injection failed to show any superiority over the placebo, suggesting that the mechanical effect of the injection or the natural history of the disease drove the improvement.
