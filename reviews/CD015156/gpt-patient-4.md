## Bottom line

**No single nonsurgical treatment has been shown to be best for every lower-limb apophysitis.** The evidence depends heavily on location:

* **Heel—calcaneal apophysitis/Sever disease:** The most treatment trials are available here. A simple heel cup or heel raise can reduce symptoms, particularly during the first one to two months, but exercise-based physical therapy, activity modification, and watchful waiting generally produce similar outcomes by about three months. Custom orthoses produced a large benefit in one trial, but that finding conflicts with other comparative trials and needs independent replication.
* **Knee—Osgood-Schlatter disease:** Pain-guided activity modification, progressive strengthening, and gradual return to sport have the best balance of plausibility, safety, and supportive evidence, but superiority over individualized usual care has not been demonstrated. Dextrose injections have conflicting randomized-trial results.
* **Hip, Sinding-Larsen-Johansson disease, and Iselin disease:** Evidence consists almost entirely of small retrospective series and case reports. These studies cannot determine which treatment is best.

## Heel apophysitis: the strongest evidence

### Heel cups and heel raises

Several studies support heel cushioning or elevation for **short-term symptom relief**:

* In 51 boys, a heel cup produced substantially less sports-related pain than a heel wedge in a randomized crossover comparison; more than 75% preferred the cup. ([PubMed][1])
* A related study of 38 boys found significantly less activity-related pain while wearing randomized insoles than during periods without them, despite maintaining sports participation. ([PubMed][2])
* A mechanistic study found that a heel cup increased functional heel-pad thickness, reduced peak heel pressure, and reduced pain compared with a sports shoe alone. ([PubMed][3])
* In a 124-child factorial trial, heel raises had a small advantage over prefabricated orthoses in the physical-function domain at one and two months, but there were no meaningful differences at six or twelve months. Replacing the child’s footwear did not provide an additional lasting benefit. ([PubMed][4])

**Interpretation:** A relatively inexpensive heel cup or heel raise is the most consistently supported mechanical treatment to try first for heel apophysitis, especially when the principal goal is short-term comfort. It has not been shown to shorten the entire condition or outperform other approaches over the longer term.

### Physical therapy, exercise, and activity modification

A pragmatic randomized trial assigned 101 children to watchful waiting, a heel-raise inlay, or supervised eccentric exercise/physical therapy. The heel-raise group reported greater early satisfaction, and both the heel-raise and physical-therapy groups had some early functional advantages. By three months, however, all three groups had improved and there was no clinically important difference in pain between them. ([PubMed][5])

A 2026 feasibility study enrolled 13 children in a 12-week program of daily Achilles loading, education, and pain-guided activity modification. Adherence and satisfaction were high, with no treatment-related adverse events, but the study had no comparison group and was designed to test feasibility—not efficacy. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** Exercise and pain-guided activity modification appear reasonable, but current evidence does not show that formal physical therapy is routinely superior to a heel raise or a simpler wait-and-adjust-activity approach.

### Custom orthoses

A randomized trial of 208 children aged 9–12 compared custom polypropylene orthoses with off-the-shelf heel lifts for 12 weeks. Both groups were also advised to stretch the calf, reduce sports intensity without necessarily stopping, and use ice. The custom-orthosis group had a much larger reduction in pain and improvement in pressure-pain threshold. ([PMC][7])

However, another large trial found only a small early advantage for a simple heel raise over a prefabricated orthosis, with no later difference, and the pragmatic trial found no three-month superiority of heel raises or physical therapy over watchful waiting. ([PubMed][4])

**Interpretation:** Custom orthoses may be useful for selected children—particularly those with persistent symptoms or substantial foot-mechanics issues—but the evidence does not yet establish them as routinely superior enough to justify their additional cost for every child.

### Taping and braces

A small randomized trial of 22 male soccer players compared Kinesio tape with sham tape. Functional scores were slightly better with Kinesio tape, but pain did not differ significantly between groups. ([PubMed][8])

Among barefoot athletes, 43 children were randomized to two different heel braces; 32 completed the study. Both groups improved, with no difference between braces in pain or function at three months. ([PubMed][9])

**Interpretation:** Taping has not demonstrated a meaningful pain advantage. For children participating in gymnastics, dance, martial arts, or other barefoot sports, either studied brace appears reasonable, with no evidence that one is better.

## Knee apophysitis

### Osgood-Schlatter: load management and strengthening

A prospective cohort of 51 adolescents with longstanding Osgood-Schlatter symptoms used a 12-week program combining a pain/load-management “activity ladder,” progressive knee strengthening, and gradual return to sport. At 12 weeks, 80% reported improvement, rising to 90% at one year. Strength and jump performance improved, but only 16% had returned to sport by 12 weeks and 69% by one year. Because there was no control group, the study cannot separate treatment effects from natural improvement or other factors. ([PubMed][10])

A subsequent randomized trial involving 130 adolescents compared a standardized self-management program with usual care. Both contained some combination of education, exercise, and activity modification. At five months, the standardized program was not superior; both groups improved by more than 20 points on the KOOS-Child Sport/Play scale, although approximately 36%–44% had not reached an acceptable symptom state. These findings are currently reported as a conference abstract rather than a complete peer-reviewed paper. ([BMJ Open Seminars][11])

**Interpretation:** An active program based on load adjustment, progressive strength, and a gradual return to sport is the most defensible low-risk approach, but no particular standardized protocol has proved better than individualized usual care. Improvement may take months rather than weeks.

### Dextrose injections

Two pediatric randomized trials conflict:

* In 54 adolescent athletes involving 65 knees, monthly lidocaine injections with or without 12.5% dextrose were compared with supervised usual care. At three months, both injection groups more often achieved unaltered sports participation than usual care; complete symptom freedom was more frequent with dextrose. The study’s later phase allowed crossover to dextrose, making the one-year comparison less straightforward. ([PubMed][12])
* In 38 adolescents involving 49 knees, three monthly injections of 20% dextrose plus lidocaine were compared with saline plus lidocaine. Both groups improved, but there was no significant difference at any measured time point. ([PubMed][13])

**Interpretation:** Pediatric evidence does not consistently show that dextrose itself is better than a placebo injection. Given the invasive nature of injections and the conflicting results, dextrose cannot currently be considered the established best treatment or a routine first-line intervention.

### Dexamethasone iontophoresis

A randomized, triple-masked trial compared physical therapy plus dexamethasone iontophoresis, physical therapy plus saline iontophoresis, and physical therapy alone. It enrolled only 45 of a planned 147 participants and was terminated because of slow enrollment; results were posted to the trial registry, but no peer-reviewed primary article was identified. The marked under-recruitment prevents a firm conclusion. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][14])

### Sinding-Larsen-Johansson disease

I found no comparative treatment trial. A case report described a 13-year-old soccer player who stopped sports and had complete clinical and ultrasound recovery after five months. A single case cannot establish that complete sports abstinence is necessary or superior to pain-guided modification. ([PubMed][15])

## Other foot and hip apophysitis

### Iselin disease—fifth-metatarsal apophysitis

A retrospective series included 27 adolescent athletes treated variously with rest, footwear modification, a controlled-ankle-motion boot, or a postoperative shoe. All healed; the mean treatment duration was 24 days and mean return to play was 38 days. Because treatment selection was not randomized and outcomes were not reported separately by treatment, the study cannot show whether a boot, shoe, or rest is best. ([PubMed][16])

### Hip and pelvic apophysitis

The available evidence is case-level:

* Four athletes aged 7–13 with greater-trochanter apophysitis stopped sports and completed flexibility and strengthening therapy. All returned to sport after approximately 8–12 weeks; one later had recurrent symptoms. ([ScienceDirect][17])
* A 15-year-old basketball player with greater-trochanter apophysitis returned to full activity after relative rest and anti-inflammatory medication. ([PubMed][18])
* A 16-year-old football player with iliac apophysitis recovered with activity modification, temporarily limited weight bearing, and physical therapy. ([PubMed][19])
* Another 16-year-old with right iliac-crest apophysitis and a separate contralateral avulsion injury returned to triathlon following a four-month staged running and return-to-sport progression. ([PubMed][20])

**Interpretation:** Relative rest or activity modification followed by progressive flexibility, strength, and sport-specific loading appears successful in reported cases, but there is no comparative evidence identifying the optimal duration, exercises, medication, or weight-bearing restrictions.

## Overall treatment hierarchy from these studies

For a child with a confirmed apophysitis, the evidence most strongly supports the following general hierarchy:

1. **Pain-guided reduction of the provoking load**, rather than assuming the child must remain completely inactive for a fixed period.
2. **Progressive strength and gradual return to sport**, particularly for Osgood-Schlatter disease and hip apophysitis.
3. **A simple heel cup or heel raise for Sever disease**, especially for short-term symptom relief.
4. **More costly custom orthoses only selectively**, because one favorable trial has not been consistently replicated.
5. **Avoid treating taping, dextrose injection, or dexamethasone iontophoresis as proven superior treatments.**

These conclusions concern children whose diagnosis is actually apophysitis. Acute inability to bear weight, a sudden pop, marked weakness, systemic symptoms, or an atypical course may indicate an avulsion fracture or another diagnosis rather than uncomplicated overuse apophysitis.
