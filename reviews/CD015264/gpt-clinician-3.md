## Scope

I included studies in which vitamin B12 was given **directly to children younger than 12 years** and compared with placebo, no treatment, or an otherwise similar supplement without B12. I grouped multiple publications from the same randomized cohort together so they are not mistaken for separate trials.

I excluded maternal-only supplementation, observational studies, and multinutrient interventions in which the contribution of B12 could not be distinguished from the other ingredients.

## 1. Nepal infant supplementation trial — NCT02272842

**Population/intervention:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months were randomized to oral vitamin B12, 2 μg daily, or placebo for 12 months. Both groups received the same 15 additional vitamins and minerals, making this the cleanest trial of adding B12 to an otherwise identical supplement.

### Primary reports

* **Strand et al., 2020 — “Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese infants.”**
  B12 supplementation improved biochemical status: methylmalonic acid and total homocysteine were lower than with placebo. It did **not** improve Bayley-III developmental scores, linear growth, weight gain, or hemoglobin. Intervention-related adverse events were not identified. ([PubMed][1])

* **Hysing et al., 2022 — sleep outcomes.**
  B12 did not improve nighttime sleep duration, total sleep duration, or wake time after sleep onset. A small difference in sleep-onset latency did not support an overall sleep benefit. ([PubMed][2])

* **Ulak et al., 2023 — neurodevelopmental follow-up.**
  At approximately 30–35 and 42–47 months of age, the groups did not differ in WPPSI-IV full-scale IQ or the other measured developmental outcomes. ([PubMed][3])

* **Secondary telomere analysis.**
  Supplementation did not affect leukocyte telomere length; the standardized mean difference was 0.04, with a 95% confidence interval from −0.14 to 0.21. ([PubMed][4])

* **Strand et al., 2026 — infections and clinic visits.**
  B12 did not reduce clinic attendance or the incidence of the 20 assessed illness outcomes. The incidence-rate ratio for clinic visits was 0.99, 95% CI 0.85–1.14. ([PubMed][5])

**Interpretation:** B12 clearly improved metabolic markers of B12 status, but this did not translate into detectable improvements in growth, anemia, development, sleep, or common infections.

## 2. India factorial trial — NCT00717730

**Population/intervention:** Approximately 1,000 children aged 6–30 months were randomized in a factorial design to vitamin B12, 1.8 μg/day; folic acid, 150 μg/day; both; or placebo for six months.

### Primary reports

* **Taneja et al., 2013 — diarrhea and respiratory infections.**
  Vitamin B12 did not reduce diarrhea or lower respiratory tract infections. Persistent diarrhea was more frequent in folic-acid-containing groups, but the study did not establish that B12 caused this finding. ([American Journal of Clinical Nutrition][6])

* **Strand et al., 2015 — growth.**
  B12 produced a very small improvement in weight-for-age z score: mean difference 0.07, 95% CI 0.01–0.13. Some larger effects were reported in children who were wasted, underweight, or stunted at baseline, but there was no convincing general improvement in linear growth. ([AAP Publications][7])

* **Kvestad et al., 2015 — early development, substudy of 422 children.**
  B12 alone improved the measured gross-motor score. B12 plus folate improved several selected developmental measures, but most developmental outcomes were not significantly different. ([PMC][8])

* **Taneja et al. — hemoglobin substudy.**
  B12, folic acid, or their combination did not improve hemoglobin. Hemoglobin declined in all groups, and approximately 90% of the assessed children remained anemic. ([PubMed][9])

* **Kvestad et al., 2020 — follow-up at 6–9 years.**
  Among 791 re-enrolled children, early B12 and/or folic acid supplementation did not improve WISC-IV India scores, verbal ability, executive function, or the other measured neuropsychological outcomes. ([PubMed][10])

* **Upadhyay et al., 2023 — cardiometabolic follow-up.**
  Children originally assigned to combined B12 and folic acid had lower total homocysteine approximately six years later. Some subgroup differences in adipokines were reported, but there was no clear general clinical benefit attributable to B12 alone. ([PMC][11])

**Interpretation:** The trial found a **small short-term weight-for-age effect** and one gross-motor signal, but no reduction in infections, no improvement in hemoglobin, and no lasting cognitive benefit.

## 3. Guatemala infant feeding and B12 trial

* **Allen et al., 2007 — “Vitamin B12 Status and Development of Young Guatemalan Children: Effects of Beef and B12 Supplements.”**
  In this trial, 304 infants aged approximately 12 months were assigned to beef providing about 0.56 μg B12/day, a B12-fortified isocaloric fruit-and-vegetable supplement providing about 0.86 μg/day, or an unfortified fruit-and-vegetable control. Foods were provided five days per week until approximately 21 months. Plasma B12 increased most in the fortified group, but no significant treatment effects on growth or development were reported. The available primary publication is a conference abstract, so methods and outcome reporting are limited. ([ResearchGate][12])

**Interpretation:** No demonstrated growth or developmental benefit, but confidence is limited because the report is abstract-only.

## Parenteral B12 studies in infants

These trials address **intramuscular treatment of biochemical B12 dysfunction**, rather than routine oral nutritional supplementation.

### 4. Norwegian biochemical-status trial

* **“Common metabolic profile in infants indicating impaired cobalamin status responds to cobalamin supplementation.”**
  A total of 107 six-week-old infants were randomized to a 400 μg intramuscular cobalamin injection or no intervention. At four months, treated infants had substantially higher B12 and lower methylmalonic acid and homocysteine. The report primarily evaluated biochemical outcomes rather than child development or growth. ([PubMed][13])

### 5. Norwegian symptomatic-infant trial

* **Torsvik et al., 2013 — “Cobalamin supplementation improves motor development and regurgitations in infants.”**
  Seventy-nine infants younger than eight months with biochemical evidence of impaired B12 function and feeding or developmental symptoms received 400 μg intramuscular hydroxycobalamin or sham treatment. At one month, B12 reduced homocysteine by 54% and methylmalonic acid by 84%. Alberta Infant Motor Scale improvement was greater with B12—7.0 versus 4.5 points—and regurgitation improved in 69% versus 29%. ([PubMed][14])

**Interpretation:** In clinically selected, symptomatic infants with biochemical B12 dysfunction, parenteral treatment produced short-term biochemical and functional benefits. This population is substantially different from unselected community children.

## Overall clinical conclusion

Across community-based oral-supplementation trials, vitamin B12 **consistently improves biochemical B12 status**, particularly methylmalonic acid and homocysteine. It has **not consistently improved** linear growth, hemoglobin or anemia, infections, sleep, general neurodevelopment, or later cognitive performance. ([PubMed][1])

The positive clinical findings are limited to:

* a very small improvement in weight-for-age in the Indian trial;
* a short-term gross-motor signal in an Indian substudy; and
* improved motor performance and regurgitation after intramuscular treatment in symptomatic infants with biochemical B12 dysfunction. ([AAP Publications][7])

Most evidence comes from infants and toddlers in populations with relatively high nutritional risk. There is little direct trial evidence for routine B12 supplementation in otherwise healthy older children, particularly school-aged children in higher-income settings.
