Based on my searches, here are individual primary studies (randomized controlled trials) examining vitamin B12 supplementation vs. placebo/no supplementation/same-supplement-without-B12 in children under 12, evaluating nutrition/health outcomes. I've excluded reviews, meta-analyses, and guideline documents.

## Growth outcomes

**Strand TA, et al. Vitamin B-12, Folic Acid, and Growth in 6- to 30-Month-Old Children: A Randomized Controlled Trial.** *Pediatrics.* 2015;135(4):e918–e926.
Factorial RCT, 1000 North Indian children 6–35 months, randomized to placebo, vitamin B12, folic acid, or both for 6 months. Children who received vitamin B-12 increased their mean weight-for-age z scores by 0.07 (95% CI: 0.01–0.13). Effects on weight and height were more pronounced in wasted, underweight, and stunted subgroups.

**Manapurath R, et al. Vitamin B12 and/or folic acid supplementation on linear growth: a 6-year follow-up.** *Br J Nutr.* 2023 (follow-up of the same trial above).
In 791 children followed 6 years after the original 6-month intervention, there was a small, non-significant effect of vitamin B12 on linear growth and no effect of folic acid.

**Strand TA, et al. Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese Infants: A randomized controlled trial.** *PLoS Medicine.* 2020.
Community-based, individually randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 600 marginally stunted 6–11-month-old Bhaktapur, Nepal infants, randomized 1:1 to vitamin B12 (2 µg/day) or placebo for 12 months, both groups also receiving 15 other micronutrients at ~1 RDA. No effects of the intervention on Bayley-III developmental scores, growth, or hemoglobin concentration were found; mean height difference was 0.20 cm (95% CI −0.23 to 0.63, P=0.354) and hemoglobin difference was 0.02 g/dL (95% CI −1.33 to 1.37, P=0.978).

## Neurodevelopment/motor outcomes

**Torsvik I, et al. Cobalamin supplementation improves motor development and regurgitations in infants: results from a randomized intervention study.** *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2013;98(5):1233–1240.
Norwegian infants <8 months old with biochemical signs of impaired cobalamin function (n=79) were randomized to receive 400 µg hydroxycobalamin intramuscularly or a sham injection. Cobalamin supplementation reduced plasma total homocysteine by 54% and methylmalonic acid by 84%, with no significant biochemical change in the placebo group, and the increase in Alberta Infant Motor Scale score was higher in the cobalamin group.

**Torsvik IK, et al. Motor development related to duration of exclusive breastfeeding, B vitamin status and B12 supplementation in infants with a birth weight between 2000–3000 g.** *BMC Pediatr.* 2015;15:218. (Related randomized intervention trial, Norway.)

**Kvestad I, et al. Vitamin B12 and Folic Acid Improve Gross Motor and Problem-Solving Skills in Young North Indian Children: A Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.** *PLoS One.* 2015;10(6):e0129915.
Total ASQ-3 developmental score was 12.6 points higher (95% CI −1.1 to 26.3, P=0.071) in children given 6 months of vitamin B12/folic acid vs. placebo, with significant improvements in Gross Motor (P=0.001) and Problem-Solving (P=0.048) subscales (same North Indian cohort as the Strand growth trial above).

## Hemoglobin/anemia outcomes

**Strand TA, et al. Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: A randomized placebo controlled trial.** *Clin Nutr.* 2016.
In the same North Indian factorial RCT (children randomized to vitamin B12, folic acid, both, or placebo for 6 months, with iron given to anemic children for the first 2 months), mean hemoglobin concentration decreased in all four groups over 6 months, and supplementation with either or both vitamins did not improve hemoglobin.

## Other micronutrient/B12 status outcomes (multi-nutrient supplements vs. comparator, isolating B12 effects)

**Hinnouho GM, et al. Daily supplementation of a multiple micronutrient powder improves folate but not thiamine, riboflavin, or vitamin B12 status among young Laotian children: a randomized controlled trial.** *Eur J Nutr.* 2022.
Double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT; 1704 Laotian children aged 6–23 months individually randomized to daily micronutrient powder (containing 0.9 µg vitamin B12 plus 13 other micronutrients) or placebo for ~36 weeks.

**Kawuma R, et al. Multiple micronutrient supplementation improves vitamin B12 and folate concentrations of HIV infected children in Uganda: a randomized controlled trial.** *Nutr J.* 2011 (approx.).
Ugandan HIV-infected children aged 1–5 years randomized to a 14-nutrient supplement at 2× RDA (n=104) vs. a 6-multivitamin "standard of care" supplement (n=114); serum vitamin B12 and folate were measured pre/post supplementation.

## Cardiometabolic follow-up

**Manapurath R, et al. Daily Folic Acid and/or Vitamin B12 Supplementation Between 6 and 30 Months of Age and Cardiometabolic Risk Markers After 6–7 Years: A Follow-Up of a Randomized Controlled Trial.** *J Nutr.* 2023.
Follow-up of the 2×2 factorial double-blind RCT of vitamin B12 and/or folic acid supplementation in 6–30-month-old children (supplement contained 1.8 µg vitamin B12, 150 µg folic acid, or both for 6 months), re-examining 791 children 6–7 years later for plasma homocysteine, leptin, and adiponectin.

## Nested cognitive/status study

**Effects of dietary intervention on vitamin B12 status and cognitive level of 18-month-old toddlers in high-poverty areas: a cluster-randomized controlled trial.** *Nutr J* (or similar), 2019.
Nested within a cluster-RCT in 60 villages in Xichou County, China; 6-month-old infants randomized to 50g/day pork, fortified cereal, or local cereal supplement for one year; a subsample of 180 toddlers had serum B12/homocysteine and neurodevelopment assessed at 18 months. (Note: this compares food sources rather than isolated B12 supplements, but is relevant as a primary comparative study.)

---

**Note on age range:** Most available pediatric B12 RCTs cluster in infants and toddlers (6 months–3 years), reflecting where clinical and public-health interest in B12 deficiency is concentrated; I found few RCTs specifically in school-age children (5–12 years) using isolated B12 supplementation with nutrition/health outcomes — most trials in that age band focus on adolescents or use B12 as part of iron-folic acid programs for anemia rather than isolating B12 effects.
