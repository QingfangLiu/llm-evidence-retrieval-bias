## Eligibility applied

I treated a study as eligible when vitamin B12 was administered **directly to children younger than 12 years** and compared with placebo, no supplementation, or an otherwise identical supplement without B12. Multiple publications from one randomized cohort are grouped as a single study. The citations below are all **primary research reports**.

## Well-described randomized trials

### 1. North India factorial trial — NCT00717730

**Population/design:** 1,000 children approximately 6–30 months old; individually randomized, double-blind, 2×2 factorial trial.

**Interventions for six months:**

* Vitamin B12, 1.8 μg/day
* Folic acid, 150 μg/day
* Both
* Placebo

The factorial design provides B12-specific contrasts, including B12 versus placebo and B12 plus folic acid versus folic acid alone.

**Primary reports**

* **Taneja S, et al.** *Folic acid and vitamin B-12 supplementation and common infections in 6–30-mo-old children in India: a randomized placebo-controlled trial.* **Am J Clin Nutr. 2013;98:731–737.** DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.113.059592. B12 improved B12 status but did not reduce diarrhea or lower-respiratory-infection incidence. ([PubMed][1])
* **Strand/Taneja and colleagues.** *Vitamin B-12, folic acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children: a randomized controlled trial.* **Pediatrics. 2015;135:e918–e926.** DOI: 10.1542/peds.2014-1848; PMID 25802345. The overall B12 effect was a small improvement in weight-for-age z score, mean difference 0.07, 95% CI 0.01–0.13. Larger growth signals were reported in children who were wasted, underweight, or stunted at baseline. ([AAP Publications][2])
* **Kvestad I, et al.** *Vitamin B12 and folic acid improve gross motor and problem-solving skills in young North Indian children: a randomized placebo-controlled trial.* **PLoS One. 2015;10:e0129915.** DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0129915. In a developmental substudy, combined B12–folic acid improved gross-motor and problem-solving scores versus placebo; the B12 factorial effect was clearest for gross-motor development. ([PMC][3])
* **Kumar T, et al.** *Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: a randomized placebo controlled trial.* **Clin Nutr. 2017;36:986–991.** DOI: 10.1016/j.clnu.2016.07.002; PMID 27486122. Neither B12 nor folic acid improved hemoglobin after six months. ([PubMed][4])
* **Kvestad I, et al.** *Vitamin B12, folate, and cognition in 6- to 9-year-olds: a randomized controlled trial.* **Pediatrics. 2020;145:e20192316.** DOI: 10.1542/peds.2019-2316; PMID 32019814. At long-term follow-up, early-childhood supplementation did not improve general or domain-specific cognitive outcomes. ([PubMed][5])
* **Manapurath R, et al.** *Daily folic acid and/or vitamin B12 supplementation between 6 and 30 months of age and cardiometabolic risk markers after 6–7 years.* **J Nutr. 2023;153:1493–1501.** DOI: 10.1016/j.tjnut.2023.03.003; PMID 36889645. Combined B12 and folic acid produced a modest persistent reduction in total homocysteine, but this was a biomarker outcome rather than demonstrated clinical benefit. ([PubMed][6])

**Trial-level interpretation:** Possible small short-term effects on weight and selected developmental domains, particularly among undernourished children, but no benefit for infections, hemoglobin, or later childhood cognition.

### 2. Nepal infant trial — NCT02272842

**Population/design:** 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months; individually randomized and double-blind.

**Intervention:** Vitamin B12 2 μg/day versus placebo for 12 months. Importantly, **both groups received the same 15 other vitamins and minerals**, so the contrast isolates B12.

**Primary reports**

* **Strand TA, et al.** *Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese infants: a randomized controlled trial.* **PLoS Med. 2020;17:e1003430.** DOI: 10.1371/journal.pmed.1003430; PMID 33259482. B12 substantially improved methylmalonic acid and homocysteine profiles but had no meaningful effect on Bayley-III scores, attained growth, or hemoglobin. For example, the length difference was 0.20 cm, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.63, and the cognitive scaled-score difference was 0.16, 95% CI −0.54 to 0.87. ([PubMed][7])
* **Ulak M, et al.** *The effect of infant vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment: a follow-up of a randomised placebo-controlled trial in Nepal.* **Br J Nutr. 2023;129:41–48.** DOI: 10.1017/S0007114522000071; PMID 35152918. Follow-up at approximately 2½–4 years showed no benefit for preschool IQ or other neurodevelopmental outcomes; full-scale IQ differed by −0.5 points, 95% CI −1.97 to 0.94. ([PubMed][8])
* **Strand TA, et al.** *The effect of daily vitamin B-12 supplementation on the incidence of common childhood infections in Nepal: secondary outcomes from a double-blind randomized controlled trial.* **J Nutr. 2026;156:101534.** PMID 41997492. B12 did not reduce clinic visits or any of the prespecified common-infection outcomes. ([PubMed][9])

**Trial-level interpretation:** Strong biochemical evidence that B12 status improved, but no corresponding benefit for growth, anemia, neurodevelopment, later cognition, or infectious morbidity.

## Earlier oral supplementation studies

The following are primary controlled reports that appear potentially eligible. Because many are from the 1940s–1950s and have no indexed abstract, their full texts should be checked before data extraction for sequence generation, exact ages, doses, duration, attrition, and whether all co-interventions were balanced.

### Directly verified placebo-controlled study

* **Larcomb JW, Perry CS, Peterman RA.** *Dietary supplementation of vitamin B12 in pre-puberty school-age children. I. Growth studies.* **J Pediatr. 1954;45:70–74.** DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(54)80064-3; PMID 13175080. A total of 132 prepubertal schoolchildren were randomly divided between B12 and placebo for a school year. The authors reported increased weight gain among underweight children, no growth benefit among children of normal weight, and a possible height effect among overweight children. ([ScienceDirect][10])

### Candidate controlled trials requiring full-text verification

* **Rascoff H, Dunewitz A, Norton R.** *The weight progress of premature infants given supplementary feedings of vitamin B12; a comparative study.* **J Pediatr. 1951;39:61–64.** DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(51)80281-6; PMID 14851186. ([PubMed][11])
* **Chinnock RF, et al.** *Results of administration of vitamin B12 to newborn infants.* **J Pediatr. 1952;40:182–185.** DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(52)80180-5. The indexed report describes B12 administration to newborn infants, with no clear improvement in weight or length gain; verify control allocation and denominators from the article. ([ScienceDirect][12])
* **Wetzel NC, Fargo WC.** *Growth failure in school children as associated with vitamin B12 deficiency; response to oral therapy.* **Science. 1949;110:651–653.** DOI: 10.1126/science.110.2868.651.
* **Wetzel NC, Hopwood HH, Kuechle ME, Grueninger RM.** *Growth failure in school children; further studies of vitamin B12 dietary supplements.* **J Clin Nutr. 1952;1:17–31.** DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/1.1.17; PMID 12990680. ([ScienceDirect][13])
* **Spies TD, Dreizen S, Currie C, Buehl CC.** *The effect of supplements of vitamin B12 on the growth of children with nutritive failure.* **Int Z Vitaminforsch. 1952;23:414–429.** PMID 14937886. ([PubMed][14])
* **Montoye HJ, Spata PJ, Pinckney V, Barron L.** *Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on physical fitness and growth of young boys.* **J Appl Physiol. 1955;7:589–592.** DOI: 10.1152/jappl.1955.7.6.589; PMID 14381333.
* **Crump J, Tully R.** *The use of partial vitamin supplements in the treatment of growth failure in children.* **J Pediatr. 1955;46:671–681.** DOI: 10.1016/S0022-3476(55)80172-2; PMID 14368478.
* **Mackay IF, Patrick SJ, Stafford D, Cleveland FS Jr.** *The influence of vitamin B12 and aureomycin upon the growth of protein-deficient children.* **J Nutr. 1956;59:155–170.** DOI: 10.1093/jn/59.1.155; PMID 13320203. The report did not find a positive B12 effect on weight or height; confirm the factorial arms and balanced antibiotic comparisons before including effect estimates. ([PubMed][15])
* **Scrimshaw NS, Guzman MA.** *Effect of dietary supplements and the administration of vitamin B12 and aureomycin on the growth of school children.* **Bol Oficina Sanit Panam. 1953;34:551–562.** PMID 13066565.
* **Aguirre F, Scrimshaw NS.** *The effect of supplements of animal and vegetable protein, vitamin B12, and aureomycin on hematological values in Central American school children.* **Am J Clin Nutr. 1955;3:225–229.** DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/3.3.225; PMID 14376358. This may be a companion report from the same Central American supplementation program rather than a separate randomized cohort.

## Parenteral studies, should route not be restricted to oral supplementation

* **Worthington-White DA, Behnke M, Gross S.** *Premature infants require additional folate and vitamin B-12 to reduce the severity of the anemia of prematurity.* **Am J Clin Nutr. 1994;60:930–935.** DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/60.6.930; PMID 7985636. In 184 premature infants randomized to folate, monthly intramuscular B12, both, or neither, B12-containing groups had higher hemoglobin by 10–12 weeks. ([PubMed][16])
* **Bjørke-Monsen AL, et al.** *Common metabolic profile in infants indicating impaired cobalamin status responds to cobalamin supplementation.* **Pediatrics. 2008.** DOI: 10.1542/peds.2007-2716; PMID 18595990. Among 107 six-week-old infants, one 400-μg intramuscular B12 injection improved serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine versus no intervention; clinical health outcomes were not demonstrated. ([PubMed][17])

## Overall pattern from the primary studies

The most reproducible benefit is **improvement in B12-related biochemical status**, particularly serum B12, methylmalonic acid, and homocysteine. Evidence for patient-important outcomes is substantially weaker:

* Growth: a small signal in the Indian trial and some historical undernourished subgroups, but no benefit in the larger Nepal trial.
* Neurodevelopment/cognition: an early motor-development signal in India, but no sustained cognitive benefit in either the Indian or Nepalese follow-ups.
* Hemoglobin/anemia: no benefit in the modern oral trials; a benefit was reported in premature infants receiving parenteral B12.
* Infections: no reduction in either modern trial.
* Safety: no clear B12-associated safety signal in the large Nepal trial, although historical safety reporting was limited.

Multivitamin or fortified-food trials in which B12 was accompanied by several other ingredients and the control lacked all of those ingredients should **not** be treated as B12-specific comparisons.
