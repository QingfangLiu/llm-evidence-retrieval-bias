Here are individual primary studies (RCTs) evaluating oral or parenteral vitamin B12 supplementation in children under 12, versus placebo, no supplementation, or the same supplement minus B12. I've excluded reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines as requested.

## Growth, neurodevelopment, and hematologic outcomes

**Strand et al. 2020 (PLOS Medicine)** — Nepal, 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months. Children were randomized 1:1 to 2 μg of vitamin B12 daily or placebo for 12 months. Vitamin B12 supplementation resulted in an improved metabolic response but did not affect neurodevelopment, growth, or hemoglobin concentration. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02272842. (PMID/PMC: PMC7707571)

**Kvestad et al. 2015 (PLOS One)** — North India, 422 children aged 6–30 months, part of a 1000-child factorial trial. Children received placebo or vitamin B12 and/or folic acid for six months in a 1:1:1:1 factorial design. Compared to placebo, children receiving both B12 and folic acid had higher gross motor and problem-solving scores; B12 alone significantly improved gross motor scores, but folic acid alone had no effect. Registered NCT00717730.

**Strand et al. 2015 (Pediatrics)** — Same North Indian cohort; the growth outcome paper from this factorial trial (cited as reference 41 in the above studies): "Vitamin B-12, folic Acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children: a randomized controlled trial."

**Kumar et al. 2017** — Same trial cohort; hemoglobin outcomes (cited within the Kvestad 2015 paper as a companion outcome paper).

**Manger/Strand follow-up studies of the North India cohort:**
- A 6-year follow-up (791 of the original 1000 children) found a small, non-significant effect of vitamin B12 on linear growth overall, but a significant subgroup effect (0.34 HAZ, 95% CI 0.11–0.58) in children with baseline plasma cobalamin <200 pmol/L.
- A separate 6–7 year follow-up of the same trial examined cardiometabolic risk markers (homocysteine, leptin, adiponectin) after supplementation of B12 with or without folic acid in early childhood.

**Torsvik et al. 2013 (Am J Clin Nutr)** — Norway. "Cobalamin supplementation improves motor development and regurgitations in infants: results from a randomized intervention study."

**Torsvik et al. 2015 (BMC Pediatrics)** — Norway, infants with birth weight 2000–3000g. "Motor development related to duration of exclusive breastfeeding, B vitamin status and B12 supplementation in infants... results from a randomized intervention trial."

**Kvestad et al. (British Journal of Nutrition)** — "The effect of infant vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment: a follow-up of a randomised placebo-controlled trial in Nepal" (follow-up of the Strand 2020 Nepal trial).

**Strand et al. 2026 (Journal of Nutrition)** — Nepal, same cohort. "The Effect of Daily Vitamin B-12 Supplementation on the Incidence of Common Childhood Infections in Nepal: Secondary Outcomes from a Double-Blind Randomized Controlled Trial."

## Other relevant primary trials

**Vitamin B Supplementation in Nigerian Children with Nephrotic Syndrome (2024/2025)** — Ibadan, Nigeria, 48 children. A single-blind RCT where children with nephrotic syndrome and elevated homocysteine were randomized to daily folic acid, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12, or placebo, for six months, with homocysteine reduction as the primary outcome. The intervention group showed a significant reduction in homocysteine from 12.8 to 6.9 μmol/L (p<0.001) versus minimal change in controls.

**Siekmann et al. 2003 (J Nutrition, ScienceDirect)** — Kenya, school children, cluster-randomized by school. Schools were randomly assigned to receive meat, milk, an energy-only supplement, or no supplemental food (control), given as a mid-morning snack 5 days/week during school terms. Increased plasma vitamin B12 was the only detectable micronutrient response to meat or milk supplementation among multiple deficiencies assessed.
