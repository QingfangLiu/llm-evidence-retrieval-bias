## Directly eligible primary studies

The most clinically informative evidence is concentrated in **two large oral randomized trials**. Each generated several primary publications, so the papers below should not be counted as independent participant samples.

### 1. North India factorial trial — NCT00717730

**Population/intervention:** 1,000 children approximately 6–30 months old were randomized for six months to vitamin B12 1.8 µg/day, folic acid approximately 150 µg/day, both, or placebo. The factorial design permits both B12-versus-placebo and B12-plus-folate-versus-folate comparisons.

## Additional primary trials using injected B12

These answer a somewhat different question—**treatment of biochemical deficiency**, rather than routine oral nutritional supplementation.

* **Bjørke-Monsen et al., 2008, *Pediatrics*. “Effect of cobalamin supplementation in infants: a randomized, controlled trial.”** In 107 healthy six-week-old infants, one 400-µg intramuscular dose versus no intervention increased circulating B12 and substantially lowered methylmalonic acid and homocysteine by four months. Clinical developmental outcomes were not reported.

* **Torsvik et al., 2015, *BMC Pediatrics*. “Motor development…in infants with a birth weight between 2000–3000 g…”** Thirty-two six-month-old infants with elevated homocysteine were randomized to 400 µg intramuscular hydroxycobalamin or sham injection. After one month, B12 lowered homocysteine and MMA and produced greater improvements in Alberta Infant Motor Scale and ASQ gross-motor scores. The sample was very small and selectively enrolled infants with biochemical evidence of impaired B12 function. ([Springer][12])

## Clinical interpretation

Across the two large oral trials:

* **Biochemical nutrition:** B12 supplementation reliably improved B12-related biomarkers.
* **Patient-important outcomes:** It generally did **not** improve hemoglobin/anemia, infections, sleep, broad neurodevelopment, later cognition, or overall linear growth.
* **Signals of possible benefit:** The North India trial found a small short-term weight effect, an isolated gross-motor improvement, and possible growth benefit in children who were already malnourished or had low baseline cobalamin. These were not consistently reproduced in the larger Nepal trial.
* **Applicability:** Most participants were infants or toddlers in South Asian populations with prevalent undernutrition or suboptimal B12 status. Evidence is sparse for routinely supplemented, otherwise healthy school-aged children in high-income settings and should not be conflated with treatment of confirmed symptomatic deficiency.
