Primary placebo-controlled trials evaluating the clinical impact of vitamin B12 supplementation in infants and young children reveal a persistent disconnect: while supplementation reliably corrects biochemical markers of B12 status, its tangible effects on physical growth and neurodevelopment are mixed and highly dependent on the population.

Here is a synthesis of the recent, rigorous primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) testing oral vitamin B12 supplementation against a placebo.

## Neurodevelopment and Cognitive Function

The evidence regarding early childhood cognitive and motor development shows conflicting results across different geographical cohorts:

* **Targeted improvements in India:** A factorial RCT of children aged 6 to 30 months in North India tested 6 months of daily vitamin B12 (1.8 µg) and/or folic acid against a placebo. Vitamin B12 alone yielded a significant improvement in gross motor scores. When combined with folic acid, children showed significant gains in both gross motor functioning and problem-solving skills. Notably, these benefits were most pronounced in children who were stunted or had poor baseline status (Kvestad et al., 2015).
* **Null neurodevelopmental effects in Nepal:** Conversely, an RCT involving 600 marginally stunted Nepalese infants (6–11 months) provided daily 2 µg doses of vitamin B12 for a full year. The intervention substantially lowered total homocysteine and methylmalonic acid—proving the supplement was biochemically effective—but produced no measurable improvements in neurodevelopmental scores on the Bayley-III scales (Strand et al., 2020).
* **Lack of long-term cognitive impact:** Following up on the Nepalese infant cohort when the children reached 30 to 47 months of age, researchers assessed whether the initial supplementation offered delayed benefits. They found no significant differences in preschool cognitive function, full-scale intelligence quotients (WPPSI-IV), or other neurodevelopmental outcomes compared to the placebo group (Ulak et al., 2022).

## Physical Growth and Anemia

Beyond neurodevelopment, the primary driver for supplementation in marginalized populations is often to correct stunting and anemia. However, standard supplemental doses have not demonstrated superiority over placebo for these outcomes:

* The 12-month Nepalese RCT tracked physical growth and hemoglobin concentrations. The children in both the vitamin B12 and placebo groups grew at the same rate, and there was no significant difference in hemoglobin levels or the alleviation of anemia by the end of the trial (Strand et al., 2020).

## Long-Term Cardiometabolic Health

While short-term developmental and growth metrics may show minimal response, there is emerging evidence of long-term metabolic benefits:

* A 6- to 7-year follow-up of the original North Indian cohort revealed that children who received combined vitamin B12 and folic acid in early childhood maintained persistently lower concentrations of total homocysteine (tHcy). Furthermore, vitamin B12 supplementation was associated with a favorable leptin-adiponectin ratio later in childhood, suggesting a lasting reduction in cardiometabolic risk markers (Manapurath et al., 2023).
