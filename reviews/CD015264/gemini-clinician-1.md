Based on primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating children under 12, routine vitamin B12 supplementation does not universally improve neurodevelopment or growth compared to a placebo. The evidence suggests that benefits are largely restricted to children with a documented baseline B12 deficiency, or when B12 is co-administered with other synergistic micronutrients like folic acid.

## Neurodevelopment and Cognitive Function

The evidence regarding early childhood neurodevelopment is mixed and suggests that isolated B12 supplementation is often insufficient to drive broad cognitive changes without accompanying folate.

A factorial RCT conducted in North India with children aged 6 to 30 months found that supplementing B12 alone resulted in a modest improvement in gross motor scores. However, the most significant improvements across multiple domains—specifically gross motor functioning and problem-solving skills—were only observed when children received a combination of both vitamin B12 and folic acid (Kvestad et al., 2015).

Conversely, a robust 12-month RCT in Nepal involving 6- to 11-month-old infants found that a daily dose of 2 µg of vitamin B12 alone significantly improved blood biomarkers (lowering homocysteine and methylmalonic acid), but it yielded **zero** measurable effect on neurodevelopment as measured by the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (Strand et al., 2020). When this exact same cohort was followed up at preschool age (30 to 47 months), researchers again found no difference in cognitive function or IQ scores between the B12 group and the placebo group (Ulak et al., 2022).

## Physical Growth and Anemia

Similar to cognitive outcomes, general physical growth and hemoglobin concentrations do not appear to benefit from prophylactic B12 supplementation unless a specific deficiency is being corrected.

In the short term, Strand et al. (2020) reported no differences in physical growth trajectories or hemoglobin concentrations between infants receiving B12 and those receiving a placebo over a 12-month period.

However, looking at long-term linear growth, Taneja et al. (2022) conducted a 6-year follow-up of the North Indian cohort originally studied by Kvestad. While they found no significant overall effect of B12 on long-term linear growth across the general study population, they did identify a significant subgroup effect. Children who were demonstrably B12-deficient at baseline (plasma cobalamin < 200 pmol/L) experienced significantly improved height-for-age z-scores compared to the placebo group.

## Clinical Takeaway

For a general pediatric population under 12, there is no strong primary evidence supporting widespread, prophylactic vitamin B12 supplementation to improve growth, hemoglobin, or neurodevelopment. If you suspect a child is failing to meet developmental or growth milestones due to nutritional deficits, confirming a baseline B12 deficiency is recommended, as the clinical benefits of supplementation are largely confined to this subgroup. Furthermore, if supplementing, consider evaluating and addressing folate status simultaneously, as the two operate synergistically in neurodevelopmental pathways.
