Here are primary (non-review) studies I located that test oral vitamin B12 supplementation against placebo, no supplementation, or an identical supplement without B12, in children under 12. All are individual RCTs (no reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines included).

## Nepal — infants (6–11 months at enrollment)

**Strand TA, et al. "Effects of vitamin B12 supplementation on neurodevelopment and growth in Nepalese infants: A randomized controlled trial." PLoS Med. 2020;17(12):e1003430.**
This was a community-based, individually randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in Bhaktapur, Nepal, enrolling 600 marginally stunted infants aged 6–11 months who received 2 μg vitamin B12 or placebo daily for 12 months, with both groups also receiving 15 other vitamins/minerals at ~1 RDA; primary outcomes were Bayley-III neurodevelopment, growth, and hemoglobin. No effects were found on Bayley-III scores, growth, or hemoglobin concentration, despite clear improvement in B12 metabolic status (lower homocysteine and methylmalonic acid).
Link: https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1003430
(Trial protocol/design papers: Strand et al., *Trials* 2017;18:187, PMC5399862; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02272842.)

## North India — Delhi factorial RCT (children 6–30 months at enrollment), with a 6-year follow-up

This is a single large factorial trial with several outcome papers:

- **Strand TA, et al. "Vitamin B-12, folic acid, and growth in 6- to 30-month-old children: A randomized controlled trial." Pediatrics. 2015;135(4):e918–26.** — growth outcomes.
- **Taneja S, et al. "Folic acid and vitamin B-12 supplementation and common infections in 6-30-mo-old children in India: A randomized placebo-controlled trial." Am J Clin Nutr. 2013;98(3):731–7.** — infectious morbidity.
- **Kvestad I, et al. "Vitamin B12 and folic acid improve gross motor and problem-solving skills in young North Indian children: A randomized placebo-controlled trial." PLoS One. 2015;10(6):e0129915.** In this randomized, double-blind trial, children aged 6–30 months received supplement with placebo or vitamin B12 and/or folic acid for six months in a 1:1:1:1 factorial design; development was measured in 422 children with the Ages and Stages Questionnaire. Children given both vitamin B12 and folic acid scored 0.45 SD higher on gross motor and 0.28 SD higher on problem-solving domains than placebo, with the largest effects in stunted children, those with high homocysteine, or younger children.
Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4476750/
- **Kumar T, et al. "Supplementation of vitamin B12 or folic acid on hemoglobin concentration in children 6–36 months of age: A randomized placebo controlled trial." Clin Nutr. 2017;36(4):986–91.** In the same New Delhi trial, children were randomized to 2 RDAs of vitamin B12, folic acid, both, or placebo daily for 6 months, with hemoglobin and iron status assessed as predefined secondary outcomes in 262 children.
Link: https://kemdiabetes.org/Paper/249%20Supplementation%20of%20vitamin%20B12%20or%20folic%20acid%20on%20hemoglobin%20concentration%20in%20children%206-36%20months%20of%20age.pdf
- **Taneja S, Chowdhury R, Kvestad I, Bhandari N, Strand TA. "Vitamin B12 and/or folic acid supplementation on linear growth: A 6-year follow-up study of a randomised controlled trial in early childhood in North India." Br J Nutr. 2023.** This follow-up measured height in 791 of the original 1000 children six years after the 6-month supplementation ended (mean age 7.4 years at follow-up) and found a small, non-significant overall effect of vitamin B12 on linear growth, but a significant benefit (0.34 HAZ) in the subgroup with baseline plasma cobalamin <200 pmol/L.
Link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10011595/

## Norway — low-birth-weight infants

**Torsvik IK, Ueland PM, Markestad T, Midttun Ø, Monsen ALB. "Motor development related to duration of exclusive breastfeeding, B vitamin status and B12 supplementation in infants with a birth weight between 2000-3000 g, results from a randomized intervention trial." BMC Pediatr. 2015;15:218.** — RCT of oral B12 vs. placebo in infants with birth weight 2000–3000 g, motor development as outcome (PMID 26678525).

## Nigeria — children with nephrotic syndrome (a chronic-illness population, not healthy children)

**"Vitamin B Supplementation and Homocysteine Reduction in Nigerian Children with Nephrotic Syndrome: A Randomized Controlled Trial."** This single-blind RCT at University College Hospital, Ibadan randomized 48 children with nephrotic syndrome and elevated homocysteine to daily folic acid, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12 versus placebo for six months, finding a significant reduction in homocysteine in the intervention group versus minimal change in placebo.
Link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12129721/
(Note: B12 is combined with other B vitamins here, so the isolated effect of B12 can't be separated, and the population has a specific renal illness rather than being generally healthy.)

## A note on scope and what I excluded

- I deliberately left out the 2026 Cochrane review "Vitamin B12 supplementation for growth, development, and cognition in children" (Larvie et al.), since it's a systematic review — but it's a useful map: it identified 16 trials (4083 children, ages 2 days to 11 years) across 10 countries including India, Japan, Thailand, Nepal, UK, Italy, Spain, the US, El Salvador, and Guatemala, so there are likely several additional eligible primary trials (particularly older European/Latin American ones treating diagnosed infantile B12 deficiency) that I haven't yet pulled individual citations for.
- The Louwman et al. 2000 AJCN study on marginal cobalamin status and cognition in adolescents is observational/cross-sectional, not a supplementation RCT, and the population (adolescents) may exceed your <12 cutoff — I excluded it.
