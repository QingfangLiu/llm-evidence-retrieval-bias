Here are individual primary studies (RCTs) — not reviews or guidelines — evaluating oral vitamin B12 supplementation (alone or combined with other micronutrients) versus placebo, no supplementation, or matched supplement without B12, in children under 12.

**1. Strand et al., 2015, *Pediatrics*** — North India
Factorial RCT, 1,000 children aged 6–35 months, randomized 1:1:1:1 to vitamin B12, folic acid, both, or placebo daily for 6 months. The overall effect of either vitamin was significant only for weight; children who received vitamin B-12 increased their mean weight-for-age z scores by 0.07 (95% CI: 0.01 to 0.13). Weight-for-age and height-for-age z-scores increased significantly after B12 supplementation specifically in wasted, underweight, and stunted subgroups.

**2. Kvestad et al., 2015, *PLOS ONE*** — North India
Same cohort/design as above; developmental outcomes measured with Ages and Stages Questionnaire-3. Children who received both vitamin B12 and folic acid had higher scores in gross motor (0.45 SD, 95% CI 0.19–0.73) and problem-solving (0.28 SD, 95% CI 0.02–0.54) domains; vitamin B12 alone significantly improved gross motor scores but not other outcomes.

**3. Taneja et al., 2013, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*** — North India
Same trial cohort, focused on infection/morbidity outcomes of B12/folic acid supplementation over 6 months (referenced in citations above; primary RCT report, not itself a review).

**4. Strand et al., 2018 follow-up, *British Journal of Nutrition*** — North India
Six-year post-intervention follow-up of the same RCT cohort (791 of the original 1,000 children), examining long-term linear growth effects. Children aged 6–30 months were randomized to placebo, vitamin B12, folic acid, or both, daily for 6 months, and height was remeasured 6 years later at mean age 7.4 years.

**5. Kvestad et al./follow-up, cardiometabolic markers, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* (~2023)**
Further 6–7 year follow-up of the same original cohort assessing cardiometabolic risk markers. Children were originally randomized 1:1:1:1 to vitamin B12 and folic acid, vitamin B12 alone, folic acid alone, or placebo daily for 6 months via a lipid-based nutritional supplement.

**6. Strand/Chandyo et al. (Nepal), *PLOS Medicine*, 2020** — Bhaktapur, Nepal
Individually randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 600 marginally stunted 6–11 month-old infants, randomized to 2 μg vitamin B12 (with 15 other vitamins/minerals given to both arms) or placebo for 12 months. B12 supplementation improved metabolic status (lower homocysteine and methylmalonic acid) but did not significantly affect Bayley-III neurodevelopment scores, growth, or hemoglobin concentration (mean growth difference 0.20 cm, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.63; hemoglobin difference 0.02 g/dL, 95% CI −1.33 to 1.37).

**7. Study protocol paper for the Nepal trial, *Trials* (BMC), 2017**
Describes the same individually randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design: 600 marginally stunted children 6–11 months old randomized to a lipid-based paste containing vitamin B12 or placebo daily for 12 months, with growth and Bayley-III neurodevelopment as main outcomes (protocol only; results reported in item 6 above).

**8. Hemoglobin-focused sub-analysis, *Clinical Nutrition* (ScienceDirect), 2016** — North India
In the same North Indian cohort, 262 children with baseline and end-of-study blood samples were analyzed for hemoglobin response to 6 months of vitamin B12 and/or folic acid versus placebo; mean hemoglobin decreased in all four groups and supplementation did not improve hemoglobin concentration, with iron given to anemic children showing limited additional effect.

**9. Nigerian nephrotic syndrome trial, *(journal via PMC)*, ~2024–2025**
Single-blind RCT at University College Hospital, Ibadan, Nigeria: 48 children with nephrotic syndrome and elevated homocysteine were randomized to daily folic acid, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12, or placebo, for six months, with homocysteine reduction as the primary outcome. The intervention group's homocysteine fell from 12.8±1.4 to 6.9±2.1 μmol/L (p<0.001), versus minimal change in controls. Note: this population has an underlying kidney condition, not healthy children.

**10. Parenteral vs. oral B12 in nutritional macrocytic anemia, *Indian Pediatrics*, 2022**
RCT of 80 children aged 2 months–18 years with nutritional macrocytic anemia; all received an initial parenteral B12 dose, then were randomized to continued parenteral vs. oral B12 for three months, with serum B12 and hemoglobin as outcomes; hemoglobin and B12 improvement were greater with parenteral than oral dosing. Note: this compares two active B12 routes/doses, not B12 vs. no-B12, and includes some participants over age 12.

**11. Erythropoietin + B12/folate in anemia of prematurity, *(PubMed)*, ~2006**
RCT of 64 premature infants (birth weight 801–1300g) already receiving erythropoietin and iron, randomized to added vitamin B12 (3 μg/kg/day) plus higher-dose folate, versus lower-dose folate alone; the B12+folate group showed a significant 10% increase in red blood cell count versus folate alone, with more stable hemoglobin/hematocrit. Note: comparator arm also received folate, so this isolates B12's added effect in a specific clinical (preterm) context rather than a clean placebo comparison.

A few notes for interpreting these:
- Most of the strongest, purpose-built trials on **healthy or stunted community children** come from two research programs — the North Indian (Delhi) cohort (Strand, Kvestad, Taneja) and the Nepal (Bhaktapur) cohort — so several "different" papers above are follow-ups or secondary-outcome analyses of the same two underlying RCTs, not fully independent samples.
- Findings across these are mixed: modest growth/motor-development benefits were seen in the Indian cohort (especially in growth-faltered subgroups), while the Nepal trial found no effect on growth, cognition, or hemoglobin despite biochemical improvement.
- The Nigerian, macrocytic-anemia, and prematurity trials involve children with specific medical conditions, so their results may not generalize to a healthy child's routine nutrition.

Since this touches your own child's health, I'd also gently suggest running any supplementation decision by their pediatrician, especially since B12 needs and deficiency risk vary a lot with diet (e.g., vegetarian/vegan households) and individual health status.
