## Bottom line

Remote pharmacist care **can work better than usual outpatient care**, but the benefit depends heavily on how it is delivered. The clearest evidence is in **uncontrolled hypertension**, particularly when pharmacists receive home blood-pressure readings, contact patients repeatedly, and can adjust or recommend medication changes. Diabetes results are more mixed. Brief, one-off telephone calls or education-only telepharmacy generally have not improved clinical outcomes. Evidence that telepharmacy prevents hospitalization, stroke, myocardial infarction, or death remains limited.

## Longer-term follow-up

The HyperLink hypertension trial produced two additional primary reports from the same original cohort:

* By approximately **54 months**, the BP advantage was no longer sustained on research-clinic measurements, although routine clinical readings suggested lower BP for up to about 24 months. This indicates that ongoing or maintenance contact may be necessary. ([PMC][13])
* At five years, cardiovascular events occurred in 4.4% of the telemonitoring group and 8.6% of usual care, but the difference was **not statistically conclusive**: OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.21–1.13. Economic modeling suggested possible savings, but clinical event reduction was an exploratory follow-up finding. ([PubMed][14])

## Overall interpretation

The primary studies suggest that telepharmacy is most likely to outperform usual care when it includes:

* repeated pharmacist contact rather than a single call;
* objective home measurements such as BP or glucose;
* authority or an efficient process for changing medication;
* integration with the patient’s primary-care record and prescriber.

The evidence is **strongest and most consistent for blood-pressure control**. For diabetes, intensive pharmacist-led video or hybrid programs can improve HbA1c, but telephone counseling alone has produced inconsistent results. For CKD, anticoagulation, and mixed home-health populations, remote pharmacist care has improved some care processes or adherence measures but has not reliably improved major clinical outcomes.
