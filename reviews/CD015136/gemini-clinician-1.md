Managing long-term health conditions for patients outside of the hospital is incredibly challenging, especially when ensuring they stay on track with complex medication regimens. Telepharmacy—which includes pharmacist-led telephone follow-ups, video counseling, and remote medication monitoring—has been tested extensively as a way to bridge this gap.

Based on primary clinical trials (excluding systematic reviews and meta-analyses, as requested), the evidence generally shows that remote pharmacist care performs better than usual care for specific chronic conditions like diabetes and hypertension, though the benefits are more modest when a patient's baseline care is already highly optimized.

Here is a breakdown of key primary studies evaluating telepharmacy against usual care in outpatient settings.

## Type 2 Diabetes Management

For outpatient diabetes care, regular remote check-ins by a pharmacist can significantly improve glycemic control compared to standard care.

In a randomized controlled trial focusing on an outpatient diabetes clinic, researchers compared usual care against a telepharmacy intervention consisting of baseline education followed by eight weekly pharmacist-led telephone follow-ups (Jarab et al., 2012).

* **Outcomes:** At the 6-month mark, the telepharmacy group saw a mean reduction in HbA1c of 0.8%, while the usual care group experienced a slight increase of 0.1%.
* **Additional benefits:** The remote intervention group also demonstrated statistically significant improvements in self-reported medication adherence, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and lipid profiles compared to the control.

## Hypertension and Blood Pressure Control

Telepharmacy appears particularly effective for hypertension when coupled with home blood pressure (BP) telemonitoring.

A large cluster-randomized trial evaluated patients with uncontrolled hypertension across 16 primary care clinics. Patients were assigned to either usual care or an intervention combining home BP telemonitoring with regular remote medication management by a pharmacist (Asche et al., 2016).

* **Outcomes:** At the 6-month follow-up, 72% of the patients receiving the remote pharmacist intervention achieved their target BP control, compared to only 45% in the usual care group.
* **Clinical impact:** The telepharmacy group achieved greater reductions in both systolic (11 mmHg lower) and diastolic (5 mmHg lower) blood pressure than those receiving standard care.

Similarly, a prospective cohort pilot study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic evaluated a pharmacist-led telemedicine medication management service for outpatients with hypertension (Li et al., 2022).

* **Outcomes:** At 6 months, 89.6% of the telepharmacy patients successfully controlled their BP, compared to 78.8% in the usual care group. The telepharmacy patients also demonstrated significantly better medication adherence (only 17.9% non-adherent vs. 29.1% in usual care).

## Broad Cardiovascular Risk Management

While telepharmacy clearly works well for poorly controlled, specific conditions, its impact is less dramatic when applied broadly to patients whose chronic diseases are already well-managed.

A cluster-randomized trial tested a centralized, remote clinical pharmacy service across 12 private family medicine offices to manage broad cardiovascular risks (Carter et al., 2018). The remote pharmacists had access to medical records and worked collaboratively with physicians to optimize medications.

* **Outcomes:** While the telepharmacy group saw significant improvements in specific process measures like appropriate statin therapy and BMI screening, the overall composite "Guideline Advantage" score only showed modest, non-significant improvements over usual care.
* **Takeaway:** The researchers noted that the modest difference was largely because the baseline guideline adherence in these clinics was already much higher than expected. Telepharmacy yields the highest return on investment when targeted at high-risk patients with actively uncontrolled conditions.
