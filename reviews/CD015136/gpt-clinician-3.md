## Scope

I treated “outside a hospital” as ambulatory, primary-care, community or home-based care. I included comparative **primary research** in which a pharmacist delivered a substantial part of the intervention remotely—telephone, video, secure messaging, an app or home-monitoring data. Searches were current through **July 18, 2026**.

## Relevant comparative studies with important limitations

* **Odegard et al., 2005 — poorly controlled diabetes:** RCT of 77 patients using an initial pharmacist meeting followed by weekly visits or telephone calls. There was **no significant between-group HbA1c or adherence benefit**, although comparable control was achieved with fewer physician visits. It is a hybrid rather than fully remote intervention. ([PubMed][9])

* **Bandiera et al., 2024 — OpTAT trial:** RCT of 118 people receiving oral protein-kinase inhibitors. Monthly pharmacist electronic feedback produced a small improvement in medication implementation—**98.1% versus 95.0%**—but no clear difference in persistence, overall adherence probability, treatment beliefs or quality of life. Some pharmacist contacts were face-to-face, so this is also hybrid care. ([PMC][10])

* **Li et al., 2022 — telemedicine medication management for hypertension:** Prospective nonrandomized cohort of 352 patients using WeChat, telephone contacts and pharmacist review of home BP. BP control was **89.6% versus 78.8%**, with lower reported nonadherence, but patients selected telemedicine according to willingness or preference; therefore, selection bias could explain part of the difference. ([Frontiers][11])

* **Fox et al., 2025 — telehealth comanagement for heart failure:** Nonrandomized controlled study of Medicare patients with HFrEF. Pharmacist telehealth was associated with more guideline-directed medication classes and fewer cardiac hospitalizations. The results are promising but cannot establish causality because treatment was not randomized. ([JACC][12])

## Clinical interpretation

**Hypertension has the most convincing evidence of improved clinical control.** Two reasonably large randomized trials found substantial improvements when pharmacists could review transmitted home BP readings and actively adjust treatment—not merely provide education. ([PubMed][1])

**Medication adherence improves more consistently than biomarkers or hard outcomes.** STIC2IT and MASE demonstrated adherence gains, but those gains did not reliably translate into improved BP, HbA1c, lipid levels or hospitalization. ([JAMA Network][3])

**Diabetes findings are mixed.** A recent multicomponent video-telehealth trial improved HbA1c, while smaller telephone-pharmacy trials found better adherence or self-care without a significant HbA1c advantage. ([PMC][6])

Overall, the primary studies suggest that remote pharmacy care is most likely to outperform usual care when pharmacists have **timely patient-generated data and authority or a clear pathway to intensify medication treatment**. Telephone education or adherence counseling alone generally produces smaller effects, often limited to adherence or process measures. Hard outcomes, adverse effects, cost-effectiveness and durability beyond one to two years remain inadequately established.
