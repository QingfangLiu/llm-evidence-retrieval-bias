## Eligibility used

I treated **telepharmacy** as pharmacist-led clinical or medication-management care delivered remotely by telephone, SMS, app, secure web/video, or transmitted home-monitoring data. Studies had to involve people receiving care outside hospital and compare the remote intervention with usual or standard care.

I excluded reviews, meta-analyses, protocols, guidelines, opinions, editorials, and uncontrolled single-group studies. The set below is a targeted, high-confidence group of primary studies rather than a reproducible database-export systematic search.

## Closely matching primary studies

### Hypertension and cardiometabolic conditions

1. **Mehos BM, Saseen JJ, MacLaughlin EJ. Effect of pharmacist intervention and initiation of home blood pressure monitoring in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Pharmacotherapy. 2000;20:1384–1389.**
   Small RCT, n=36. Patients used home BP monitoring, with results reviewed by a pharmacist by telephone. BP reductions were larger in the intervention arm; 8 versus 4 patients reached <140/90 mmHg. Medication adherence and quality of life did not differ. PMID 11079287; DOI 10.1592/phco.20.17.1384.34891. ([PubMed][1])

2. **Green BB, et al. Effectiveness of home blood pressure monitoring, Web communication, and pharmacist care on hypertension control: a randomized controlled trial—the e-BP trial. JAMA. 2008;299:2857–2867.**
   Three-arm RCT, n=778. BP control at 12 months was 56% with home monitoring plus secure-web pharmacist management, versus 31% with usual care. Home monitoring and web access without pharmacist management achieved 36%, which was not significantly better than usual care. PMID 18577730; DOI 10.1001/jama.299.24.2857. ([PubMed][2])

3. **Margolis KL, et al. Effect of home blood pressure telemonitoring and pharmacist management on blood pressure control: the HyperLink cluster randomized clinical trial. JAMA. 2013;310:46–56.**
   Cluster RCT, n=450. BP was controlled at both 6 and 12 months in 57.2% of intervention patients versus 30.0% with usual care. The intervention produced approximately 10 mmHg greater systolic reduction at 6 and 12 months. Benefits persisted to 18 months, but the research-clinic difference was no longer significant at 54 months after the intervention ended. PMID 23821088; DOI 10.1001/jama.2013.6549. ([PubMed][3])

4. **Bosworth HB, et al. Telemedicine cardiovascular risk reduction in veterans: the CITIES trial. American Heart Journal. 2018;199:122–129.**
   RCT, n=428, comparing clinical-pharmacist telehealth with an education control among veterans with poorly controlled hypertension and/or hypercholesterolemia. There was no significant benefit for Framingham cardiovascular risk, BP, LDL cholesterol, or—among patients with diabetes—HbA1c. Total cholesterol was modestly lower at six months only. PMID 29754649; DOI 10.1016/j.ahj.2018.02.002. ([PubMed][4])

5. **Choudhry NK, et al. Effect of a remotely delivered tailored multicomponent approach to enhance medication taking for patients with hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes: the STIC2IT cluster randomized clinical trial. JAMA Internal Medicine. 2018;178:1182–1189.**
   Pragmatic cluster RCT, n=4,078. Pharmacists used telephone behavioral interviewing, texts, pillboxes, and mailed reports. Adherence improved by 4.7 percentage points, and emergency-department attendance was lower, but control of LDL cholesterol, BP, or HbA1c did not significantly improve. PMID 30083727; DOI 10.1001/jamainternmed.2018.3189. ([PubMed][5])

6. **Ibrahim OM, et al. The impact of telepharmacy on hypertension management in the United Arab Emirates. Pharmacy Practice. 2022;20:2734.**
   Two-arm randomized trial across 16 community pharmacies, n=239. Telepharmacy improved BP, adherence, patient knowledge, and identification of drug-related problems. Between-group systolic BP differences were significant at 3, 6, and 9 months but narrowly missed significance at 12 months; diastolic differences remained significant through 12 months. PMID 36793920; DOI 10.18549/PharmPract.2022.4.2734. ([PubMed][6])

7. **Mozu IE, et al. Utilization of telepharmacy in the management of hypertension. Journal of Pharmaceutical Policy and Practice. 2023.**
   Six-month RCT, n=118, conducted in Ghana. Telephone-based pharmaceutical care plus standard care reduced mean BP from approximately 148/86 to 135/80 mmHg; the proportion with controlled BP rose from 39.0% to 66.1%. Adherence and therapeutic lifestyle behaviors also improved. PMID 38145235. ([PubMed][7])

8. **Li X, Hu J, et al. Evaluation of pharmacist-led telemedicine medication management for hypertension established patients during the COVID-19 pandemic: a pilot study. Frontiers in Public Health. 2022;10:1091484.**
   Prospective **nonrandomized cohort**, n=352; allocation reflected patient demand and willingness. At six months, BP control was 89.6% with telemedicine versus 78.8% with usual care; nonadherence was 17.9% versus 29.1%. Cardiovascular events did not differ significantly. Confounding by self-selection is an important limitation. DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2022.1091484. ([Frontiers][8])

### Diabetes

9. **Lauffenburger JC, et al. Impact of a novel pharmacist-delivered behavioral intervention for patients with poorly controlled diabetes: the ENGAGE-DM pragmatic randomized trial. PLOS ONE. 2019;14:e0214754.**
   Pragmatic RCT, n=1,400. A clinical-pharmacist telephone intervention did not improve HbA1c or adherence in intention-to-treat analysis: between-group HbA1c difference +0.04 percentage points, 95% CI −0.22 to 0.30. An as-treated analysis favored patients who actually received the intervention, but this is less robust. PMID 30939143; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0214754. ([PubMed][9])

10. **Sarayani A, et al. Efficacy of a telephone-based intervention among patients with type-2 diabetes: a randomized controlled trial in pharmacy practice. International Journal of Clinical Pharmacy. 2018;40:345–353.**
    RCT, n=100. Sixteen pharmacist calls over three months improved medication adherence and self-care more than usual care, with effects maintained at nine months. HbA1c improved in both groups, without a clear significant clinical advantage attributable to the calls. PMID 29435911; DOI 10.1007/s11096-018-0593-0. ([PubMed][10])

11. **Gautier J-F, et al. Impact of personalized text messages from pharmacists on medication adherence in type 2 diabetes in France: a real-world, randomized, comparative study. Patient Education and Counseling. 2021;104:2250–2258.**
    Randomized study recruiting 499 patients through 114 pharmacies. Daily tailored SMS messages for three months improved MMAS-8 adherence while messages were active, but the improvement was not sustained after messages stopped. BMI also improved. PMID 33750593; DOI 10.1016/j.pec.2021.02.022. ([PubMed][11])

12. **Hoda F, et al. Impact of a mHealth intervention in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients: a randomized clinical trial. SN Comprehensive Clinical Medicine. 2023;5:219.**
    Open-label RCT, n=100. The intervention comprised three to five pharmacist messages and one pharmacist call weekly. The authors reported greater HbA1c improvement and favorable adherence, lifestyle, and quality-of-life outcomes. However, the published abstract contains inconsistent follow-up timing—describing a three-month trial but reporting a “six-month” result—and confusing confidence-interval and P-value formatting; numerical extraction should therefore be checked against the full tables. DOI 10.1007/s42399-023-01564-3. ([ResearchGate][12])

13. **Ihekoronye MR, Osemene KP, Oamen TE. Pharmacist-led intervention to improve treatment outcomes in type 2 diabetes: a randomized controlled trial. Journal of Pharmaceutical Health Services Research. 2024;15:rmae005.**
    RCT, n=309, lasting 25 weeks in Nigeria. Weekly pharmacist health-education and follow-up SMS, added to standard care, improved HbA1c, refill adherence, diabetes knowledge, and self-reported adherence. DOI 10.1093/jphsr/rmae005. ([OUP Academic][13])

14. **Hussain N, Haddad AHHI, Abbass S. Assessing the impact of mobile-based interventions provided by pharmacists on glycemic control and diabetes-related distress in adolescent patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus in Pakistan. Pharmacy Practice. 2024;22:2946.**
    Randomized study, n=112 adolescents. Pharmacist-supported mobile-app care was reported to improve HbA1c and diabetes distress and reduce severe hypoglycemic and ketoacidotic episodes compared with routine management. DOI 10.18549/PharmPract.2024.2.2946. ([ResearchGate][14])

15. **Gay CL, et al. Reinforced follow-up for children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes and inadequate glycaemic control: a randomized controlled trial intervention via the local pharmacist and telecare. Diabetes & Metabolism. 2006;32:159–165.**
    RCT, n=100. Patients attended local pharmacies to download glucometer data, which pharmacists faxed to the hospital team. HbA1c did not differ significantly at approximately six months—9.12% versus 9.27%—and intervention uptake was poor. This is a mixed in-person/remote model rather than fully remote pharmacist–patient care. PMID 16735965; DOI 10.1016/S1262-3636(07)70263-X. ([PubMed][15])

### Asthma

16. **Kosse RC, et al. Effect of a mHealth intervention on adherence in adolescents with asthma: a randomized controlled trial. Respiratory Medicine. 2019;149:45–51.**
    Cluster RCT across 66 Dutch pharmacies; 234 adolescents completed follow-up. The ADAPT app improved adherence among adolescents who had low baseline adherence, with a MARS between-group effect of +2.12 points. It did not significantly improve asthma control or quality of life. PMID 30803885; DOI 10.1016/j.rmed.2019.02.009. ([PubMed][16])

17. **Shdaifat MBM, Khasawneh RA, Alefan Q. Clinical and economic impact of telemedicine in the management of pediatric asthma in Jordan: a pharmacist-led intervention. Journal of Asthma. 2022;59:1452–1462.**
    Twelve-week RCT, n=90 children with uncontrolled asthma. Pharmacist-led synchronous telemedicine every four weeks improved Childhood Asthma Control Test scores and reduced parental wage loss and economic burden compared with standard care. Quality of life and satisfaction also favored the intervention. PMID 33941032; DOI 10.1080/02770903.2021.1924774. ([PubMed][17])

## Scope-dependent but relevant primary studies

These fit remote pharmacist care and a usual-care comparator but concern specialist or post-discharge populations.

18. **Gammaitoni AR, Gallagher RM. Palliative pharmaceutical care: a randomized, prospective study of telephone-based prescription and medication counseling services for treating chronic pain. Pain Medicine. 2000;1:317–331.**
    Of 107 randomized pain-clinic patients, 74 met the study’s analysis criteria. Telephone-based pharmacist prescription and medication counseling improved aspects of medication access, service experience, and satisfaction; it provides less persuasive evidence for objective pain outcomes. DOI 10.1046/j.1526-4637.2000.00043.x. ([OUP Academic][18])

19. **Sudas Na Ayutthaya N, Sakunrak I, Dhippayom T. Clinical outcomes of telemonitoring for patients on warfarin after discharge from hospital. International Journal of Telemedicine and Applications. 2018;2018:7503421.**
    Post-discharge RCT, n=50. Three months of pharmacist telephone follow-up increased INR measurements in range—45.6% versus 24.1%—and mean time in therapeutic range—49.8% versus 28.0%. Bleeding, thromboembolic complications, and other adverse events did not differ. PMID 30158972; DOI 10.1155/2018/7503421. ([PubMed][19])

20. **Gonzales HM, et al. Pharmacist-led mobile health intervention and transplant medication safety: a randomized controlled clinical trial. Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology. 2021;16:776–784.**
    Twelve-month RCT, n=136 ambulatory kidney-transplant recipients. An app with pharmacist monitoring, risk-triggered televisits, and home BP/glucose data reduced medication errors by 61%, grade ≥3 adverse-event rates by 45%, and hospitalization rates by 54% relative to usual transplant care. PMID 33931415; DOI 10.2215/CJN.15911020. ([PubMed][20])

21. **Chaemkiri A, et al. Effects of telepharmacy in post-ischemic stroke patients: a randomized controlled trial. Hospital Pharmacy. Published online March 27, 2026.**
    Post-discharge RCT, n=126. Structured pharmacist follow-ups at one week, one month, and two months improved medication knowledge, adherence, and BP target attainment—52.4% versus 30.2%. Systolic and diastolic BP modestly favored telepharmacy, but LDL cholesterol and HbA1c did not differ. The trial was registered retrospectively. DOI 10.1177/00185787261423000. ([PMC][21])

22. **Fox DS, et al. Impact of pharmacist telehealth comanagement for heart failure: a nonrandomized controlled study. JACC: Advances. 2025;4:101906.**
    Controlled nonrandomized study, n=467 people with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Pharmacist telehealth medication reconciliation, initiation/titration, and safety monitoring were associated with fewer cardiac hospitalizations and greater achievement of guideline-directed medication classes. Selection and residual-confounding risks are substantially greater than in the randomized trials. PMID 40570610. ([PubMed][22])

## Overall interpretation from the primary studies

**The clearest clinical benefit is for hypertension**, particularly when pharmacists receive home BP data and have an active role in medication adjustment. The e-BP and HyperLink trials produced large improvements in BP control, while smaller trials from the UAE and Ghana generally point in the same direction. ([PubMed][2])

**Medication adherence frequently improves, but this does not consistently translate into better disease control.** STIC2IT improved adherence without significantly improving BP, LDL cholesterol, or HbA1c; ENGAGE-DM was null for both HbA1c and adherence; pharmacist SMS improved diabetes adherence in France, but the effect disappeared after messaging stopped. ([PubMed][5])

**Diabetes findings are mixed.** Some smaller recent studies report improvements in HbA1c, but the largest pragmatic diabetes trial was null, and several studies either found no clear between-group glycemic advantage or have reporting limitations. ([PubMed][9])

**Specialist programs may improve medication safety or healthcare use**, as seen after kidney transplantation and in the nonrandomized heart-failure study, but these results may not generalize to routine community telepharmacy. ([PubMed][20])

Studies I did **not** include as effectiveness evidence included single-group pre/post telepharmacy studies, service-implementation reports without a usual-care group, protocols, pharmacist care delivered entirely face to face, and comparisons of telephone care with in-person pharmacist care rather than with usual care.
