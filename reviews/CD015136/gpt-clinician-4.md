## Bottom line

**Telepharmacy can outperform usual care, but the benefit depends on the condition and intervention intensity.** The strongest and most consistent results are for **hypertension**, particularly when pharmacists receive home BP readings and can initiate or recommend medication changes. Repeated, tailored pharmacist contact also improves **medication adherence**. Effects on **HbA1c, asthma control, healthcare utilization, and clinical events** are less consistent.

I treated telepharmacy as pharmacist-delivered care by telephone, secure messaging, web platform, telemonitoring, or SMS. Participants had to be ambulatory or community dwelling, although some were recruited through hospital outpatient clinics. The following are **individual primary comparative studies**, not reviews, guidelines, protocols, opinions, or editorials.

## Direct telepharmacy studies

### Hypertension

1. **Mehos, Saseen & MacLaughlin, 2000 — Pharmacotherapy**
   *Effect of pharmacist intervention and initiation of home blood pressure monitoring in patients with uncontrolled hypertension.*
   Randomized trial, **n=36**. Patients received home BP monitoring with pharmacist telephone review and recommendations to the family physician, or usual care. At six months, mean BP fell by **17.0/10.5 mmHg** in the intervention group versus **7.0/3.8 mmHg** in controls; medication regimens were changed more frequently. Adherence and quality of life were not significantly different. **PMID 11079287.** ([PubMed][1])

2. **Green et al., 2008 — JAMA**
   *Effectiveness of home blood pressure monitoring, Web communication, and pharmacist care on hypertension control.*
   Three-arm RCT, **n=778**: usual care; home monitoring plus web access; or monitoring/web access plus pharmacist management. BP was controlled at 12 months in **56%** of the pharmacist group versus **31%** with usual care and **36%** with monitoring/web access alone. This suggests that monitoring technology without active pharmacist management was insufficient. **PMID 18577730.** ([PubMed][2])

3. **Margolis et al., 2013 — JAMA**
   *Effect of home blood pressure telemonitoring and pharmacist management on blood pressure control.*
   Cluster RCT, **n=450**, across 16 primary-care clinics. Pharmacists reviewed transmitted BP data and adjusted antihypertensive therapy. BP was controlled at both six and 12 months in **57.2%** versus **30.0%** with usual care. The adjusted between-group SBP difference was approximately **−10 mmHg** during treatment, with some benefit persisting six months after the intervention ended. **PMID 23821088.** ([PubMed][3])
   A five-year follow-up of the same cohort found fewer cardiovascular events numerically, but the difference was not statistically conclusive; modeled costs favored the intervention. **PMID 32862713.** ([PubMed][4])

4. **Lau et al., 2022 — Journal of Hypertension; TECHNOMED**
   *Telemonitoring and protocolized case management for hypertensive community-dwelling older adults.*
   RCT, **n=120**, mean age about 80. Pharmacist case management plus home BP telemonitoring was compared with enhanced usual care that already included home monitoring. It did **not** significantly improve the primary ambulatory-BP target outcome. Home BP was modestly lower, but SBP below 110 mmHg occurred more often with pharmacist management: **21% versus 5%**. This raises an overtreatment concern in frail or older populations. **PMID 35943099.** ([PubMed][5])

5. **Mozu et al., 2023 — Exploratory Research in Clinical and Social Pharmacy**
   *Utilization of telepharmacy in the management of hypertension.*
   Ghanaian RCT, **n=118**, comparing telephone-based pharmaceutical care plus standard care with standard care alone. The intervention was associated with better SBP control, medication adherence, lifestyle behaviors, and identification/resolution of medication-related problems; the DBP advantage was less clear. **PMID 38145235.** ([PubMed][6])

### Diabetes, dyslipidemia and medication adherence

6. **Lyons et al., 2016 — BMJ Quality & Safety; MASE trial**
   *The Medicines Advice Service Evaluation.*
   RCT, **n=677**, involving people taking established medications for type 2 diabetes and/or lipid regulation. Two tailored pharmacist telephone consultations plus written follow-up were compared with usual mail-order pharmacy care. Self-reported nonadherence was **10.6% versus 19.6%**, and dispensing-based adherence also favored telepharmacy. Glycemic and lipid measurements in the laboratory subsample were not significantly different. **PMID 26755665.** ([PubMed][7])

7. **Odegard & Christensen, 2012 — Journal of the American Pharmacists Association; MAP trial**
   *RCT of a medication adherence program for patients with type 2 diabetes.*
   Cluster/pharmacy-level RCT, **n=265**. Computer-generated missed-refill alerts triggered pharmacist telephone adherence support. At 12 months, medication possession ratios were significantly better than with usual care; the odds of achieving an MPR above 80% were **4.77 times higher**, with the largest effect among initially nonadherent patients. **PMID 23229961.** ([PubMed][8])

8. **Sarayani et al., 2018 — International Journal of Clinical Pharmacy**
   *Efficacy of a telephone-based intervention among patients with type-2 diabetes.*
   RCT, **n=100**. The intervention involved 16 pharmacist calls over three months. Both groups had received an initial in-person education session. Adherence and diabetes self-care improved more with telephone follow-up and remained better at nine months, but **HbA1c did not show a clear between-group advantage** because both groups improved substantially. **PMID 29435911.** ([PubMed][9])

9. **Ihekoronye, Osemene & Oamen, 2024 — Journal of Pharmaceutical Health Services Research**
   *Pharmacist-led intervention to improve treatment outcomes in type 2 diabetes.*
   RCT, **n=309**, in Nigeria. Weekly pharmacist-generated SMS education and follow-up plus standard care were compared with standard care alone for 25 weeks. The investigators reported improvements in HbA1c, refill adherence, diabetes knowledge, and self-reported medication adherence; **76%** of intervention participants versus **58%** of controls met the refill-adherence threshold. **DOI 10.1093/jphsr/rmae005.** ([OUP Academic][10])

10. **O’Connor et al., 2014 — Diabetes Care**
    *Randomized trial of telephone outreach to improve medication adherence and metabolic control in adults with diabetes.*
    Pragmatic RCT, **n=2,378**. Participants received one scripted call from either a clinical pharmacist or diabetes educator after a new medication was prescribed, or usual care. This low-intensity intervention did **not** significantly improve initial filling, persistence, HbA1c, BP, or LDL cholesterol. Because delivery was shared between pharmacists and educators, this is not a pure pharmacist-only trial, but it is an informative negative study. **PMID 25315207.** ([PubMed][11])

### Asthma

11. **Young et al., 2012 — Telemedicine and e-Health; PARTE pilot**
    *Patient And phaRmacist Telephonic Encounters in an underserved rural patient population with asthma.*
    RCT, **n=98**, comparing structured pharmacist telephone consultations over three months with standard care. Patient activation was significantly higher, but there was **no significant between-group difference in asthma control**. Asthma control and adherence improved within the intervention group, although within-group changes alone do not establish superiority. **PMID 22656403.** ([PubMed][12])

### Long-term anticoagulation

12. **Sudas Na Ayutthaya, Sakunrak & Dhippayom, 2018 — International Journal of Telemedicine and Applications**
    *Clinical Outcomes of Telemonitoring for Patients on Warfarin after Discharge from Hospital.*
    RCT, **n=50**. Pharmacists conducted telephone medication reviews for three months after discharge. Target-range INR measurements were **45.6% versus 24.1%**, and mean time in therapeutic range was **49.8% versus 28.0%**, favoring telepharmacy. Warfarin complications and adverse events were not significantly different. This is ambulatory follow-up but specifically begins after hospitalization. **PMID 30158972.** ([PubMed][13])

## Hybrid studies worth considering separately

These included substantial in-person pharmacist care as well as remote follow-up, so the effect cannot be attributed specifically to telepharmacy.

* **Odegard et al., 2005:** initial pharmacist care plan followed by weekly visits **or telephone calls**, **n=77**. HbA1c and adherence were not significantly better than usual care, although similar control was achieved with fewer physician visits. **PMID 15701763.** ([PubMed][14])
* **Aguiar et al., 2018:** face-to-face pharmacist consultations plus telephone support, **n=80**. HbA1c decreased by **0.79% versus 0.16%** with usual care; individualized HbA1c targets were achieved by **25.0% versus 5.4%**. **PMID 27452158.** ([PubMed][15])
* **Barbanel, Eldridge & Griffiths, 2003:** initial community-pharmacist asthma self-management session plus weekly telephone follow-up, **n=24**. Asthma symptom scores improved compared with no pharmacist input, but the study was very small and the intervention was hybrid rather than wholly remote. **PMID 14514935.** ([PubMed][16])

## Clinical interpretation

The individual studies suggest that telepharmacy is most effective when it includes:

* **Repeated, tailored contact**, rather than one generic call.
* **Objective monitoring data**, such as transmitted home BP or refill alerts.
* **A mechanism for medication adjustment**, either pharmacist prescribing/management or rapid recommendations to the prescriber.
* **Selection of patients with uncontrolled disease or demonstrated nonadherence**, rather than broad, unselected outreach.

For practice, the best-supported application is home BP telemonitoring combined with active pharmacist medication management. Adherence interventions are also promising, but improved adherence does not necessarily translate into better HbA1c, lipids, symptoms, hospital use, or clinical events during the relatively short follow-up periods studied. In older adults, active medication titration should include explicit hypotension surveillance.
