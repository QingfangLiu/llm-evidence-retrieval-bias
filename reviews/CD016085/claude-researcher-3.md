Based on my research, here's a synthesis of the primary studies on intensive versus standard blood pressure control after reperfusion therapy (thrombolysis and/or endovascular thrombectomy) for acute ischemic stroke.

## Randomized Controlled Trials

**ENCHANTED (BP-lowering arm)** — the largest RCT in the thrombolysis population. Patients eligible for thrombolysis with systolic blood pressure of 150 mm Hg or more were randomized within 6 hours of stroke onset to intensive (target systolic blood pressure 130–140 mm Hg within 1 hour) or guideline-recommended (target systolic blood pressure less than 180 mm Hg) blood pressure lowering treatment over 72 hours after treatment with alteplase. Several secondary analyses of this trial dataset examined specific mechanisms, including effects on cerebral swelling, cerebral ischemia, and outcomes by vascular occlusion status.

**BP-TARGET** — an RCT in the thrombectomy population. 324 patients were randomized after successful endovascular therapy to an intensive systolic blood pressure target (achieving a mean of 128 mm Hg) versus a standard target (achieving a mean of 138 mm Hg), with the primary outcome (radiographic hemorrhage) observed in 42% versus 43% of patients respectively, showing no significant difference.

**OPTIMAL-BP** — an RCT in the thrombectomy population conducted in South Korea. 306 patients with large-vessel occlusion stroke treated with thrombectomy were randomized to intensive (systolic blood pressure target under 140 mm Hg) versus conventional (target 140–180 mm Hg) management; 39.4% in the intensive group versus 54.4% in the conventional group achieved functional independence at 3 months, a significant difference suggesting harm from intensive lowering.

**ENCHANTED2/MT** — a large RCT in the thrombectomy population, stopped early for safety concerns. Conducted at 44 tertiary-level hospitals in China, eligible patients had persistently elevated systolic blood pressure (≥140 mm Hg for more than 10 minutes) following successful reperfusion with endovascular thrombectomy, and patients were randomly assigned to more intensive treatment (systolic blood pressure target under 120 mm Hg) or less intensive treatment (target 140–180 mm Hg), to be achieved within one hour and sustained for 72 hours. The trial's conclusion was that intensive control of systolic blood pressure to lower than 120 mm Hg should be avoided to prevent compromising functional recovery after thrombectomy.

**Pilot RCT of early intensive BP lowering on cerebral perfusion (Mistry et al.)** — a smaller physiological RCT in thrombolysed patients. 54 thrombolysed patients with systolic blood pressure of 160–180 mm Hg were randomized to early intensive blood pressure lowering (target 140–160 mm Hg) or guideline-based management (target 160–180 mm Hg) over 72 hours, primarily with intravenous labetalol, assessing effects on cerebral blood flow and hypoperfusion volume via CT perfusion, along with 90-day functional outcome and symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage. (Registered as NCT03443596.)

## Observational Study

**International multicenter observational study of SBP goals after mechanical thrombectomy** — an observational study of 11,019 patients found that patients with a systolic blood pressure goal under 140 mm Hg had increased odds of good functional outcomes and lower odds of requiring hemicraniectomy compared with those with a goal under 180 mm Hg.

Note: I excluded INTERACT4 from this list since it targeted pre-hospital/pre-treatment blood pressure in a mixed suspected-stroke population (not specifically post-reperfusion), and I excluded the low- vs standard-dose alteplase arm of ENCHANTED since that compares thrombolytic dosing rather than blood pressure targets.
