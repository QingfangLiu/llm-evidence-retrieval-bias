## Bottom line

The evidence depends on **how blood flow was restored**:

* **After mechanical thrombectomy:** Using one aggressively low systolic blood-pressure target for nearly everyone—particularly **below 120 mm Hg or below 140 mm Hg during the first 24–72 hours—has generally produced no bleeding benefit and has sometimes worsened neurological recovery or functional independence.**
* **After IV alteplase thrombolysis:** Targeting **130–140 mm Hg** reduced the frequency of any intracranial bleeding, but it did **not improve disability or functional recovery at 90 days**.
* A newer 2026 thrombectomy trial suggests that **individualized targets based on how completely the artery was reopened** may be beneficial. This strategy differed importantly from simply lowering everyone below 140 mm Hg and requires confirmation.

These studies concern the **acute first 1–3 days after reperfusion**. They do not address the benefits of treating chronic hypertension during later stroke prevention.

## Potential benefits and risks

### Possible benefits of more intensive lowering

The proposed benefit is to reduce reperfusion-related injury, hemorrhagic transformation, cerebral edema and symptomatic brain hemorrhage. In practice:

* After **alteplase**, ENCHANTED found less “any intracranial hemorrhage”: **14.8% versus 18.7%**, but no corresponding improvement in 90-day function. ([PubMed][1])
* After **thrombectomy**, BP-TARGET found essentially identical radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage rates—**42% versus 43%**—despite targeting 100–129 rather than 130–185 mm Hg. ([PubMed][2])
* Most thrombectomy trials found no significant reduction in **symptomatic** intracranial hemorrhage with intensive treatment. ([PubMed][3])

### Possible harms

After thrombectomy, the recovering brain may remain dependent on systemic blood pressure because of incomplete microvascular reperfusion, impaired autoregulation, residual arterial narrowing or poor collateral flow. Excessive lowering can therefore plausibly reduce perfusion of vulnerable tissue.

The clinical signals include:

* Worse overall disability and more early neurological deterioration with a target **below 120 mm Hg** in ENCHANTED2/MT. ([PubMed][3])
* A **15.1-percentage-point reduction in functional independence** with a target below 140 mm Hg in OPTIMAL-BP. ([JAMA Network][4])
* More hypotensive episodes with a target below 130 mm Hg in IDENTIFY: **57.5% versus 32.7%**. ([PMC][5])
* In OPTIMAL-BP, the early disadvantage remained evident at one year: functional independence was **40.5% versus 52.7%**, although the intention-to-treat comparison narrowly missed conventional statistical significance. ([PubMed][6])

## How I would interpret the evidence

For someone who underwent **mechanical thrombectomy**, the strongest completed randomized trials argue against routinely and rapidly pushing SBP:

* below **120 mm Hg**, or
* below **140 mm Hg using a single target regardless of reperfusion status**.

There is no convincing evidence that those fixed strategies prevent symptomatic brain hemorrhage, and several trials demonstrate worse recovery. Avoiding hypotension, large abrupt reductions and excessive BP variability appears especially important.

The 2026 HOPE result is encouraging but answers a somewhat different question: it tested **hemodynamic management tailored to the final reperfusion grade**, including maintaining a higher pressure in patients with incomplete reperfusion. It should not be interpreted as proof that SBP below 140 is beneficial for all post-thrombectomy patients.

For someone treated with **alteplase but not thrombectomy**, intensive lowering to 130–140 may reduce radiographic or clinically detected bleeding, but ENCHANTED did not demonstrate better survival or functional recovery. The balance may differ according to stroke severity, arterial patency and how much potentially salvageable tissue remains.
