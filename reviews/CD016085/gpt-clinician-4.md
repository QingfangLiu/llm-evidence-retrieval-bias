## Bottom line

Among patients with **successful endovascular reperfusion**, a universal strategy of promptly lowering systolic blood pressure to very low targets has not improved outcomes:

* **SBP <120 mm Hg caused clear harm** in ENCHANTED2/MT.
* **SBP <140 mm Hg reduced functional independence** in OPTIMAL-BP, with the adverse effect still evident at 1 year.
* Targets of **<130, 100–129, <140, or <160 mm Hg** were otherwise neutral or unlikely to provide benefit in IDENTIFY, BP-TARGET, BEST-II, and DETECT.
* A notable exception is the 2026 **HOPE trial**, where a **reperfusion-guided target range with prevention/treatment of hypotension** improved functional outcomes. HOPE should not be interpreted as evidence for indiscriminate intensive lowering.

After **IV thrombolysis**, ENCHANTED found that targeting 130–140 mm Hg reduced radiographic intracranial hemorrhage but did not improve 90-day function. Importantly, thrombolysis-treated patients were not required to have documented successful recanalization.

## Individual trial findings

### IV thrombolysis

**ENCHANTED, 2019 — n=2,227**

Thrombolysis-treated patients with SBP ≥150 mm Hg were randomized to an intensive SBP target of **130–140 mm Hg within 1 hour**, maintained for 72 hours, or standard management with SBP **<180 mm Hg**.

The achieved 24-hour mean SBPs were only modestly separated—144.3 versus 149.8 mm Hg. There was **no improvement in the 90-day mRS distribution**: common OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.87–1.17. Any intracranial hemorrhage was reduced from 18.7% to 14.8%, OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.94, without a significant difference in serious adverse events. Thus, intensive control produced an imaging benefit but not a patient-centered functional benefit. ([PubMed][1])

A prespecified severe-stroke analysis of 1,311 ENCHANTED participants found no reduction in death or disability. Intensive treatment reduced clinician-reported ICH but was associated with **higher mortality**, OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.09–2.13. This subgroup finding warrants caution because it was not the main trial result. ([PubMed][2])

### Successful endovascular reperfusion: fixed lower targets

**BP-TARGET, 2021 — n=324**

After successful anterior-circulation thrombectomy, patients were assigned to SBP **100–129 mm Hg** or **130–185 mm Hg** for 24 hours. Mean achieved SBPs were approximately 128 and 138 mm Hg.

The primary imaging outcome—radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage—occurred in 42% versus 43%, adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.60–1.51. Hypotension occurred numerically more often with intensive control, 8% versus 3%. The study therefore showed no hemorrhage-prevention benefit from targeting below 130 mm Hg and was not powered to establish modest differences in clinical outcomes. ([PubMed][3])

**ENCHANTED2/MT, 2022 — n=821**

Patients with successful reperfusion and persistent SBP ≥140 mm Hg were assigned to **SBP <120 mm Hg** or **140–180 mm Hg** for 72 hours. The trial was stopped early.

Intensive treatment produced a worse 90-day mRS distribution, common OR for worse outcome 1.37, 95% CI 1.07–1.76; more early neurologic deterioration, OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.18–1.97; and more major disability, OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.47–2.93. It did **not** significantly reduce symptomatic ICH or mortality. This is the strongest evidence that post-thrombectomy SBP lowering below 120 mm Hg is harmful. ([PubMed][4])

**OPTIMAL-BP, 2023 — n=306**

Patients with successful reperfusion and SBP ≥140 mm Hg were randomized to **SBP <140 mm Hg** or **140–180 mm Hg** for 24 hours. The trial was stopped early for safety concerns.

Functional independence at 3 months occurred in 39.4% with intensive treatment versus 54.4% with conventional treatment—an absolute difference of −15.1 percentage points and adjusted OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.96. Symptomatic ICH was similar, 9.0% versus 8.1%, as was stroke-related death, 7.7% versus 5.4%. Malignant cerebral edema was more frequent with intensive control, 7.7% versus 1.3%, adjusted OR 7.88, although this estimate was imprecise. ([PubMed][5])

At 1 year, functional independence remained numerically lower with intensive treatment, 40.5% versus 52.7%, adjusted OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.34–1.00; the per-protocol analysis was statistically significant. One-year mortality did not differ. This suggests that the early functional harm was not subsequently recovered. ([PubMed][6])

**BEST-II, 2023 — n=120**

This phase 2 futility trial compared targets of **<140, <160, and ≤180 mm Hg** for 24 hours. It did not cross its prespecified futility or harm boundaries for infarct volume or utility-weighted 90-day mRS. However, the predicted probabilities that a larger phase 3 superiority trial would succeed were only 25% for <140 and 14% for <160.

Symptomatic ICH was 5%, 3%, and 5%, respectively. Median 90-day mRS was 3, 3, and 2. BEST-II did not prove harm, but it provided little evidence that either moderately lower target would improve outcomes. ([JAMA Network][7])

**DETECT pilot — n=30**

This single-center feasibility trial compared **SBP <140** with **<180 mm Hg** for 48 hours. Mean SBPs were approximately 131 and 139 mm Hg. Recruitment was difficult, and spontaneous BP normalization limited separation between groups. No clear differences appeared in exploratory neurologic, functional, hemorrhagic, or transcranial Doppler outcomes.

The publication also contains a meta-analysis; only its original randomized pilot-trial data are considered here. ([PubMed][8])

**IDENTIFY, 2025 — n=383**

After successful anterior-circulation reperfusion, patients were assigned to **SBP <130** or **<180 mm Hg** for 24 hours. The trial was terminated after a neutral interim analysis and publication of related trials.

Unfavorable 90-day outcome, mRS 3–6, occurred in 71.0% versus 67.5%, adjusted RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.95–1.15. Symptomatic ICH, malignant cerebral edema, and mortality did not differ significantly. This trial found no benefit from a fixed target below 130 mm Hg. ([PMC][9])

### Successful endovascular reperfusion: individualized range strategy

**HOPE, 2026 — n=440**

HOPE used a different intervention:

* Final mTICI 2b: SBP **140–160 mm Hg**
* Final mTICI 2c/3: SBP **100–140 mm Hg**
* Control: SBP **<180 mm Hg**

Targets were maintained for 72 hours using antihypertensives **or vasopressors** as required, with defined lower safety thresholds.

Favorable 90-day outcome, mRS 0–2, occurred in 60.0% versus 47.1%, absolute difference 13.3 percentage points; adjusted OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.11–2.63. Hemorrhagic transformation was reduced, 22.3% versus 31.6%, OR 0.62, while symptomatic ICH, mortality, neurologic deterioration, and serious adverse events were not significantly different. ([JAMA Network][10])

Interpretation requires care. HOPE was stopped before reaching its revised sample size, and most participants had mTICI 2c/3 reperfusion; only 43 of 440 had mTICI 2b. The intervention also bundled target selection with intensive monitoring, a BP floor, longer protocol duration, and active hypotension management. It therefore supports a **protocolized, reperfusion-tailored hemodynamic strategy**, not simply “lower is better.” ([JAMA Network][10])

## Practical interpretation of benefits and risks

The most consistent risk signal is **cerebral hypoperfusion or infarct progression from overly aggressive lowering**, manifested clinically as worse disability, early neurologic deterioration, and possibly malignant edema. This is most convincing for SBP <120 and is also supported by OPTIMAL-BP at <140.

The expected benefit—less reperfusion-related hemorrhage—has been inconsistent. ENCHANTED after thrombolysis and HOPE after thrombectomy reduced broadly defined radiographic hemorrhage, but BP-TARGET, ENCHANTED2/MT, OPTIMAL-BP, BEST-II, and IDENTIFY did not demonstrate a meaningful symptomatic ICH benefit.

Taken together, the trials argue against using a **single low SBP target for every successfully reperfused patient**. HOPE raises the possibility that target ranges based on completeness of reperfusion, coupled with avoidance of hypotension, may be beneficial, but confirmation is important before regarding it as settled evidence.
