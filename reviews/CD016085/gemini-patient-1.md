First, I want to acknowledge what you’ve been through. Experiencing an ischemic stroke and undergoing a procedure to reopen a blocked artery—whether through clot-busting medication or a mechanical thrombectomy—is an incredibly intense medical event. It is completely understandable that you want to understand the exact science behind how your recovery and blood pressure are being managed.

After an artery is successfully unblocked (a process called *reperfusion*), doctors face a delicate balancing act with your blood pressure:

* **The Risk of High Blood Pressure:** When blood suddenly rushes back into fragile, previously starved brain tissue, blood pressure that is too high can cause swelling or bleeding in the brain (reperfusion injury).
* **The Risk of Low Blood Pressure:** The brain still needs adequate, forceful blood flow. The smaller surrounding blood vessels in the stroke area are often still struggling. Dropping the blood pressure too low can starve vulnerable but salvageable brain tissue of the oxygen it needs to survive.

To figure out the safest balance, researchers have conducted several major **randomized controlled trials** (primary studies) to see if "intensive" blood pressure lowering is better or worse than "standard/conventional" management.

Here is what the primary clinical studies have found.

## What the Primary Studies Show

Recent primary trials have consistently shown that **intensive blood pressure lowering after a successfully reopened artery is either not helpful or actively harmful** compared to standard blood pressure control.

**1. Intensive Lowering Worsens Functional Recovery**
In the **ENCHANTED2/MT trial**, researchers compared an intensive systolic blood pressure target (under 120 mm Hg) with a standard target (140–180 mm Hg) in patients who had their arteries successfully opened via endovascular thrombectomy (Yang et al., 2022). The trial had to be stopped early because the intensive blood pressure control group had significantly *worse* functional recovery and a higher rate of early neurological deterioration compared to the standard group.

**2. Intensive Lowering Reduces Functional Independence**
The **OPTIMAL-BP trial** similarly compared an intensive target (under 140 mm Hg) to a conventional target (140–180 mm Hg) for 24 hours after successful thrombectomy (Nam et al., 2023). Like the ENCHANTED2/MT study, this trial was also terminated early due to safety concerns. Patients in the intensive blood pressure group were significantly less likely to achieve functional independence at 3 months compared to those in the conventional group.

**3. Intensive Lowering Does Not Prevent Brain Bleeds**
The rationale for lowering blood pressure intensively is often to prevent brain bleeding. However, the **BP-TARGET trial** tested whether an intensive target (under 130 mm Hg) would reduce the rate of hemorrhage compared to a standard target (under 185 mm Hg) (Mazighi et al., 2021). The researchers found no significant reduction in brain bleeding with intensive control, and it provided no clinical or functional benefit to the patients' outcomes.

## The Takeaway

While it might intuitively seem like lower blood pressure is always healthier, the clinical evidence points in the exact opposite direction immediately following an ischemic stroke treatment. Pushing blood pressure down too aggressively in the first 24 hours seems to restrict essential blood flow to the recovering brain penumbra, leading to worse long-term recovery.

Allowing the blood pressure to run a bit higher (often keeping the upper number between 140 and 180 mm Hg) ensures the brain gets the oxygen it needs to heal.
