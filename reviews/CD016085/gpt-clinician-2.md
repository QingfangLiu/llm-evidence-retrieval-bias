## Scope

I searched for peer-reviewed **comparative primary studies** in patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis or successful endovascular thrombectomy, focusing on randomized blood-pressure targets during the first 24–72 hours after treatment. I found **one major thrombolysis trial and seven post-thrombectomy randomized trials** through July 25, 2026.

I excluded protocols, guidelines, reviews, editorials, and pooled/meta-analytic results. The DETECT publication contains both a pilot trial and a meta-analysis; below, I use only its randomized pilot-trial data.

## Bottom line

**After IV alteplase**, targeting SBP 130–140 mm Hg rather than <180 mm Hg reduced radiographic intracranial hemorrhage but did **not** improve 90-day disability. A secondary analysis raised concern about increased mortality among patients with severe strokes. ([PubMed][1])

**After successful thrombectomy**, routine fixed intensive targets—particularly **<120 mm Hg**, but also <130 or <140 mm Hg—have not shown a functional benefit. Larger trials found worse functional recovery, while hemorrhagic outcomes were generally unchanged. ([PubMed][2])

The important exception is the **2026 HOPE trial**, which did not impose the same low target on everyone. It used **140–160 mm Hg after incomplete mTICI 2b reperfusion** and **100–140 mm Hg after mTICI 2c/3 reperfusion**, permitting antihypertensives or vasopressors to remain within range. This tailored strategy improved functional independence and reduced any hemorrhagic transformation, although it requires replication. ([JAMA Network][3])

## Benefits of more intensive control

### Reduced radiographic bleeding

The clearest bleeding benefit occurred with thrombolysis in ENCHANTED: any ICH was reduced by approximately four absolute percentage points. However, that did not translate into better disability outcomes. ([PubMed][1])

After thrombectomy, fixed low-target trials did **not** consistently reduce intraparenchymal hemorrhage, symptomatic ICH, or other clinically important bleeding. BP-TARGET was explicitly designed around radiographic hemorrhage and was neutral. ([PubMed][4])

HOPE reduced **any hemorrhagic transformation**, but not symptomatic ICH. Its result may reflect matching the BP range to the completeness of reperfusion rather than simply lowering BP more aggressively. ([JAMA Network][3])

### Possible functional benefit from individualized targets

HOPE produced a 13.3% absolute increase in mRS 0–2, corresponding to an approximate number needed to treat of eight. This is the first randomized trial to demonstrate a functional benefit, but the intervention included both avoidance of excessive BP after near-complete reperfusion and preservation or augmentation of pressure after incomplete reperfusion. It therefore should not be interpreted as evidence for a universal SBP target below 140 mm Hg. ([JAMA Network][3])

## Risks of more intensive control

### Worse functional recovery

The most consistent risk is worsened disability rather than increased symptomatic hemorrhage. ENCHANTED2/MT found worse ordinal mRS, early neurologic deterioration and major disability with SBP <120 mm Hg. OPTIMAL-BP found a 15.1% absolute reduction in functional independence with SBP <140 mm Hg. ([ScienceDirect][5])

The adverse effect in OPTIMAL-BP appears durable. At one year, functional independence remained numerically lower with intensive treatment—**40.5% versus 52.7%**, adjusted OR 0.59, P=0.051 in intention-to-treat analysis—and was significantly lower in the per-protocol analysis. Mortality did not differ. ([PubMed][10])

### Hypotension and possible hypoperfusion

IDENTIFY documented substantially more protocol-defined hypotension with intensive treatment. This provides a plausible pathway for worsened outcomes: even after angiographic reperfusion, residual distal occlusion, microvascular no-reflow, collateral dependence and impaired autoregulation may make cerebral perfusion pressure sensitive to systemic BP. The trials establish the clinical association with assigned treatment, although they do not directly prove this mechanism. ([PMC][9])

### Greater concern in severe or vulnerable brain disease

In a prespecified ENCHANTED analysis of severe ischemic stroke, intensive treatment reduced clinician-reported ICH but increased mortality, OR **1.52**, 95% CI 1.09–2.13. Because this was a subgroup analysis rather than a separate randomized trial, it should be treated as an important safety signal rather than definitive proof. ([PubMed][11])

A 2026 secondary OPTIMAL-BP analysis found that among patients with severe white-matter hyperintensity, functional independence was **29.9% with intensive management versus 51.3% with conventional management**, adjusted OR 0.36. There was no statistically significant treatment difference in patients with mild WMH. This supports—without definitively establishing—the possibility that impaired autoregulation increases susceptibility to BP reduction. ([PubMed][12])

## Practical interpretation of the evidence

The trials support several distinctions:

* **SBP <120 mm Hg after thrombectomy is harmful** and should not be considered analogous to ordinary prevention-oriented intensive BP control.
* Routine forced reduction to **<130 or <140 mm Hg** after successful EVT has not shown benefit and may cause functional harm, particularly when it produces hypotension.
* A ceiling such as “<180 mm Hg” is not the same as deliberately maintaining SBP near 180; spontaneous BP trajectories and treatment exposure differed substantially between studies.
* The final reperfusion grade matters. HOPE suggests that patients with mTICI 2b may require a higher pressure range than those with mTICI 2c/3.
* Evidence for thrombolysis without thrombectomy is somewhat different: moderate lowering reduced any ICH but did not improve functional outcome.
* No fixed intensive post-EVT strategy has shown a convincing reduction in symptomatic ICH or mortality.
