Based on the most recent and rigorously conducted primary randomized clinical trials, intensive blood pressure (BP) lowering after successful endovascular thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke does not improve patient outcomes compared to standard BP control. In fact, multiple trials have demonstrated that intensive BP lowering actually **worsens** functional recovery and increases the likelihood of major disability.

The physiological rationale suggests that aggressive BP lowering might compromise cerebral perfusion in areas with impaired autoregulation (the ischemic penumbra) or where microcirculation was damaged, ultimately exacerbating ischemic injury.

Here is a breakdown of the primary clinical trial evidence comparing intensive versus standard or conventional BP targets following successful reperfusion.

## Trials Demonstrating Harm from Intensive BP Control

Two major trials explicitly concluded that intensive blood pressure lowering is detrimental to patient outcomes.

* **ENCHANTED2/MT (2022):** This large multicenter trial randomized patients with large-vessel occlusion ischemic stroke to intensive BP control (systolic target <120 mm Hg) versus less intensive control (target 140–180 mm Hg). The trial was stopped early due to clear safety concerns. Patients in the intensive BP arm experienced significantly worse functional recovery (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] at 90 days), with higher rates of early neurological deterioration and major disability (Yang et al., 2022).
* **OPTIMAL-BP (2023):** Aiming for a systolic target of <140 mm Hg was compared to a conventional target of 140–180 mm Hg for 24 hours post-reperfusion. Like ENCHANTED2/MT, this trial found that intensive BP lowering significantly reduced the likelihood of a favorable functional outcome (mRS 0–2) at three months. Aiming for <140 mm Hg was associated with nearly twice the odds of a poor outcome without any meaningful reduction in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or death (Nam et al., 2023).

## Trials Demonstrating No Benefit from Intensive BP Control

Two other key trials failed to show any clinical advantage for tighter BP parameters and echoed similar caution.

* **BP-TARGET (2021):** This trial evaluated an intensive systolic target (100–129 mm Hg) compared with standard care (130–185 mm Hg) in the first 24 hours. The primary aim was to see if intensive control reduced the rates of intraparenchymal hemorrhage. The rates of radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage at 24 to 36 hours were nearly identical (42% in the intensive group vs. 43% in the standard group), with no significant differences in clinical outcomes or mortality (Mazighi et al., 2021).
* **BEST-II (2023):** This phase 2 futility trial randomized patients to three different systolic targets: ≤180 mm Hg, <160 mm Hg, or <140 mm Hg. The study found that aiming for the lower targets (<160 or <140 mm Hg) did not meet criteria for futility outright in reducing infarct volume or improving clinical scores, but subsequent predictive analysis concluded there was an exceptionally low probability that future, larger trials would ever demonstrate a clinical benefit for the lower BP targets (Mistry et al., 2023).
