## What the primary trials show

This evidence concerns **blood-pressure management during approximately the first 24–72 hours after reperfusion treatment**. It is separate from long-term blood-pressure control to prevent another stroke.

### Main conclusion

For patients whose blocked artery was reopened with **mechanical thrombectomy**, using the same low systolic blood-pressure target for everyone has generally **not improved outcomes**:

* A target below **120 mm Hg** worsened neurological and functional recovery.
* A target below **140 mm Hg** worsened functional outcome in one major trial and showed no benefit in several others.
* These lower targets generally **did not reduce symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage**.
* A 2026 trial found benefit from a **reperfusion-tailored strategy**, but this was not simple aggressive lowering: patients with incomplete reperfusion were kept at a higher pressure, and vasopressors could be used to prevent pressure from falling too low.

Thus, the likely risk is not merely drug-related hypotension. Lowering pressure too far may reduce blood flow through injured small vessels or incompletely reperfused tissue, potentially enlarging ischemic injury despite reopening of the main artery. The potential benefit—less reperfusion-related bleeding—has been inconsistent and has usually involved radiographic bleeding rather than clinically symptomatic bleeding. ([DOI][1])

## Trials after mechanical thrombectomy

### BP-TARGET, 2021

This trial randomized 324 patients after successful thrombectomy to systolic BP **100–129 mm Hg** or **130–185 mm Hg** for 24 hours.

Radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage occurred in 42% versus 43%, respectively. Intensive treatment therefore produced **no bleeding benefit**. Hypotension occurred numerically more often with intensive treatment, 8% versus 3%, although the difference was not statistically conclusive. ([PubMed][2])

### ENCHANTED2/MT, 2022

Among 821 patients with successful reperfusion and persistently elevated BP, the targets were **below 120 mm Hg** versus **140–180 mm Hg** for 72 hours. The trial was stopped early.

The below-120 strategy caused:

* Worse overall 90-day disability: common odds ratio 1.37.
* More early neurological deterioration: common odds ratio 1.53.
* More major disability: odds ratio 2.07.

There was **no significant reduction in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or mortality**. This is the strongest evidence against very intensive post-thrombectomy lowering. ([PubMed][3])

### BEST-II, 2023

This phase 2 futility trial randomized 120 patients to targets of **below 140**, **below 160**, or **180 mm Hg or less** for 24 hours.

It was not powered to establish clinical superiority or harm. Patient-centered outcome estimates numerically favored the highest target, and the predicted chances that a future large trial would prove benefit were only 25% for below 140 and 14% for below 160. Symptomatic hemorrhage was similar among groups. ([JAMA Network][4])

### OPTIMAL-BP, 2023

This trial randomized 306 patients after successful thrombectomy to **below 140 mm Hg** or **140–180 mm Hg** for 24 hours. It was stopped early because of safety concerns.

Functional independence at 90 days occurred in:

* **39.4%** with intensive treatment.
* **54.4%** with conventional treatment.

That was an absolute difference of 15.1 percentage points against intensive treatment. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was essentially unchanged, 9.0% versus 8.1%. Malignant cerebral edema occurred in 7.7% versus 1.3%, although this secondary result had a wide confidence interval. ([DOI][1])

A 2026 one-year follow-up continued to show fewer independent patients in the intensive group—40.5% versus 52.7%. The intention-to-treat result narrowly missed conventional statistical significance, while the per-protocol analysis remained significant; mortality was not different. ([PubMed][5])

### DETECT feasibility trial, 2024

Thirty patients were randomized to **below 140 mm Hg** or **below 180 mm Hg** for 48 hours. There were no clear differences in neurological deterioration, functional improvement, intracranial hemorrhage, or transcranial Doppler measurements.

The study mainly demonstrated feasibility problems: BP frequently declined without medication, and recruitment was slow. It was far too small to determine clinical benefit or harm. The associated publication also contains a meta-analysis, but only its individual randomized feasibility-trial results are used here. ([PubMed][6])

### IDENTIFY, 2025

This multicenter trial randomized 383 patients after successful thrombectomy to **below 130 mm Hg** or **below 180 mm Hg** through 24 hours.

Unfavorable outcome—modified Rankin Scale 3–6—occurred in 71.0% versus 67.5%, respectively: no significant benefit and a numerical direction against intensive treatment. Symptomatic hemorrhage, malignant edema, and 90-day mortality were not significantly different. ([PMC][7])

### CLEVER, 2026

Eighty patients with near-complete or complete reperfusion were randomized to **90–120 mm Hg** or **90–160 mm Hg**, using clevidipine as the principal treatment.

Any hemorrhagic transformation occurred in 32.5% versus 35.0%, showing no benefit. Good functional outcome occurred in 47.5% versus 57.5%, a nonsignificant numerical difference against intensive treatment. Symptomatic hemorrhage, mortality, and transient hypotension were uncommon and not clearly different. ([OUP Academic][8])

### HOPE, 2026: the important exception

HOPE tested a **reperfusion-guided hemodynamic strategy**, not one fixed low target:

* For substantial but incomplete reperfusion, mTICI 2b: **140–160 mm Hg**.
* For near-complete or complete reperfusion, mTICI 2c/3: **100–140 mm Hg**.
* Control group: **below 180 mm Hg**.

Antihypertensives or vasopressors were used as needed for 72 hours. ([JAMA Network][9])

Among 440 patients, favorable 90-day outcome occurred in **60.0% versus 47.1%**, an absolute difference of 13.3 percentage points. Any radiographic hemorrhagic transformation was also lower, 22.3% versus 31.6%. However:

* Symptomatic hemorrhage was unchanged: 3.5% versus 3.9%.
* Mortality was unchanged: 15.4% versus 15.6%.
* Serious adverse events were not significantly different. ([JAMA Network][9])

The result requires caution. The trial stopped before reaching its planned enrollment because funding ended; patients with significant extracranial or intracranial stenosis were commonly excluded; most participants had near-complete or complete reperfusion, leaving relatively few patients in the higher-target mTICI 2b group. The investigators themselves described the result as **hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing**. ([JAMA Network][9])

## Trial after intravenous thrombolysis

### ENCHANTED, 2019

This trial is relevant when artery-opening treatment was **intravenous alteplase**, but it did not require documented successful reopening of a large artery. It randomized 2,196 patients to systolic BP **130–140 mm Hg** or **below 180 mm Hg** for 72 hours.

There was no improvement in 90-day disability: common odds ratio 1.01. Any intracranial hemorrhage was reduced from 18.7% to 14.8%, but this radiographic benefit did not translate into better functional recovery. ([PubMed][10])

## Benefits and risks in practical terms

**Possible benefits**

* Less radiographic hemorrhagic transformation was demonstrated in ENCHANTED after alteplase and in the tailored HOPE thrombectomy strategy.
* HOPE also found better functional recovery, but confirmation is needed because its strategy and study population differed substantially from the earlier trials.

**Demonstrated or plausible risks**

* Worse disability and neurological deterioration when systolic BP was pushed below 120 mm Hg.
* Less functional independence with a fixed target below 140 mm Hg in OPTIMAL-BP.
* Possible worsening of cerebral ischemia or edema if systemic pressure becomes insufficient for vulnerable tissue.
* Hypotensive episodes and the need for close titration, although serious medication-related events were not consistently increased.
* No dependable compensating reduction in **symptomatic** brain hemorrhage.

These targets were delivered in closely monitored stroke units, often with continuous intravenous medications. They should not be used to adjust home medications independently. The most personally relevant details are whether you received thrombectomy, how complete the reperfusion was, whether residual intracranial stenosis remained, and what your BP was during the first day.
