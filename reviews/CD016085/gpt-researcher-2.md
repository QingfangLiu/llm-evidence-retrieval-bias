## Scope and search approach

I searched for primary comparative studies available through **July 25, 2026** that assigned patients to more-intensive versus standard blood-pressure management after:

* intravenous thrombolysis, or
* successful endovascular thrombectomy/reperfusion.

I excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinion pieces, protocols without results, and studies examining only an association between achieved blood pressure and outcomes. The main evidence base comprises randomized, open-label trials with blinded outcome assessment.

## Overall interpretation

### After intravenous thrombolysis

The largest trial, ENCHANTED, found that targeting systolic blood pressure (SBP) **130–140 mm Hg**, rather than **<180 mm Hg**, reduced any intracranial hemorrhage but did **not** improve 90-day functional outcome. The mean achieved SBP separation was only about 5.5 mm Hg. ([PubMed][1])

A prespecified analysis among patients with more severe ischemic stroke found a potentially important tradeoff: intensive treatment reduced clinician-reported intracranial hemorrhage but was associated with higher mortality. Because this was a subgroup analysis rather than a separate randomized comparison, it should be treated as hypothesis-generating. ([PubMed][2])

A second, small 2026 conference-only randomized study found no functional or mortality benefit from an SBP target below 160 mm Hg. Its estimates were very imprecise, and any intracranial hemorrhage was numerically more frequent with intensive control. ([PMC][3])

**Interpretation:** after thrombolysis, moderate intensive lowering may reduce radiographic hemorrhage, but there is no demonstrated functional benefit. Excessive lowering may be problematic in severe strokes or patients who remain perfusion-dependent.

### After successful thrombectomy: fixed low targets

The post-thrombectomy trials testing a fixed, lower target for nearly all patients are predominantly neutral or harmful:

* **100–129 mm Hg:** no reduction in radiographic hemorrhage in BP-TARGET.
* **<120 mm Hg:** worse functional outcome and more neurological deterioration in ENCHANTED2/MT.
* **<140 mm Hg:** worse functional independence in OPTIMAL-BP.
* **<130 mm Hg:** no benefit in IDENTIFY.
* **<140 or <160 mm Hg:** no convincing benefit and low predicted probability of success in BEST-II.
* The small DETECT feasibility trial was underpowered and found no clinical differences.

Importantly, the fixed-target trials generally did **not** show a reduction in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage sufficient to offset the apparent functional harm. ([PubMed][4])

### After successful thrombectomy: individualized strategy

HOPE, published in June 2026, differs substantially from the preceding trials. It did not simply assign every patient to the same lower target. Instead, treatment was based on reperfusion grade:

* mTICI 2b: SBP 140–160 mm Hg
* mTICI 2c/3: SBP 100–140 mm Hg
* control: SBP <180 mm Hg

The protocol could use either antihypertensives or vasopressors to keep patients within range. Favorable 90-day outcome occurred in 60.0% versus 47.1%, while hemorrhagic transformation was lower and symptomatic hemorrhage and mortality were similar. ([JAMA Network][5])

This finding supports **reperfusion-guided hemodynamic management**, but it should not be interpreted as showing that routine intensive BP lowering is beneficial. In patients with incomplete reperfusion, the intervention actually preserved a higher SBP range.

## Longer-term and organ-safety findings

The OPTIMAL-BP 1-year extension suggests that the early functional disadvantage may persist. Functional independence was 40.5% with intensive treatment versus 52.7% with conventional treatment; the intention-to-treat adjusted OR was 0.59, with the confidence interval reaching 1.00. Mortality and change between 3 months and 1 year were similar. ([PubMed][11])

In an ENCHANTED renal analysis, intensive treatment did not produce a significant early decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate relative to standard management. Greater BP variability, however, was associated with a larger decline in renal function. ([PubMed][12])

## Important applicability considerations

The evidence does not identify one universally optimal post-reperfusion SBP. Several factors modify how the findings should be interpreted:

1. **Successful versus incomplete reperfusion:** residual ischemic tissue may remain pressure-dependent after mTICI 2b reperfusion.
2. **Magnitude and speed of reduction:** abrupt declines may be more important than the nominal target.
3. **Achieved separation:** ENCHANTED, BP-TARGET and DETECT achieved relatively modest between-group BP differences.
4. **Elevated-BP populations:** most trials enrolled patients with persistent hypertension; their results do not apply directly to patients whose BP is already low or normal.
5. **Early stopping:** ENCHANTED2/MT, OPTIMAL-BP and IDENTIFY stopped before their originally intended completion, reducing precision and potentially affecting effect estimates.
6. **HOPE’s distinct intervention:** HOPE tested an individualized hemodynamic protocol, including vasopressors, rather than uniform antihypertensive intensification.

## Evidence-based conclusion

For patients receiving **IV thrombolysis**, an SBP target around 130–140 mm Hg may reduce any intracranial hemorrhage, but it has not improved functional recovery and may be hazardous in severe stroke subgroups.

For patients with **successful thrombectomy**, routinely forcing SBP below 120–140 mm Hg for all patients provides no established hemorrhagic benefit and has repeatedly produced neutral or worse functional outcomes. The strongest harms were seen with **<120 mm Hg** and with the **<140 mm Hg** strategy in OPTIMAL-BP.

The emerging alternative is not simply “lower BP more.” HOPE suggests that targets may need to be individualized according to reperfusion completeness, potentially maintaining moderately higher pressure after incomplete reperfusion and allowing lower pressure after near-complete or complete reperfusion.
