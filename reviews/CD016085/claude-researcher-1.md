## Overview of the primary trials

Five randomized controlled trials directly address intensive vs. standard/conventional blood pressure targets after reperfusion therapy (thrombolysis and/or endovascular thrombectomy) for acute ischemic stroke:

**1. ENCHANTED (2019)** — the earliest and largest trial in this space, tested intensive systolic BP lowering (target 130–140 mmHg) vs. guideline-standard care (<180 mmHg) after **intravenous thrombolysis**. It found no improvement in functional outcome with intensive lowering, though it was associated with fewer symptomatic intracranial hemorrhages.

**2. BP-TARGET (2021)** — a French multicenter trial in 324 patients after **successful endovascular thrombectomy (EVT)**, comparing an intensive systolic BP target (<130 mmHg) with a standard target (<185 mmHg) over 24 hours. It found intensive control (achieved mean 128 mmHg) versus standard control (achieved mean 138 mmHg) was similarly safe but did not improve functional outcome.

**3. OPTIMAL-BP (2023)** — a Korean multicenter trial in 306 patients after successful EVT, comparing intensive (<140 mmHg) vs. conventional (140–180 mmHg) systolic BP targets. It was stopped early: 39.4% of patients with intensive BP management and 54.4% of those with conventional BP management achieved functional independence at 3 months, a significant difference favoring conventional management, suggesting intensive lowering may be harmful.

**4. ENCHANTED2/MT (2022)** — a multicenter Chinese trial testing intensive (<120 mmHg) vs. standard (140–180 mmHg) systolic BP targets after successful EVT. It was stopped early because of harm from intensive BP lowering, after 347 of a planned 816 patients had been randomized.

**5. BEST-II (2023)** — a US multicenter, dose-finding trial comparing two intensive systolic BP targets (<140 mmHg and <160 mmHg) against a standard target (<180 mmHg) after EVT. It found no clear benefit from lower targets, with results suggesting low probability of benefit from more intensive lowering.

Together, these five trials converge on a consistent signal: intensive BP lowering after reperfusion therapy does not improve — and in several trials appeared to worsen — functional outcomes, without consistently reducing hemorrhagic risk, though achieved BP separations were sometimes modest (as in BP-TARGET), which complicates interpretation.
