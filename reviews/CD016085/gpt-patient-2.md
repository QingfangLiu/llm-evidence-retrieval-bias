## What the primary studies show

This evidence concerns **blood-pressure management during roughly the first 24–72 hours after reperfusion treatment**, not long-term hypertension treatment after discharge. Most studies enrolled people whose artery had been successfully reopened by mechanical thrombectomy and whose systolic pressure remained elevated.

### Overall interpretation

A **uniform, aggressively low systolic target—especially below 120–140 mm Hg—has generally not improved recovery after successful thrombectomy**. In two major trials, it worsened functional outcomes, without meaningfully reducing symptomatic brain hemorrhage.

The potential benefit of lowering pressure is theoretically a reduction in reperfusion-related edema or bleeding. The main potential harm is that injured brain tissue, residual penumbra, collateral circulation, or incompletely reperfused microvasculature may still depend on systemic pressure. A rapid reduction can therefore reduce cerebral perfusion despite successful reopening of the large artery.

A newer study suggests that the answer may be **individualized rather than simply “lower is better”**: a target based on how complete the reperfusion was produced better outcomes than a single permissive upper limit. That strategy included maintaining a higher range after incomplete reperfusion and lowering more after near-complete or complete reperfusion.

## Individual trial findings

### 1. ENCHANTED: primarily intravenous thrombolysis

This international trial randomized 2,196 thrombolysis-eligible patients with systolic pressure at least 150 mm Hg to:

* Intensive: target 130–140 mm Hg within one hour
* Standard: target below 180 mm Hg
* Treatment continued for 72 hours

There was **no improvement in 90-day disability** with intensive treatment: ordinal modified Rankin Scale odds ratio 1.01, 95% CI 0.87–1.17.

There was a potential bleeding benefit: any intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 14.8% with intensive control versus 18.7% with standard control, OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.94. Serious adverse events were not significantly different.

Important limitation: randomization occurred around the time of thrombolysis, not specifically after angiographically confirmed reopening of the artery. The achieved difference in mean systolic pressure was also relatively small—about 144 versus 150 mm Hg. ([PubMed][1])

**Interpretation:** Moderate lowering during alteplase treatment reduced imaging-detected hemorrhage but did not translate into better functional recovery.

### 2. BP-TARGET: intensive lowering after successful thrombectomy

This French trial randomized 324 patients after successful endovascular reperfusion to:

* Intensive: 100–129 mm Hg
* Standard: 130–185 mm Hg
* Maintained for 24 hours

Radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage occurred in 42% versus 43%, adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.60–1.51. Thus, the intensive target provided **no detectable hemorrhage benefit**.

Hypotensive events occurred in 8% versus 3%, although the difference was not statistically significant. First-week mortality was 7% versus 4%. The study was not powered to establish modest differences in clinical recovery or mortality. ([PubMed][2])

**Interpretation:** A target of 100–129 mm Hg did not prevent radiographic hemorrhage and produced more numerical hypotension.

### 3. ENCHANTED2/MT: very intensive lowering after successful thrombectomy

This trial enrolled 821 patients with persistent systolic pressure at least 140 mm Hg following successful thrombectomy. Participants were assigned to:

* Very intensive: below 120 mm Hg
* Standard: 140–180 mm Hg
* Maintained for 72 hours

The trial was stopped early because of efficacy and safety concerns. Intensive treatment produced:

* Worse overall 90-day functional outcome: common OR for poorer outcome 1.37, 95% CI 1.07–1.76
* More early neurological deterioration: common OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.18–1.97
* More major disability at 90 days: OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.47–2.93

Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, serious adverse events, and mortality were not significantly reduced. ([ScienceDirect][3])

**Interpretation:** Lowering systolic pressure below 120 mm Hg after thrombectomy was harmful and should not be considered equivalent to moderately controlled pressure.

### 4. BEST-II: phase 2 target-finding study

BEST-II randomized 120 patients after successful thrombectomy to targets of:

* Below 140 mm Hg
* Below 160 mm Hg
* At or below 180 mm Hg

The primary outcomes were infarct volume and a utility-weighted 90-day disability score. The study did not definitively establish either benefit or harm, partly because it was a small phase 2 futility-design study.

However, mean utility-weighted outcomes were numerically less favorable in both lower-target groups, and the estimated probability that a future large superiority trial would succeed was only:

* 25% for below 140 mm Hg
* 14% for below 160 mm Hg

Symptomatic hemorrhage was uncommon and similar across groups. ([PubMed][4])

**Interpretation:** BEST-II did not prove harm, but it provided little indication that routine targets below 140 or 160 mm Hg would improve recovery.

### 5. OPTIMAL-BP: intensive versus conventional control

This South Korean trial enrolled 306 successfully reperfused thrombectomy patients with systolic pressure at least 140 mm Hg and compared:

* Intensive: below 140 mm Hg
* Conventional: 140–180 mm Hg
* Maintained for 24 hours

The trial was stopped early because of safety concerns. Functional independence at three months occurred in:

* 39.4% with intensive treatment
* 54.4% with conventional treatment

The absolute difference was −15.1 percentage points, 95% CI −26.2 to −3.9; adjusted OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.96.

There was no significant reduction in symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage: 9.0% versus 8.1%. Stroke-related death was 7.7% versus 5.4%, also not statistically different. ([JAMA Network][5])

A 2026 follow-up found that the unfavorable functional signal persisted at one year: functional independence was 40.5% versus 52.7%, adjusted OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.34–1.00, with no significant mortality difference. The per-protocol analysis was statistically significant. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** A uniform target below 140 mm Hg reduced the probability of functional independence, without reducing symptomatic hemorrhage. The effect appeared durable.

### 6. DETECT: small feasibility trial

DETECT randomized 30 successfully reperfused thrombectomy patients to:

* Intensive: below 140 mm Hg
* Standard: below 180 mm Hg
* Maintained for 48 hours

The trial demonstrated difficulty maintaining a clear separation between groups: mean systolic pressures were approximately 131 versus 139 mm Hg. No significant differences were found in the prespecified exploratory neurological, hemorrhage, or functional outcomes, but the sample was far too small to determine clinical benefit or harm. ([PubMed][7])

The publication also contains a separate meta-analysis; the findings described here are solely from its randomized primary-trial component.

### 7. IDENTIFY: below 130 versus below 180 mm Hg

IDENTIFY randomized 383 patients with successful anterior-circulation reperfusion after endovascular treatment to:

* Intensive: below 130 mm Hg
* Standard: below 180 mm Hg
* Maintained until 24 hours after treatment

Unfavorable 90-day outcome—modified Rankin Scale 3–6—occurred in:

* 71.0% with intensive management
* 67.5% with standard management

The risk ratio was 1.05, 95% CI 0.92–1.20. There were no significant differences in symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, malignant brain edema, or 90-day mortality. The trial was terminated after a neutral interim analysis and the publication of other trials. ([ScienceDirect][8])

**Interpretation:** Lowering below 130 mm Hg provided no clinical benefit and did not clearly reduce major complications.

### 8. HOPE: reperfusion-guided personalized targets

The 2026 HOPE trial is important because it did **not** test one uniformly low target. It randomized 440 patients after successful anterior-circulation endovascular treatment to:

* If reperfusion was incomplete, mTICI 2b: 140–160 mm Hg
* If reperfusion was near-complete or complete, mTICI 2c/3: 100–140 mm Hg
* Control: below 180 mm Hg regardless of reperfusion grade
* Management continued for 72 hours, using antihypertensives or vasopressors as needed

Functional independence at 90 days occurred in:

* 60.0% with reperfusion-guided management
* 47.1% with standard management

The absolute difference was 13.3 percentage points, 95% CI 4.1–22.6; OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.17–2.50.

Hemorrhagic transformation was lower, 22.3% versus 31.6%, OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41–0.95. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage—3.5% versus 3.9%—and mortality—15.4% versus 15.6%—were similar. Serious adverse events were not significantly different. ([JAMA Network][9])

**Interpretation:** This study suggests that maintaining enough pressure when reperfusion is incomplete, while allowing a lower range after complete reperfusion, may be more effective than either permissive control or indiscriminate intensive lowering. It does not establish that every patient should be treated below 140 mm Hg.

## Practical meaning of the evidence

**Possible benefits of more active management**

* Lower radiographic intracranial hemorrhage during alteplase treatment in ENCHANTED.
* Lower hemorrhagic transformation and better functional recovery with the individualized HOPE strategy.
* Avoidance of very high pressure and large pressure fluctuations may still be important, although these trials were primarily comparisons of assigned targets.

**Risks of uniformly intensive lowering**

* Worse disability and early neurological deterioration when targeting below 120 mm Hg.
* Lower functional independence when targeting below 140 mm Hg in OPTIMAL-BP.
* A numerical increase in hypotensive episodes in BP-TARGET.
* No consistent reduction in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or mortality after thrombectomy.
* Potential underperfusion of tissue supplied by residual collaterals, incomplete reperfusion, or injured microcirculation.

The evidence therefore distinguishes **avoiding uncontrolled hypertension** from **actively forcing systolic pressure to very low levels**. It also suggests that the completeness of reperfusion, baseline pressure, arterial stenosis, collateral circulation, infarct size, cardiac status, and neurological changes may all matter.

Do not alter acute or chronic antihypertensive treatment based solely on these averages. Acute post-stroke pressure targets need to be directed by the treating stroke/neurocritical-care team; long-term secondary-prevention blood-pressure goals are a separate clinical question.
