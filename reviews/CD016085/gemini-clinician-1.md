For patients who have undergone successful reperfusion therapy—particularly endovascular thrombectomy (EVT)—recent primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have fundamentally shifted how we view post-procedure blood pressure (BP) management.

The consensus from recent high-quality trials is clear: **intensive BP lowering after successful reperfusion offers no significant clinical benefits and carries a substantial risk of worsening functional outcomes.**

Here is the breakdown of the benefits and risks based entirely on recent primary literature.

## The Lack of Benefit

Historically, intensive BP reduction was hypothesized to prevent reperfusion injury and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) after a blocked artery was opened. However, primary RCTs have not supported this:

* **No Reduction in Hemorrhage:** The BP-TARGET trial evaluated patients after successful EVT, comparing an intensive systolic blood pressure (SBP) target (100–129 mm Hg) to standard care (130–185 mm Hg). Intensive lowering failed to reduce the rates of radiographic intraparenchymal hemorrhage at 24–36 hours (Mazighi et al., 2021).
* **Futility:** The BEST-II phase 2 trial tested intensive targets (<140 and <160 mm Hg) against a standard guideline target (≤180 mm Hg). The investigators concluded there was an exceptionally low probability that intensive lowering would offer any clinical benefit in future, larger-scale efficacy trials (Mistry et al., 2023).

## The Risks of Intensive Lowering

Recent trials demonstrate that overly aggressive BP control actively harms patients, likely by compromising collateral blood flow to the vulnerable ischemic penumbra where cerebral autoregulation is impaired.

* **Worse Functional Recovery:** The OPTIMAL-BP trial (targeting SBP <140 vs 140–180 mm Hg) was stopped early due to safety concerns. Patients in the intensive group were significantly less likely to achieve functional independence at 3 months (Nam et al., 2023).
* **Increased Neurological Deterioration:** The ENCHANTED2/MT trial (targeting SBP <120 vs 140–180 mm Hg) was also terminated early. Intensive BP control led to higher rates of early neurological deterioration and nearly doubled the odds of major disability at 90 days (Yang et al., 2022).
* **Potential for Larger Infarcts:** Data from BEST-II indicated a trend toward larger final infarct volumes in patients subjected to intensive BP lowering, reinforcing the physiological risk of hypoperfusion (Mistry et al., 2023).

---

## Summary of Key Primary Trials

| Trial | Intensive SBP Target | Standard SBP Target | Primary Conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **BP-TARGET** | 100–129 mm Hg | 130–185 mm Hg | No reduction in hemorrhage rates; no clinical benefit. |
| **ENCHANTED2/MT** | <120 mm Hg | 140–180 mm Hg | **Terminated early:** Intensive lowering worsened major disability. |
| **OPTIMAL-BP** | <140 mm Hg | 140–180 mm Hg | **Terminated early:** Intensive lowering reduced functional independence. |
| **BEST-II** | <140 or <160 mm Hg | ≤180 mm Hg | Highly unlikely to provide benefit; potential for larger infarcts. |

Standard or permissive blood pressure management (often targeting SBP between 140–180 mm Hg or simply ≤180 mm Hg, per standard guidelines) is currently the safer, evidence-based approach following successful reperfusion.
