## Scope

I treated evidence as **directly applicable** when all participants had hypertension or the primary report presented an identifiable hypertension/baseline-BP subgroup. I separately included large cardiovascular-outcome trials when hypertension was common but no hypertension-specific randomized estimate was reported.

This was a targeted search for primary randomized studies and primary secondary analyses available through **July 18, 2026**. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, opinions, editorials, and comments.

## Overall conclusion

Weight-loss medicines can improve **weight, blood pressure, metabolic risk factors, and sometimes antihypertensive-medication requirements** in people with hypertension. However:

* Most hypertension-specific studies were short and assessed **surrogate outcomes**, principally weight and blood pressure.
* I did not identify a hypertension-only trial adequately powered for myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular death, or all-cause mortality.
* The strongest evidence for a patient-important cardiovascular benefit is from **SELECT**, in which semaglutide reduced major cardiovascular events. SELECT required established cardiovascular disease and did not provide a hypertension-specific randomized result, so it should not automatically be generalized to every person with hypertension.
* Historical sibutramine trials demonstrate that weight loss does not necessarily mean better cardiovascular health: sibutramine reduced weight but increased cardiovascular events in SCOUT.

## Findings by medicine

### Semaglutide

In a post hoc STEP 6 analysis, semaglutide produced substantial weight loss across baseline subgroups, including participants with hypertension. This report did not establish hypertension-specific effects on cardiovascular events or other major health outcomes. ([PubMed][1])

SELECT provides the strongest hard-outcome evidence. Among 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity, established cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes, semaglutide reduced cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke from 8.0% to 6.5% over a mean 39.8 months, HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.72–0.90). Treatment discontinuation because of adverse events was more frequent, 16.6% versus 8.2%. Because SELECT was not a hypertension-specific trial, this is supportive but indirect evidence for the broader hypertensive population. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

### Tirzepatide

A primary post hoc analysis of SURMOUNT-1 found a placebo-adjusted reduction of **6.8 mm Hg systolic and 4.2 mm Hg diastolic** at 72 weeks. Normal BP at week 72 occurred in 58.0% with tirzepatide versus 35.2% with placebo. Effects were broadly consistent across baseline BP and hypertension categories; mediation analysis attributed approximately 68%–71% of the BP effect to weight loss. Low-BP adverse events were uncommon but more frequent with tirzepatide. The trial was not limited to people with hypertension. ([PubMed][3])

### Cagrilintide–semaglutide, or CagriSema

In the REDEFINE 1 BP analysis, mean changes at 68 weeks were:

* Systolic BP: −10.9 mm Hg with CagriSema versus −2.8 mm Hg with placebo.
* Diastolic BP: −5.4 versus −1.7 mm Hg.
* BP target attainment: 63.0% versus 32.0%.
* Among participants using antihypertensives, 39.6% versus 18.8% decreased or stopped medication.

Among 167 participants with resistant hypertension, 42.0% versus 29.3% attained the BP target, but the estimate was imprecise, OR 1.7 (95% CI 0.7–4.4), and therefore did not rule out no difference. These were secondary and post hoc analyses rather than a dedicated hypertension trial. ([PubMed][4])

### Phentermine–topiramate and topiramate

In the CONQUER hypertension analysis, phentermine–topiramate produced greater weight loss than placebo. Among participants with hypertension, losing at least 5% of baseline weight was associated with systolic reductions of approximately 7.5–11.8 mm Hg. Because the reported BP comparison was stratified by achieved weight loss, it is less straightforward as a randomized treatment-effect estimate. ([PubMed][5])

A subsequent eight-week ambulatory-BP trial enrolled 565 adults, 67.6% of whom had diagnosed or treated hypertension. Twenty-four-hour systolic BP changed by −0.1 mm Hg with placebo, +1.4 mm Hg with phentermine alone, and −3.3 mm Hg with phentermine–topiramate. The treatment difference for the combination versus placebo was −3.2 mm Hg (95% CI −5.48 to −0.93). Entry BP had to be no higher than 140/90 mm Hg, so this principally addresses controlled hypertension. ([PubMed][6])

Topiramate monotherapy was evaluated in 531 people with obesity and essential hypertension. Weight decreased by 5.9%–6.5% with topiramate versus 1.9% with placebo; diastolic BP decreased by 5.5–6.3 versus 2.1 mm Hg. The trial was ended early by the sponsor during development of a new formulation, limiting confidence in longer-term outcomes. ([PubMed][7])

### Orlistat

Several relatively small trials directly enrolled people with hypertension:

* In a 12-week trial of 204 participants, orlistat plus diet produced greater weight loss than diet alone, −3.7 versus −2.0 kg. Diastolic BP fell by 11.4 versus 5.2 mm Hg, whereas the between-group systolic difference was not statistically significant. Glucose and total cholesterol also improved more with orlistat. ([PubMed][8])
* In a six-month trial, the hypertension stratum included 614 participants. Weight decreased by 6.2% with orlistat versus 1.9% with placebo. Gastrointestinal adverse events were more frequent. The abstract does not provide a clear randomized hypertension-stratum BP effect. ([PubMed][9])
* A 12-month head-to-head trial of 113 randomized hypertensive participants found weight reduction with both orlistat and sibutramine, but significant BP and broader lipid improvements were reported only in the orlistat group. The lack of a placebo arm and reliance on within-group significance limit interpretation. ([PubMed][10])

Thus, orlistat probably provides modest additional weight and BP benefit, particularly for diastolic BP, but direct evidence for preventing cardiovascular events in people with hypertension is absent.

### Naltrexone–bupropion

The LIGHT cardiovascular trial randomized 8,910 high-risk participants; 92% were taking antihypertensive medication. At the planned 50% interim analysis, the MACE HR was 0.88, with a very wide adjusted 99.7% CI of 0.57–1.34. The trial was terminated early after inappropriate public release of confidential interim results. Consequently, it cannot establish cardiovascular benefit or provide definitive long-term cardiovascular-safety evidence. Gastrointestinal and central nervous system adverse effects were more frequent with treatment. ([JAMA Network][11])

### Sibutramine: important evidence of harm

Dedicated hypertension trials consistently found greater weight loss but little BP benefit and sometimes adverse hemodynamic effects:

* In 127 participants, weight loss was greater with sibutramine, but the diastolic BP reduction was numerically smaller than with placebo. ([PubMed][12])
* In a 52-week trial of 220 people whose hypertension was controlled with an ACE inhibitor, sibutramine produced 4.5 kg versus 0.4 kg weight loss but approximately 3 mm Hg higher systolic and diastolic BP and a 5.7-beat/min pulse increase relative to baseline. ([PubMed][13])
* In 86 hypertensive participants, sibutramine reduced weight and left-ventricular mass index, but did not improve BP and increased heart rate. ([PubMed][14])
* In the HOS trial, sibutramine improved weight but significantly increased ambulatory diastolic BP relative to placebo. ([PubMed][15])

Most importantly, SCOUT randomized 9,804 high-risk participants after a sibutramine lead-in. The primary cardiovascular outcome occurred in 11.4% with sibutramine versus 10.0% with placebo, HR 1.16 (95% CI 1.03–1.31). Nonfatal myocardial infarction and nonfatal stroke were both increased, while cardiovascular and all-cause mortality were not significantly different. ([New England Journal of Medicine][16])

### Lorcaserin: neutral cardiovascular outcome, but not hypertension-specific

CAMELLIA–TIMI 61 randomized 12,000 high-risk adults. Lorcaserin increased the probability of at least 5% weight loss and produced small improvements in BP and other risk factors. It was noninferior for three-component MACE, HR 0.99 (95% CI 0.85–1.14), but did not reduce the extended cardiovascular outcome, HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.87–1.07). This was a mixed high-risk population rather than a hypertension-specific analysis. ([New England Journal of Medicine][17])

## Evidence-synthesis interpretation

For a review focused specifically on **people with hypertension**, the evidence supports three different conclusions:

1. **Weight and BP control:** Tirzepatide, CagriSema, phentermine–topiramate, topiramate, and orlistat generally improve weight and BP, although effect sizes, study designs, and directness differ.
2. **Cardiovascular event prevention:** Semaglutide reduces MACE in people with established cardiovascular disease and overweight/obesity, but a hypertension-specific treatment effect has not been established.
3. **Safety cannot be inferred from weight loss:** Sibutramine caused weight loss while increasing cardiovascular events. Naltrexone–bupropion still lacks a completed definitive cardiovascular-outcome trial.
