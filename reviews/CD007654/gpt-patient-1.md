## Bottom line

**Some weight-loss medicines improve blood pressure and cardiovascular health, but the evidence differs substantially by drug and patient population.**

* **Semaglutide and tirzepatide** produce the largest weight losses and generally lower blood pressure. Semaglutide has randomized evidence of fewer major cardiovascular events in people who already had cardiovascular disease. Tirzepatide has randomized evidence of fewer heart-failure events in people with obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.
* **Liraglutide, orlistat, and phentermine/topiramate** can produce smaller blood-pressure improvements, principally alongside weight loss.
* **Phentermine alone and naltrexone/bupropion require particular caution when blood pressure is a concern:** trials found increases or transient increases in blood pressure or heart rate.
* Most trials excluded people with **uncontrolled hypertension** or required stable antihypertensive treatment. Results therefore apply best to people whose pressure is reasonably controlled.

Importantly, lowering blood pressure in an obesity trial does not automatically prove that a medication prevents heart attacks or strokes.

## What the individual studies found

### Semaglutide

In **STEP 1**, 1,961 adults with overweight or obesity but without diabetes received semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo for 68 weeks. Mean weight change was −14.9% versus −2.4%; the placebo-adjusted systolic blood-pressure reduction was approximately **5.1 mm Hg**. Gastrointestinal adverse effects were common, and 4.5% discontinued semaglutide because of gastrointestinal events versus 0.8% with placebo. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

The more clinically important evidence comes from **SELECT**, involving 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity, established cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes. Over approximately 40 months, cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke occurred in **6.5% with semaglutide versus 8.0% with placebo**, hazard ratio 0.80. This corresponds to an absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points and a trial-level number needed to treat of about 67 over the follow-up period. However, SELECT does **not** establish the same benefit in someone whose only cardiovascular risk factor is hypertension. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

### Tirzepatide

In **SURMOUNT-1**, 2,539 adults with obesity or overweight plus a complication received tirzepatide or placebo for 72 weeks. Mean weight losses were approximately **15.0%, 19.5%, and 20.9%** with the three tirzepatide doses, versus 3.1% with placebo. ([PubMed][3])

An original analysis of SURMOUNT-1 data found placebo-adjusted reductions of **6.8 mm Hg systolic and 4.2 mm Hg diastolic** at 72 weeks. Normal blood pressure was recorded in 58.0% of tirzepatide-treated participants versus 35.2% receiving placebo. Statistical mediation analysis suggested that most of the pressure reduction was explained by weight loss. Low-blood-pressure adverse events were uncommon but more frequent with tirzepatide. ([PubMed][4])

A prospective ambulatory-monitoring substudy found placebo-adjusted reductions in 24-hour systolic pressure of **7.4–10.6 mm Hg**, depending on dose. Heart rate increased approximately 2–5 beats per minute. Participants had baseline blood pressure below 140/90 and stable antihypertensive therapy, so this was not a trial of uncontrolled hypertension. ([PMC][5])

In **SUMMIT**, 731 patients with obesity and symptomatic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction received tirzepatide or placebo. Cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure occurred in **9.9% versus 15.3%**, hazard ratio 0.62, and health-status scores improved. This is strong evidence for patients with this particular type of heart failure, rather than for hypertension alone. ([PubMed][6])

### Liraglutide

In the **SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes** trial, 3,731 adults without diabetes—many qualifying because of hypertension or dyslipidemia—received liraglutide 3 mg or placebo. At 56 weeks, average weight loss was **8.4 kg versus 2.8 kg**. Systolic pressure decreased by approximately 4.2 versus 1.5 mm Hg, a modest placebo-adjusted advantage of about 2.8 mm Hg. Pulse increased modestly. ([New England Journal of Medicine][7])

Unlike semaglutide in SELECT, this weight-management trial was not designed to demonstrate prevention of heart attacks or strokes.

### Orlistat

A trial specifically enrolling **204 adults with hypertension and overweight or obesity** compared orlistat plus diet with diet alone for 12 weeks. Weight loss was 3.7 versus 2.0 kg. Diastolic pressure fell by 11.4 versus 5.2 mm Hg, a statistically significant difference; systolic pressure fell in both groups, but the between-group difference was not statistically significant. Glucose and total cholesterol also improved more with orlistat. ([PubMed][8])

Thus, orlistat can improve some risk factors, although the weight loss and evidence for prevention of cardiovascular events are substantially less impressive than with newer incretin-based medications.

### Phentermine/topiramate and phentermine alone

A 2024 randomized ambulatory blood-pressure trial included 565 adults; about two-thirds had treated hypertension. At eight weeks, 24-hour systolic pressure changed by:

* **−3.3 mm Hg with phentermine/topiramate**
* −0.1 mm Hg with placebo
* **+1.4 mm Hg with phentermine alone**

The combination’s placebo-adjusted difference was −3.2 mm Hg. Heart rate increased by about 2.6 beats per minute with the combination and 6.2 beats per minute with phentermine alone. Anyone with screening pressure above 140/90 was excluded. ([PubMed][9])

This suggests that the combination may lower pressure as weight falls, while **phentermine by itself can increase systolic pressure and pulse**, at least initially. These findings should not be extrapolated to uncontrolled hypertension.

### Naltrexone/bupropion

In **COR-I**, naltrexone/bupropion produced approximately 5–6% weight loss versus 1.3% with placebo. However, systolic and diastolic pressure initially increased by approximately 1.5 mm Hg before moving back toward or slightly below baseline. It therefore did not show the consistent pressure-lowering pattern seen with semaglutide or tirzepatide. ([PubMed][10])

The **LIGHT** cardiovascular trial enrolled 8,910 higher-risk participants but was terminated prematurely after inappropriate public release of interim results. Consequently, it could not establish the prespecified cardiovascular-safety conclusion, and it provides no reliable evidence that naltrexone/bupropion prevents cardiovascular events. ([PubMed][11])

## Practical interpretation for someone with hypertension

Among the studied options, **semaglutide or tirzepatide generally have the most favorable combination of weight loss and blood-pressure improvement**. Selection can be influenced by accompanying disease:

* Established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease: semaglutide has direct major-event evidence.
* Obesity-related HFpEF: tirzepatide has direct heart-failure-outcome evidence.
* Hypertension without established cardiovascular disease or heart failure: both lower pressure, but prevention of future heart attacks or strokes has not been proved specifically for this lower-risk group.

As weight and pressure decline, an existing antihypertensive regimen may occasionally need adjustment. Home readings are particularly useful during dose escalation and after substantial weight loss. Dizziness, faintness, orthostatic symptoms, persistent vomiting, or very low readings warrant prompt review. Conversely, medications containing sympathomimetic or bupropion components merit greater caution when pressure is poorly controlled.
