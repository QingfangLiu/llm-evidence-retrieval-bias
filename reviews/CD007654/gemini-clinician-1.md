Managing both obesity and hypertension simultaneously is one of the most common—and challenging—tasks in primary and cardiovascular care. The short answer to your question is a definitive yes: recent primary clinical trials demonstrate that modern anti-obesity medications (specifically GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists) not only lower blood pressure but also significantly reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events.

Here is a breakdown of how these medications impact the health of patients with, or at risk for, hypertension, based strictly on primary trial data.

## Blood Pressure Reduction

The major phase 3 trials for both semaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) and tirzepatide (a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist) tracked blood pressure as a key cardiometabolic endpoint.

* **Tirzepatide (SURMOUNT-1):** In adults with obesity (without diabetes), tirzepatide 15 mg once weekly led to a mean body weight reduction of over 20% at 72 weeks. A stratified analysis of this primary trial showed a net reduction of **6.8 mm Hg in systolic** and **4.2 mm Hg in diastolic** blood pressure. Notably, mediation analyses indicated that the physical weight loss itself explained roughly 68% of the systolic reduction, while the remainder is likely due to the drug's direct effects on endothelial function, systemic inflammation, and natriuresis (sodium excretion).
* **Semaglutide (STEP 1):** In a similar population, semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly resulted in a ~15% body weight reduction at 68 weeks, accompanied by a mean systolic blood pressure drop of **5.1 mm Hg**.

While these reductions might seem modest compared to dedicated antihypertensive agents, they shift the population distribution curve significantly. In the SURMOUNT-1 trial, patients on tirzepatide were vastly more likely to revert to normotensive ranges than those on placebo.

## Hard Cardiovascular Outcomes

Improving surrogate markers like blood pressure is helpful, but as a clinician, you want to know if this translates to fewer heart attacks and strokes.

The definitive evidence for this comes from the **SELECT trial** (published in late 2023). This event-driven trial specifically enrolled patients over 45 who had overweight or obesity and *pre-existing cardiovascular disease* (but no history of diabetes). The vast majority of this cohort was hypertensive.

Patients receiving semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly experienced a **20% relative risk reduction** in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which is a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke. This proved that pharmacological weight management provides independent, disease-modifying cardiovascular benefits beyond just lowering the numbers on a blood pressure cuff.
