Yes, current evidence from primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) shows that modern weight-loss medications—specifically GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists—meaningfully improve the cardiovascular health of patients with overweight or obesity, including direct reductions in blood pressure.

Here is a breakdown of the primary data on how these medications impact hypertension and overall cardiovascular health, sourced exclusively from individual clinical trials and their exploratory analyses.

## Blood Pressure Reductions

* **Tirzepatide:** In a stratified analysis of the SURMOUNT-1 RCT, patients taking tirzepatide experienced a rapid decline in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure over the first 24 weeks. By 72 weeks, patients saw a significant net reduction of **6.8 mm Hg in systolic blood pressure** and **4.2 mm Hg in diastolic pressure** compared to placebo. A mediation analysis indicated that approximately 70% of this blood pressure reduction was directly explained by the weight loss itself (Krumholz et al., 2024).
* **Semaglutide:** The foundational STEP 1 trial demonstrated that 2.4 mg of once-weekly semaglutide produced clinically relevant reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure alongside substantial weight loss (Wilding et al., 2021). Further exploratory analyses of the STEP 1 and 4 trials corroborated these cardiometabolic improvements and noted a **net reduction in the use of antihypertensive medications** in the treatment groups compared to placebo (Kosiborod et al., 2022).

## Major Cardiovascular Outcomes

Beyond lowering surrogate markers like blood pressure, there is primary evidence that these medications improve hard clinical outcomes in high-risk patient populations.

* **The SELECT Trial:** This landmark event-driven trial evaluated semaglutide in patients with preexisting cardiovascular disease (such as a prior myocardial infarction or stroke) and overweight or obesity, explicitly *excluding* patients with diabetes. It found that weekly semaglutide **reduced the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events** (a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke) by 20% compared to the placebo group (Lincoff et al., 2023).
