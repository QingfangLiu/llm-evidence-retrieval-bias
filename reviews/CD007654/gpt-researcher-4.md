## Bottom line

The primary-study evidence suggests that **some weight-loss medicines improve blood-pressure control in people with hypertension**, especially orlistat, phentermine/topiramate, tirzepatide, semaglutide, and cagrilintide/semaglutide. However, BP lowering is not a class effect: sibutramine produced weight loss while offsetting BP benefits and increasing heart rate.

Evidence for **patient-important health outcomes specifically in hypertension-selected populations** remains limited. Semaglutide reduced major cardiovascular events in SELECT, and semaglutide and tirzepatide improved outcomes in obesity-related HFpEF, but these trials were not restricted to people with hypertension and did not establish the clinical-outcome effect specifically within a hypertension subgroup. ([PubMed][1])

All evidence reports cited below are individual primary studies or analyses of individual randomized trials. This is a focused evidence map rather than a fully database-deduplicated systematic review.

## Studies directly enrolling people with hypertension

### Orlistat

**Bakris et al., 2002 — inadequately controlled hypertension**

In 544 adults with obesity and treated but inadequately controlled hypertension, one year of orlistat plus a calorie-deficit diet produced greater weight loss than placebo plus diet: −5.4 versus −2.7 kg. DBP decreased by −11.4 versus −9.2 mm Hg, and 67% versus 53% achieved the study’s DBP goal. Lipid measures also improved more with orlistat. This is among the most directly applicable trials because hypertension was an inclusion criterion and was not already optimally controlled. ([CNGBdb][2])

**Bloch et al., 2003 — hypertension-clinic pragmatic RCT**

Among 204 hypertensive adults with overweight or obesity, 12 weeks of orlistat plus diet produced greater weight loss than diet alone: −3.7 versus −2.0 kg. The additional SBP reduction was not statistically significant, but DBP fell by 11.4 versus 5.2 mm Hg. Fasting glucose and total cholesterol also improved more with orlistat. ([PubMed][3])

**Derosa et al., 2005 — orlistat versus sibutramine**

This 12-month active-comparator trial randomized 113 treated hypertensive adults to orlistat or sibutramine. Both groups lost weight, but significant SBP and DBP improvements were reported only in the orlistat group; lipid improvements also generally favored orlistat. There was no placebo group, limiting causal interpretation of the BP changes. ([PubMed][4])

### Sibutramine

These trials consistently show why weight loss itself cannot be assumed to confer BP or cardiovascular benefit.

**Hazenberg, 2000**

In 127 adults with stable hypertension, sibutramine produced more weight loss over 12 weeks than placebo, −4.4 versus −2.2 kg, but did not produce superior BP reduction. DBP fell by 4.0 mm Hg with sibutramine and 5.7 mm Hg with placebo. ([PubMed][5])

**McMahon et al., 2000**

In 224 adults whose hypertension was controlled with calcium-channel-blocker therapy, 52-week weight loss was −4.4 kg with sibutramine and −0.5 kg with placebo. Nevertheless, DBP increased 2.0 mm Hg with sibutramine versus falling 1.3 mm Hg with placebo, and pulse increased 4.9 versus 0 beats/min. Hypertension caused discontinuation in 5.3% versus 1.4%. ([JAMA Network][6])

**McMahon et al., 2002**

Among 220 adults whose hypertension was controlled with an ACE inhibitor, with or without a thiazide, sibutramine produced −4.5 versus −0.4 kg weight loss over 52 weeks. At study end, SBP and DBP were each approximately 3 mm Hg higher with sibutramine than placebo, and pulse changed by +5.7 versus −0.3 beats/min. ([Nature][7])

**Faria et al., 2002**

In 86 adults with obesity and hypertension followed for six months, sibutramine produced greater weight loss, 6.7% versus 2.5%, but BP did not change and heart rate increased. A reduction in left-ventricular-mass/height index was reported, although the trial was small and not powered for clinical events. ([PubMed][8])

**Scholze et al., 2007 — HOS**

The HOS trial randomized 171 adults with obesity-related hypertension receiving one of three antihypertensive combinations. Sibutramine caused greater weight loss but also increased 24-hour DBP relative to placebo. Its weight and metabolic effects were attenuated among participants receiving metoprolol/hydrochlorothiazide compared with ACE-inhibitor/calcium-channel-blocker regimens. ([PubMed][9])

### Phentermine/topiramate

**Davidson et al., 2013 — CONQUER hypertension analysis**

This analysis evaluated participants with hypertension or dyslipidemia in the 56-week CONQUER RCT. Phentermine/topiramate produced greater dose-related weight loss than placebo. Within the hypertension subgroup, participants achieving at least 5% weight loss had SBP reductions of approximately 7.5–11.8 mm Hg. Because the analysis stratified results partly by achieved weight loss, these figures are not equivalent to the randomized treatment contrast. ([PubMed][10])

**Bays et al., 2024 — ambulatory BP RCT**

Of 565 randomized adults, 67.6% had diagnosed or treated hypertension. At eight weeks, mean 24-hour SBP changed by −0.1 mm Hg with placebo, +1.4 mm Hg with phentermine alone, and −3.3 mm Hg with phentermine/topiramate. The placebo-adjusted effect of the combination was −3.2 mm Hg. Importantly, screening BP above 140/90 mm Hg was excluded, so this study does not establish safety or efficacy in uncontrolled hypertension. ([PubMed][11])

## Newer incretin-based medicines: hypertension subgroups and BP outcomes

**Semaglutide — STEP 1 and STEP 4 exploratory analyses**

Hypertension was one of the most prevalent baseline comorbidities in these trials. Semaglutide produced greater reductions in SBP and DBP than placebo and a net decrease in antihypertensive-medication use. In STEP 4, continuing semaglutide maintained the cardiometabolic improvements, whereas switching to placebo led to deterioration, supporting the need for continued treatment to maintain benefit. The report did not provide a standalone randomized estimate restricted to participants with hypertension. ([PubMed][12])

**Tirzepatide — SURMOUNT-1 hypertension-category analysis**

In this post hoc analysis of the 72-week SURMOUNT-1 RCT, the placebo-adjusted BP effects were −6.8 mm Hg for SBP and −4.2 mm Hg for DBP. Normal BP at week 72 was observed in 58.0% of tirzepatide recipients and 35.2% of placebo recipients. Effects were broadly consistent across baseline hypertension and BP categories; mediation analysis attributed approximately 68%–71% of the BP reduction to weight loss. ([PubMed][13])

**Tirzepatide — SURMOUNT-1 ambulatory-BP substudy**

This individual substudy used 24-hour ambulatory monitoring and confirmed reductions in daytime, nighttime, and overall ambulatory BP after 36 weeks. It strengthens the clinic-BP evidence but was a substudy of a mixed obesity population rather than a dedicated uncontrolled-hypertension trial. ([PubMed][14])

**Cagrilintide/semaglutide — REDEFINE 1 BP analysis**

In the 3,417-participant trial, 36.3% had investigator-identified hypertension. At 68 weeks, SBP/DBP changed by −10.9/−5.4 mm Hg with the combination versus −2.8/−1.7 mm Hg with placebo. Among participants using antihypertensives during the trial, 39.6% versus 18.8% reduced or stopped medication. In the small resistant-hypertension subgroup, BP-target attainment was numerically higher, 42.0% versus 29.3%, but the confidence interval was wide and included no effect. These were secondary and post hoc analyses. ([PubMed][15])

## Trials measuring cardiovascular or functional health outcomes

These studies offer more clinically meaningful outcomes, but their applicability is **indirect** because they were not trials confined to people with hypertension.

**Semaglutide — SELECT**

SELECT randomized 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease but no diabetes. Cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, or nonfatal stroke occurred in 6.5% with semaglutide and 8.0% with placebo, HR 0.80. Treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was more frequent with semaglutide, 16.6% versus 8.2%. This establishes cardiovascular benefit in a high-risk obesity population, but not a separate treatment effect for hypertensive participants. ([PubMed][1])

**Semaglutide — STEP-HFpEF**

Among 529 adults with symptomatic HFpEF and obesity, semaglutide improved the KCCQ clinical summary score by 7.8 points more than placebo, reduced body weight by an additional 10.7 percentage points, and increased six-minute walking distance by 20.3 m. Serious adverse events were less frequent with semaglutide. HFpEF populations commonly have substantial hypertensive disease burden, but hypertension was not the defining inclusion condition. ([PubMed][16])

**Tirzepatide — SUMMIT**

In 731 adults with HFpEF and obesity, cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure occurred in 9.9% with tirzepatide and 15.3% with placebo, HR 0.62. The effect was driven by fewer worsening-HF events; cardiovascular deaths were few and not reduced. KCCQ score improved by 6.9 points more than placebo. ([PubMed][17])

**Naltrexone/bupropion — LIGHT**

LIGHT randomized 8,910 adults with overweight or obesity and elevated cardiovascular risk. The trial was terminated prematurely after confidential interim results were publicly released. At the 50% interim analysis, the MACE HR was 0.88, but the planned noninferiority margin could not be evaluated; therefore, cardiovascular safety and benefit were not established. ([PubMed][18])

**Sibutramine — SCOUT**

In 9,804 randomized high-risk adults, sibutramine produced additional weight loss but less BP reduction than placebo. The primary cardiovascular outcome occurred in 11.4% versus 10.0%, HR 1.16, with significantly more nonfatal MI and nonfatal stroke. This is strong evidence that weight loss accompanied by adverse sympathetic effects may worsen cardiovascular health. ([New England Journal of Medicine][19])

**Lorcaserin — CAMELLIA–TIMI 61**

Among 12,000 high-risk adults, lorcaserin produced more weight loss and slightly better cardiovascular risk-factor values, including BP, but did not reduce major cardiovascular events: HR 0.99 for cardiovascular death, MI, or stroke. Thus, it demonstrated cardiovascular noninferiority rather than improved cardiovascular health. ([PubMed][20])

**Rimonabant — CRESCENDO**

This 18,695-participant cardiovascular-outcome trial was stopped prematurely because of psychiatric safety concerns. Cardiovascular death, MI, or stroke was not reduced, HR 0.97, while serious psychiatric adverse events and suicides were more frequent with rimonabant. ([PubMed][21])

## Interpretation for an evidence synthesis

The most defensible conclusions are:

1. **BP control probably improves with effective pharmacological weight loss, but not with every drug.** Direct hypertension trials support modest additional BP reductions with orlistat and phentermine/topiramate. Tirzepatide and cagrilintide/semaglutide show larger clinic-BP effects, although much of this evidence comes from secondary or post hoc analyses of mixed obesity populations.

2. **Clinical-outcome benefits are established for selected newer agents in particular high-risk populations, not for “weight-loss medicines” as a class.** Semaglutide reduced MACE in adults with established cardiovascular disease, and semaglutide and tirzepatide improved obesity-related HFpEF outcomes. ([PubMed][1])

3. **Direct evidence for preventing MI, stroke, kidney failure, or death specifically among people recruited because they have hypertension remains insufficient.** The hypertension-selected trials were generally small or moderate-sized and focused on weight, BP, metabolic markers, or cardiac structure rather than adjudicated clinical events.

4. **Higher or uncontrolled BP is underrepresented in some contemporary trials.** For example, the ambulatory phentermine/topiramate trial excluded screening BP above 140/90 mm Hg. Extrapolation to resistant or poorly controlled hypertension therefore requires caution. ([PubMed][11])
