## Bottom line

For people with overweight/obesity and hypertension, several weight-loss medicines **lower blood pressure in addition to producing weight loss**. The clearest blood-pressure reductions are seen with **tirzepatide and semaglutide**. However, evidence that these medicines prevent heart attacks, strokes, heart-failure events, or death depends heavily on the patient population:

* **Semaglutide** reduced major cardiovascular events in patients who already had cardiovascular disease.
* **Tirzepatide** reduced worsening heart-failure events in patients with obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.
* For someone with hypertension but **without established cardiovascular disease or heart failure**, studies primarily demonstrate lower blood pressure, weight, glucose and lipid risk factors—not yet fewer heart attacks, strokes or deaths.

I limited the evidence below to reports of individual randomized trials or analyses of individual randomized trials. I did not use systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee statements or editorials as evidence.

## Findings by medication

### Tirzepatide

The SURMOUNT-1 blood-pressure analysis found that after 72 weeks, tirzepatide produced a placebo-adjusted reduction of **6.8 mm Hg systolic and 4.2 mm Hg diastolic**. Normal blood pressure at week 72 was recorded in 58.0% of tirzepatide recipients versus 35.2% with placebo. Approximately 70% of the BP effect appeared to be mediated by weight loss. Low-BP adverse events were uncommon but more frequent with tirzepatide. ([PubMed][1])

A prospectively planned SURMOUNT-1 ambulatory-BP substudy provided stronger measurement evidence. At 36 weeks, placebo-adjusted 24-hour systolic reductions were:

* 5 mg: **−7.4 mm Hg**
* 10 mg: **−10.6 mm Hg**
* 15 mg: **−8.0 mm Hg**

The effect occurred during both daytime and nighttime. However, baseline 24-hour systolic BP averaged only 124.6 mm Hg, so most participants did not have markedly uncontrolled hypertension. Heart rate increased by approximately 2–5 beats/minute, depending on dose. ([PMC][2])

In SUMMIT, 731 patients with obesity and symptomatic HFpEF were randomized to tirzepatide or placebo. Cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure occurred in **9.9% versus 15.3%**, respectively, HR 0.62. The benefit was driven by fewer worsening-heart-failure events; cardiovascular death alone was not reduced and had wide confidence intervals. Therefore, this is compelling for obesity-related HFpEF but cannot be generalized to hypertension alone. ([PubMed][3])

### Semaglutide

An analysis of the STEP 1 randomized trial examined participants with controlled and uncontrolled hypertension. Among those with uncontrolled hypertension at baseline, systolic BP changed by **−4.67 mm Hg with semaglutide versus +1.59 mm Hg with placebo**; diastolic BP changed by −2.31 versus +1.43 mm Hg. Overall, 51.5% versus 38.2% reached a BP below 130/80 mm Hg. Participants receiving semaglutide were also more likely to reduce or discontinue an antihypertensive medication. In STEP 4, BP improvements deteriorated when semaglutide was withdrawn, suggesting that continued treatment was required to preserve the benefit. ([PMC][4])

SELECT provides the strongest evidence that a weight-loss medicine can improve hard cardiovascular outcomes. Among 17,604 adults with overweight/obesity, established cardiovascular disease and no diabetes, cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI or nonfatal stroke occurred in **6.5% with semaglutide versus 8.0% with placebo**, HR 0.80, over an average follow-up of approximately 40 months. Treatment discontinuation because of adverse events was more common with semaglutide, 16.6% versus 8.2%. SELECT did not enroll a general primary-prevention hypertension population: everyone already had cardiovascular disease. ([PubMed][5])

### Phentermine/topiramate and phentermine alone

In an analysis of the 56-week CONQUER trial, hypertensive participants who lost at least 5% of their body weight experienced systolic reductions of approximately **7.5–11.8 mm Hg**, with greater weight loss and risk-factor improvement in the phentermine/topiramate groups. Because the analysis related BP to achieved weight loss rather than simply comparing randomized groups, it provides less definitive treatment-effect evidence than an intention-to-treat comparison. ([PubMed][6])

A more recent 565-person ambulatory-BP trial directly compared phentermine/topiramate, phentermine alone and placebo. At eight weeks, 24-hour systolic BP changed by:

* Phentermine/topiramate: **−3.3 mm Hg**
* Placebo: **−0.1 mm Hg**
* Phentermine alone: **+1.4 mm Hg**

The combination was significantly better than placebo and phentermine alone. Importantly, people with screening BP above 140/90 mm Hg or unstable antihypertensive treatment were excluded. This suggests that the combination can lower BP in controlled hypertension, but phentermine alone should not be presumed to do so. ([PubMed][7])

### Orlistat

A small pragmatic trial specifically enrolled 204 patients with hypertension. After 12 weeks, orlistat plus a hypocaloric diet produced more weight loss than diet alone. Systolic BP declined by 15.3 versus 11.6 mm Hg, but the between-group difference was not significant. Diastolic BP declined by **11.4 versus 5.2 mm Hg**, a significant difference. The short follow-up and large BP improvement in the control group limit interpretation. ([PubMed][8])

In the 54-week UK Multimorbidity Study, 531 patients with obesity and cardiovascular risk were randomized to orlistat or placebo. Systolic BP declined by **6.0 versus 2.3 mm Hg**, and diastolic BP by **5.5 versus 3.1 mm Hg**. Orlistat also improved weight, waist circumference, glucose and LDL cholesterol. This trial was not restricted to people with hypertension. ([PubMed][9])

### Liraglutide

The 3,731-person SCALE trial included adults with obesity or overweight accompanied by hypertension or dyslipidemia. Liraglutide produced a mean weight loss of 8.4 kg versus 2.8 kg with placebo over 56 weeks. However, its published primary abstract did not provide a hypertension-specific cardiovascular-outcome analysis. ([DOI.org][10])

An earlier dose-ranging randomized trial found that liraglutide produced greater weight loss than placebo or orlistat and reported BP reductions at all liraglutide doses. These were intermediate risk-factor outcomes; the trial did not establish prevention of cardiovascular events. ([PubMed][11])

### Naltrexone/bupropion

COR-II illustrates that weight loss does not automatically translate into better blood pressure. Naltrexone/bupropion produced approximately **6.4% weight loss versus 1.2% with placebo** at 56 weeks, but mean BP remained near baseline and was slightly lower with placebo. At week 56, systolic BP changed by +0.6 mm Hg with naltrexone/bupropion versus −0.5 mm Hg with placebo; pulse increased by approximately one beat/minute. ([PubMed][12])

The LIGHT cardiovascular-outcomes trial enrolled 8,910 high-risk patients but was terminated early after confidential interim data were publicly released. At the 50% interim analysis, MACE occurred in 2.0% with naltrexone/bupropion and 2.3% with placebo, HR 0.88, but the confidence interval was too wide to meet the intended cardiovascular-safety criterion. Consequently, the trial could establish neither cardiovascular benefit nor adequately precise cardiovascular safety. ([PubMed][13])

## Clinical interpretation

For **hypertension without prior cardiovascular disease or HFpEF**, the most supportable conclusion is that tirzepatide and semaglutide generally lower BP by several mm Hg, and some patients may need less antihypertensive medication. That is likely beneficial, but it is not equivalent to trial proof of fewer strokes or heart attacks in this particular population.

For **established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease**, SELECT provides direct event-reduction evidence for semaglutide. For **obesity-related HFpEF**, SUMMIT provides direct evidence for tirzepatide.

Phentermine/topiramate and orlistat can improve BP, although their evidence is older or less extensive. Phentermine alone did not lower ambulatory BP in the direct comparative trial. Naltrexone/bupropion produced weight loss but no meaningful BP improvement relative to placebo, and definitive cardiovascular-outcome evidence remains incomplete.

A major limitation across these trials is that severe or unstable hypertension was frequently excluded and average baseline BP was often already controlled. During successful weight loss, home BP should be followed because antihypertensive doses may eventually need clinician-directed reduction, particularly if dizziness, orthostasis or low readings develop.
