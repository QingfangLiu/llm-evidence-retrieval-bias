## Bottom line

For adults with overweight/obesity and hypertension, several weight-loss medicines **lower blood pressure and improve metabolic risk factors**, particularly tirzepatide, semaglutide, phentermine/topiramate, liraglutide, and orlistat. The average additional systolic reduction is commonly in the range of roughly **3–10 mm Hg**, depending on the drug, population, measurement method, and amount of weight lost. ([Heart][1])

Evidence that these changes translate into fewer major clinical events is much narrower:

* **Semaglutide 2.4 mg** reduced myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death in people who already had atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
* **Tirzepatide** reduced worsening-heart-failure events in people with obesity-related HFpEF.
* Cardiovascular benefit has **not been established for otherwise uncomplicated hypertension** with most other weight-loss drugs.
* Centrally acting or sympathomimetic drugs require particular caution: phentermine alone increased ambulatory systolic pressure slightly in one trial; naltrexone/bupropion produced early BP increases and lacks a definitive completed cardiovascular-outcome trial; sibutramine increased cardiovascular events. ([PubMed][2])

## Evidence by medication

### Tirzepatide

**SURMOUNT-1** randomized 2,539 adults without diabetes to tirzepatide or placebo for 72 weeks. Tirzepatide produced approximately 15–21% weight loss versus 3.1% with placebo and improved prespecified cardiometabolic measures. About one-third had diagnosed hypertension. ([PubMed][3])

An original analysis of SURMOUNT-1 found a placebo-adjusted BP reduction of approximately **6.8 mm Hg systolic and 4.2 mm Hg diastolic**. At 72 weeks, 58.0% of tirzepatide recipients versus 35.2% of placebo recipients were in the normal BP category. Approximately 68–71% of the BP effect was statistically mediated by weight loss. Symptomatic low-BP events were uncommon but more frequent with tirzepatide. ([PubMed][4])

In a 600-participant ambulatory-monitoring substudy, placebo-adjusted 24-hour systolic BP fell by **7.4, 10.6, and 8.0 mm Hg** with the 5-, 10-, and 15-mg doses, respectively. Effects were similar regardless of hypertension diagnosis or antihypertensive use. ([PMC][5])

**Clinical outcomes:** In SUMMIT, 731 patients with obesity and HFpEF were randomized to tirzepatide or placebo. Cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure occurred in 9.9% versus 15.3%—hazard ratio 0.62. The difference was driven principally by fewer worsening-heart-failure events; cardiovascular mortality alone was not reduced. Symptoms and health status also improved. This applies to patients with established HFpEF, not hypertension alone. ([PubMed][6])

### Semaglutide 2.4 mg

In **STEP 1**, 1,961 adults without diabetes received semaglutide or placebo for 68 weeks. Mean weight change was −14.9% versus −2.4%, with greater improvements in cardiometabolic measures and physical functioning. ([PubMed][7])

In **STEP 4**, after a semaglutide run-in, continued treatment versus withdrawal produced an additional placebo-adjusted systolic BP improvement of **3.9 mm Hg** and improved physical-function scores. BP and metabolic improvements deteriorated when semaglutide was stopped. Original STEP analyses also found less need for antihypertensive therapy in semaglutide-treated participants. ([PubMed][8])

**Clinical outcomes:** SELECT randomized 17,604 adults with overweight/obesity, established cardiovascular disease, and no diabetes. Major cardiovascular events occurred in 6.5% with semaglutide versus 8.0% with placebo over approximately 40 months—hazard ratio 0.80, an absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points and an approximate number needed to treat of 67. Treatment discontinuation, primarily related to adverse effects, was more frequent with semaglutide. This is strong outcome evidence for secondary cardiovascular prevention, but it does not establish the same benefit in someone whose only cardiovascular condition is hypertension. ([PubMed][2])

### Phentermine/topiramate

**CONQUER** included 2,487 adults with at least two weight-related comorbidities, including hypertension. At 56 weeks, weight loss was approximately 7.8–9.8% with active treatment versus 1.2% with placebo. ([PubMed][9])

A newer 565-participant ambulatory-BP trial is particularly relevant: approximately two-thirds had hypertension. At eight weeks, 24-hour systolic BP changed by:

* Placebo: −0.1 mm Hg
* Phentermine 30 mg alone: **+1.4 mm Hg**
* Phentermine/topiramate: **−3.3 mm Hg**

The combination lowered systolic pressure by 3.2 mm Hg relative to placebo, although heart rate increased by about 2.6 beats/min. Participants with screening BP above 140/90 mm Hg were excluded, so these results do not establish safety in uncontrolled hypertension. ([PubMed][10])

There is no adequately powered randomized trial demonstrating fewer heart attacks, strokes, or deaths with phentermine/topiramate.

### Orlistat

One trial specifically enrolled **628 obese patients with hypertension**. At 56 weeks, weight loss was 8.0% with orlistat versus 4.0% with placebo. Among those with isolated systolic hypertension, systolic BP fell 9.4 versus 4.6 mm Hg; among those with diastolic hypertension, diastolic BP fell 7.7 versus 5.6 mm Hg. ([PubMed][11])

A separate pragmatic trial of 204 hypertensive adults found greater weight loss and a significantly greater diastolic reduction with orlistat plus diet than with diet alone, along with improvements in glucose and cholesterol. ([PubMed][12])

These studies support modest BP and metabolic benefits, but there is no definitive orlistat trial showing fewer major cardiovascular events.

### Liraglutide 3.0 mg

**SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes** randomized 3,731 adults without diabetes; hypertension or dyslipidemia qualified as an obesity-related comorbidity. Weight loss at 56 weeks was 8.4 kg with liraglutide versus 2.8 kg with placebo, accompanied by improved metabolic control. Gastrointestinal symptoms were common. ([PubMed][13])

The obesity-dose trial was not powered to determine whether liraglutide prevents cardiovascular events in patients with hypertension.

### Naltrexone/bupropion

In **COR-I**, which allowed participants with hypertension, weight loss was approximately 5–6% versus 1.3% with placebo. Mean systolic and diastolic BP initially increased by about 1.5 mm Hg before declining to approximately 1 mm Hg below baseline. Thus, weight loss did not produce the consistent early BP improvement seen with incretin drugs. ([PubMed][14])

The 8,910-participant **LIGHT** cardiovascular trial was terminated early after confidential interim results were publicly released. At the 50% interim analysis, the hazard ratio for major cardiovascular events was 0.88, but the confidence interval was too wide to establish the prespecified cardiovascular-safety margin. The investigators concluded that definitive cardiovascular safety remained uncertain. ([PubMed][15])

### Historical safety signals

The importance of evaluating the specific medication—not merely the amount of weight lost—is illustrated by **SCOUT**. In high-risk adults, sibutramine produced weight loss but increased the primary cardiovascular endpoint from 10.0% to 11.4%, including more nonfatal myocardial infarctions and strokes. BP also fell less than with placebo. ([New England Journal of Medicine][16])

In contrast, **CAMELLIA–TIMI 61** found that lorcaserin was cardiovascularly noninferior but not superior to placebo: major events occurred in 6.1% versus 6.2%, and the broader cardiovascular endpoint was not reduced. Thus modest weight loss and slightly improved BP did not automatically translate into fewer events. ([New England Journal of Medicine][17])

## Practical interpretation

The strongest evidence for a patient with hypertension and obesity favors medicines that produce substantial sustained weight loss without stimulating BP—particularly tirzepatide or semaglutide—provided there are no patient-specific contraindications. BP should be followed closely because antihypertensive requirements can decrease, and dizziness or hypotension can occur as weight and BP fall. ([PubMed][18])

A major limitation is that most weight-loss trials were designed around weight, BP, laboratory markers, or quality of life—not stroke, kidney failure, myocardial infarction, or death. SELECT and SUMMIT provide important clinical-outcome evidence, but only for patients with established cardiovascular disease or HFpEF, respectively.
