## Bottom line

**Quit-smoking treatment begun during psychiatric hospitalization can improve smoking abstinence, particularly when hospital counseling is combined with medication and continued support after discharge.** The most informative randomized trials found absolute improvements of approximately **5–8 percentage points in 6-month point-prevalence abstinence**, although quit rates remained low overall. ([PMC][1])

Results were not uniformly positive:

* Prochaska, Metse, and Brown found statistically significant 6-month abstinence benefits.
* Hickman found numerically higher quit rates but was a small feasibility trial.
* Stockings found a benefit at four months, but not six months.
* Evidence beyond six months was mixed: Prochaska found a persisting difference through 18 months, whereas Metse did not find a significant 12-month difference. ([PMC][1])
* Small medication-only, reduction-only, digital, and implementation studies provide promising but much less certain evidence.

I located **13 potentially relevant published primary reports through July 25, 2026**. These include randomized trials, one controlled longitudinal study, a nonrandomized implementation evaluation, and a single-arm pilot. I excluded reviews, guidelines, editorials, protocols, registry-only records, adolescent studies, outpatient-only interventions, and general-medical inpatient studies.

## Interpretation for an evidence synthesis

The intervention model with the most consistent evidence is:

> **Initiate motivational or behavioral counseling on the psychiatric ward, provide cessation pharmacotherapy, and actively maintain treatment for several months after discharge.**

Three reasonably sized randomized trials demonstrated statistically significant 6-month point-prevalence abstinence effects, but their absolute quit rates were only 9–16% in intervention groups. Point-prevalence abstinence also permits smoking outside the brief assessment window and should not be interpreted as continuous cessation. ([PMC][1])

The evidence is less convincing for:

* **Brief or short-lived programmes:** Stockings’ effect was present when the four-month intervention ended but not at six months.
* **Medication alone:** studies were small, comparisons were sometimes between doses or active treatments, and quit rates were often very low.
* **Smoking-reduction programmes:** these may reduce consumption but do not necessarily produce abstinence.
* **Digital and implementation approaches:** promising, but currently supported by pilot or nonrandomized data.

For a review focused strictly on cessation, I would treat Gelkopf and Kacmarek as **indirect reduction/feasibility evidence**, and Chou as **borderline on setting** because it was conducted in a day-care or partial-hospital ward.
