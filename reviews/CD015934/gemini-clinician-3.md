Yes. Primary randomized controlled trials demonstrate that quit-smoking programs initiated during an inpatient psychiatric stay are highly effective at helping adults stop smoking.

Historically, there has been a reluctance in psychiatry to treat tobacco dependence concurrently with acute mental illness. The prevailing assumption was often that quitting would interfere with psychiatric recovery, or that patients were too ill or unmotivated to quit. However, primary clinical trials directly challenge these assumptions and show strong positive outcomes when intervention begins in the hospital.

Here is what the primary research reveals:

## Increased Long-Term Abstinence

Initiating treatment in a smoke-free psychiatric ward capitalizes on a unique window of forced abstinence. Programs that provide motivationally tailored counseling alongside nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) during the hospital stay—and crucially, sustain that support for months after discharge—significantly increase long-term quit rates compared to standard care (Brown et al., 2021; Prochaska et al., 2014).

Even among patients who report low motivation to quit upon admission, meeting them where they are with tailored interventions yields significantly higher abstinence rates at 12 and 18 months post-discharge (Prochaska et al., 2014).

## No Harm to Psychiatric Recovery

One of the most persistent clinical myths is that quitting smoking exacerbates acute psychiatric symptoms or jeopardizes recovery. The trial data shows the opposite. Treating tobacco dependence does not increase psychiatric severity (Prochaska et al., 2014). In fact, patients who receive cessation interventions while hospitalized are actually less likely to be rehospitalized for their psychiatric conditions down the line compared to those who receive usual care (Prochaska et al., 2014).

## Benefits for Co-occurring Addictive Disorders

For patients with dual diagnoses (co-occurring mental illness and substance use disorders), smoking cessation interventions are equally effective. Importantly, the intervention does not jeopardize recovery from other substances. Instead, successful smoking cessation in this population is associated with decreased use of alcohol and cannabis in the months following discharge (Das et al., 2017).
