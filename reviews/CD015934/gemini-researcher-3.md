Yes, quit-smoking programmes initiated during a psychiatric hospitalization are effective at helping adults with mental illness stop smoking. Evidence from primary clinical trials shows that capitalizing on a hospital stay—especially in facilities with smoke-free policies—can serve as a critical turning point for tobacco cessation without jeopardizing the patient's mental health recovery.

Here is a synthesis of the key findings from the primary literature.

## The Inpatient Opportunity

Many inpatient psychiatric facilities enforce smoke-free policies, which provide a period of forced abstinence. Clinical trials leverage this "teachable moment" by initiating interventions while the patient is stabilized and away from their usual environmental smoking triggers.

Crucially, **readiness to quit is not a prerequisite for success**. In a landmark trial, only 16% of participants intended to quit smoking within the next 30 days upon admission, yet a motivationally tailored intervention successfully helped them build commitment and eventually quit (Prochaska et al., 2014). Effective inpatient programmes generally do not rely on a single intervention; rather, they use a multicomponent approach that starts in the hospital and bridges the transition back home. These typically include:

* In-hospital motivational interviewing and cognitive-behavioral counseling.
* Provision of nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) during the stay to manage withdrawal.
* Discharge planning that includes free transdermal nicotine patches and sustained care, such as connections to phone-based quitlines or ongoing outpatient support (Brown et al., 2021).

## Impact on Abstinence and Mental Health Outcomes

Initiating treatment in the hospital significantly improves long-term quit rates. In a sustained-care trial, patients receiving an intervention that started during their hospital stay and continued post-discharge were almost three times more likely to achieve verified abstinence at a 6-month follow-up compared to those receiving usual care (Brown et al., 2021).

Furthermore, these interventions challenge the outdated clinical assumption that smoking is a necessary coping mechanism for severe mental illness. Trials show that providing cessation treatment does not worsen psychiatric symptoms. In fact, patients who received comprehensive tobacco cessation treatment were found to have a significantly **lower risk of psychiatric rehospitalization** over an 18-month period compared to those who received standard care (Prochaska et al., 2014).
