## Bottom line

**Yes—quit-smoking treatment started on an inpatient psychiatry ward can improve cessation, but the benefit is generally modest and is most convincing when inpatient treatment is used to launch continued care after discharge.**

The most clinically applicable programs combined:

* motivational or behavioral counseling begun on the ward;
* nicotine replacement therapy during admission and/or supplied at discharge; and
* proactive counseling, medication access, telephone, text, or web support for several months after discharge.

Three comparatively large trials found a significant abstinence advantage: Prochaska et al. through 18 months, Metse et al. at 6 months, and Brown et al. at 6 months. ([PMC][1]) However, the effect was not uniform: Stockings et al. found a benefit at 4 months but not 6 months, and the smaller Hickman replication had numerically higher abstinence without a statistically significant between-group effect. ([PubMed][2])

A reasonable interpretation is that **hospital initiation helps primarily when it bridges patients into sustained treatment**. Ward-only behavioral programs, comparisons of different NRT doses or formulations, and small medication trials provide much less certain evidence of quitting. They more consistently demonstrate fewer cigarettes smoked, more quit attempts, or less nicotine withdrawal than sustained abstinence.

### Findings most useful clinically

* **Prochaska 2014:** verified 7-day abstinence was 14.4% versus 6.5% at 6 months and 20.0% versus 7.7% at 18 months. Only 16% initially intended to quit within 30 days, suggesting treatment need not be restricted to patients already ready to quit. ([PMC][1])
* **Metse 2017:** 7-day abstinence at 6 months was 15.8% versus 9.3%; the difference was no longer significant at 12 months, and prolonged abstinence did not improve. ([Sage Journals][3])
* **Brown/Helping HAND 3:** biochemically verified 7-day abstinence at 6 months was 8.9% versus 3.5%; adjusted OR 2.95, with an estimated NNT of 18.5. ([PMC][4])
* **Stockings 2014:** point-prevalence abstinence improved at the end of the four-month intervention, 11.5% versus 2.0%, but not at six months. Quit attempts, cigarette consumption, and dependence nevertheless improved. ([PubMed][2])
* **Hickman 2015:** abstinence consistently favored intervention—17.5% versus 8.5% at 6 months and 26.2% versus 16.7% at 12 months—but the treatment-group difference was not statistically significant in this 100-person feasibility trial. ([PMC][5])

Thus, for a hospital program, the evidence supports arranging medication supply and active follow-up before discharge rather than providing withdrawal management or brief advice alone. The trials do **not** establish that one particular cessation medication is best for psychiatric inpatients.

## Individual primary studies

The following are original study reports. I did not count protocols, trial registrations, secondary subgroup analyses, reviews, guidelines, or commentaries as separate studies.

1. **Akbarpour F, Rezaei O, Khodaie-Ardakani MR, et al.** A double-blind placebo-controlled trial of bupropion for smoking abstinence and cognition improvement in schizophrenia. *Minerva Psichiatrica.* 2010;51(4):263–269.
   Double-blind trial of 32 hospitalized patients with schizophrenia: bupropion 300 mg/day versus placebo for eight weeks. The authors reported greater end-of-treatment smoking abstinence/reduction with bupropion, but the published abstract does not provide numerical quit counts and uses an unclear combined abstinence/reduction outcome. ([Minerva Medica][6])

2. **Gelkopf M, Noam S, Rudinski D, et al.** Nonmedication smoking reduction program for inpatients with chronic schizophrenia: a randomized control design study. *J Nerv Ment Dis.* 2012;200(2):142–146. doi:10.1097/NMD.0b013e3182438e92.
   Fifty-three long-stay inpatients were randomized to a five-session behavioral group or waitlist. Cigarettes smoked decreased at three months without clinical worsening, but the reported endpoint was reduction rather than smoking abstinence. ([PubMed][7])

3. **Chen HK, Lan TH, Wu BJ.** A double-blind randomized clinical trial of different doses of transdermal nicotine patch for smoking reduction and cessation in long-term hospitalized schizophrenic patients. *Eur Arch Psychiatry Clin Neurosci.* 2013;263(1):75–82. doi:10.1007/s00406-012-0338-3.
   Among 184 long-stay inpatients receiving psychoeducation, higher-dose patch was compared with standard-dose patch. Only 5 participants, 2.7%, achieved 7-day abstinence, and higher dosing did not improve cessation or overall reduction. There was no placebo or non-NRT group. ([NYMU Academic Hub][8])

4. **Prochaska JJ, Hall SE, Delucchi K, Hall SM.** Efficacy of initiating tobacco dependence treatment in inpatient psychiatry: a randomized controlled trial. *Am J Public Health.* 2014;104(8):1557–1565. doi:10.2105/AJPH.2013.301403.
   In 224 acute psychiatric inpatients, motivationally tailored counseling plus ten weeks of postdischarge NRT produced significantly greater verified abstinence at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months than usual care. ([PMC][1])

5. **Stockings EA, Bowman JA, Baker AL, et al.** Impact of a postdischarge smoking cessation intervention for smokers admitted to an inpatient psychiatric facility: a randomized controlled trial. *Nicotine Tob Res.* 2014;16(11):1417–1428. doi:10.1093/ntr/ntu097.
   In 205 inpatients, psychosocial and pharmacological treatment continued for four months after discharge improved abstinence at four months, quit attempts, consumption, and dependence, but did not significantly improve abstinence at six months. ([PubMed][2])

6. **Hickman NJ III, Delucchi KL, Prochaska JJ.** Treating tobacco dependence at the intersection of diversity, poverty, and mental illness: a randomized feasibility and replication trial. *Nicotine Tob Res.* 2015;17(8):1012–1021. doi:10.1093/ntr/ntv034.
   One hundred public-hospital psychiatric inpatients received usual care or tailored computer/manual counseling, brief counseling, and ten weeks of postdischarge NRT. Abstinence favored intervention at every follow-up, but the between-group treatment effect was not statistically significant. ([PubMed][9])

7. **Metse AP, Wiggers J, Wye P, et al.** Efficacy of a universal smoking cessation intervention initiated in inpatient psychiatry and continued post-discharge: a randomised controlled trial. *Aust N Z J Psychiatry.* 2017;51(4):366–381. doi:10.1177/0004867417692424.
   In 754 participants across four facilities, brief inpatient motivational treatment followed by four months of pharmacological and psychosocial support improved 7-day abstinence at six months, but not at 12 months; prolonged abstinence did not differ. ([Sage Journals][3])

8. **Chan HY, Chen JJ, Pan YJ, Hsu CC.** Nicotine replacement therapy and healthy lifestyle psychoeducation for smoking reduction in acute psychiatric inpatients: a cluster-randomized parallel study. *J Clin Psychopharmacol.* 2020;40(2):149–156. doi:10.1097/JCP.0000000000001170.
   Three hundred ten acute inpatients received nicotine inhaler, gum, or patch plus healthy-lifestyle psychoeducation. The inhaler group had significant within-group reductions in exhaled CO at weeks 4 and 8. The study primarily evaluated smoking reduction and withdrawal, not abstinence, and lacked a non-NRT control. ([ResearchGate][10])

9. **Brown RA, Minami H, Hecht J, et al.** Sustained care smoking cessation intervention for individuals hospitalized for psychiatric disorders: the Helping HAND 3 randomized clinical trial. *JAMA Psychiatry.* 2021;78(8):839–847. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.0707.
   Three hundred fifty-three adults were randomized to usual care or inpatient motivational counseling, free discharge nicotine patches, connection to quitline/text/web counseling, and automated follow-up. Verified six-month abstinence was 8.9% versus 3.5%. ([JAMA Network][11])

10. **Tavakoli-Ardakani M, Gholamzadeh Sani Z, Beyraghi N, et al.** Comparison between cytisine and nicotine replacement therapy in smoking cessation among inpatient psychiatric patients. *J Addict Dis.* 2024;42(4):352–359. doi:10.1080/10550887.2023.2237395.
    Forty-seven motivated inpatients were randomized open-label to cytisine or 2-mg nicotine gum. At six months, 3 of 17 cytisine participants and 2 of 30 NRT participants reported complete cessation. The very small number of events does not establish cytisine superiority. ([Taylor & Francis Online][12])

11. **Bennett ME, Dickerson F, Cha S, et al.** Bridging the hospital discharge gap: pilot trial of a digital smoking cessation program for psychiatric inpatients. *Psychiatr Serv.* 2026;77(5):421–428. doi:10.1176/appi.ps.20250326.
    In this 58-person randomized pilot, a digital program oriented during hospitalization was compared with brief counseling. At one month, 6 digital-intervention participants versus none receiving brief counseling reported 7-day abstinence. This is a promising early signal, not definitive evidence of durable cessation. ([Psychiatry Online][13])

12. **Kacmarek CN, Smith HC, Lucksted A, et al.** Efficacy, acceptability, and feasibility of StayQuit for sustaining smoking abstinence after psychiatric hospitalization: a pilot study. *J Patient Cent Res Rev.* 2026;13(1):42–50. doi:10.17294/2330-0698.2184.
    Uncontrolled mixed-methods pilot involving 26 VA psychiatric inpatients. Fifteen completed follow-up; among those completers, mean smoking decreased from 15 to 8 cigarettes/day at 12 weeks and self-efficacy improved. Because there was no control group and substantial attrition, it supports feasibility rather than proving cessation efficacy. ([PMC][14])
