Evidence from primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrates that smoking cessation programs initiated in inpatient psychiatric wards are effective for helping adults with mental illness stop smoking. The acute inpatient setting—particularly in facilities with strict smoke-free policies—provides a unique "teachable moment" to initiate treatment by capitalizing on a period of forced abstinence.

Here is a synthesis of the evidence from key primary studies in the field.

## Key Findings from Primary Trials

**Efficacy of Tailored, Multi-Component Interventions**
Providing standard care (such as basic pamphlets and nicotine replacement therapy [NRT] limited only to the hospital stay) generally results in low long-term quit rates. However, interventions that are motivationally tailored to the patient's readiness to quit show high efficacy. One landmark RCT found that combining a stage-tailored behavioral intervention (delivered via counseling and computer) with NRT during the stay—and ensuring NRT access continued after discharge—significantly increased abstinence (Prochaska et al., 2014). At 18 months post-hospitalization, 20.0% of the intervention group maintained abstinence, compared to only 7.7% of the usual care group.

**The Necessity of Sustained Post-Discharge Care**
Because the risk of relapse is exceptionally high in the days immediately following psychiatric discharge, interventions that bridge the inpatient-to-outpatient transition are critical. The Helping HAND 3 trial evaluated a "sustained care" model that included inpatient motivational counseling, free transdermal nicotine patches provided at the time of discharge, and proactive connections to post-discharge quitlines (Brown et al., 2021). This sustained approach resulted in significantly higher 6-month abstinence rates (8.9%) compared to usual care (3.5%), alongside much higher rates of ongoing patient engagement with cessation treatments.

**Debunking the Risk to Psychiatric Recovery**
A longstanding barrier to inpatient tobacco treatment has been the clinical assumption that quitting smoking could worsen acute psychiatric symptoms or remove a necessary coping mechanism. Primary data directly refutes this. Implementing intensive smoking cessation treatments does not harm mental health recovery; in fact, patients receiving the tailored cessation intervention had a significantly *lower* likelihood of subsequent psychiatric re-hospitalization compared to those who only received usual care (Prochaska et al., 2014).
