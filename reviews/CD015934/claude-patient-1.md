Based on the searches, several completed **primary studies (RCTs and a controlled trial)** have directly tested smoking-cessation programmes initiated during a psychiatric hospital admission for adults with mental illness — as opposed to review articles, which I excluded per your request.

**Key findings across studies:**

Prochaska and colleagues randomized participants recruited from a locked acute psychiatry unit with a 100% smoking ban to a motivational tobacco cessation treatment combined with nicotine replacement or to usual care. Verified abstinence was significantly higher for the intervention group than usual care at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months, and the usual-care group had a significantly greater likelihood of psychiatric rehospitalization.

A larger four-site Australian trial randomised 754 participants across four psychiatric inpatient facilities to usual care or an intervention combining a brief motivational interview and self-help material in hospital, followed by a 4-month pharmacological and psychosocial programme after discharge. The treatment was efficacious in increasing 7-day point-prevalence smoking cessation and quitting behaviours at 6 months, with sustained effects on quitting behaviour at 12 months.

A related, smaller Australian trial at a single inpatient psychiatric facility allocated 205 patient smokers to treatment-as-usual or an intervention with psychosocial and pharmacological support for 4 months postdischarge, finding that postdischarge cessation support was effective in encouraging quit attempts and reducing cigarette consumption up to 6 months postdischarge.

The "Helping HAND 3" trial examined the effectiveness of a multicomponent, sustained-care smoking cessation intervention versus usual care for smoking abstinence in adults with serious mental illness during and after inpatient care, published in JAMA Psychiatry.
