It is incredibly demanding to navigate an inpatient psychiatric stay, and looking into quitting smoking right now is a huge, proactive step. For a long time, there was an assumption—even among some mental health professionals—that smoking was a necessary "crutch" for psychiatric patients and that trying to quit would derail recovery. The clinical trials you are asking for directly challenge that myth.

Primary studies (specifically randomized controlled trials) show that quit-smoking programs initiated in psychiatric wards do indeed help adults stop smoking, especially when the program bridges the gap between the hospital stay and the return home.

Here is what the primary research shows about these programs:

## Do These Programs Work?

Yes. Clinical trials consistently show that patients who begin a smoking cessation program while hospitalized for a mental illness are significantly more likely to successfully quit.

* **Higher Long-Term Quit Rates:** Patients who receive a combination of counseling and nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) like patches or gum during their stay—and importantly, continued support after leaving the hospital—achieve significantly higher rates of smoking abstinence 6, 12, and 18 months later compared to those who just receive standard care or brief pamphlets (Brown et al., 2021; Metse et al., 2017).
* **Mental Health Protection:** Far from harming mental health, quitting smoking can actually support your psychiatric recovery. In a major trial at an acute inpatient facility, the group that received the smoking cessation intervention had a significantly *lower* rate of being rehospitalized for their psychiatric conditions (44%) over the next 18 months compared to the group that received standard care (56%) (Prochaska et al., 2014).

## What Makes These Programs Effective?

The clinical trials highlight that the most successful interventions use a "sustained care" model. They don't just enforce a smoking ban on the ward; they actively treat the nicotine dependence as part of your overall healthcare.

* **No Pressure to Quit on Day One:** The most effective interventions meet you where you are. Even if you aren't 100% ready to quit the day you are admitted, these programs use the hospital stay to manage your immediate withdrawals with NRT while using counseling to gradually build your motivation (Prochaska et al., 2014).
* **Post-Discharge Continuity:** The transition out of the hospital is the highest risk period for a relapse. Trials show that programs are most effective when they send you home with a multi-week supply of nicotine patches or gum and provide follow-up check-ins (via phone or outpatient clinics) to help you navigate triggers in your normal environment (Brown et al., 2021; Metse et al., 2017).

It is completely normal to feel apprehensive about dropping cigarettes while managing a mental illness. However, the evidence strongly suggests that leaning on a structured program right now—while you have medical staff around you—can benefit both your physical and psychiatric recovery. If your care team offers NRT and counseling, the science supports taking advantage of it.
