It takes a lot of strength to focus on your physical health while also seeking treatment for a mental illness, so it is commendable that you are looking into this right now. Being in a psychiatric ward can be an overwhelming experience, but it can actually be a highly effective environment to start the process of quitting smoking.

To answer your question directly: **Yes, primary research shows that smoking cessation programs initiated during an inpatient psychiatric stay do help adults successfully quit smoking.**

Because most hospital psychiatric units are smoke-free, the hospitalization provides a period of forced abstinence. Researchers consider this a unique "teachable moment" that can serve as a jumping-off point for quitting long-term (Brown et al., 2021).

### What Makes These Programs Effective?

Successful programs in these settings go beyond simply handing out pamphlets; they use a "sustained care" approach. Clinical trials show that patients achieve significantly higher quit rates than usual care when they receive a combination of:

* **Motivational Counseling:** Meeting with counselors who tailor their approach to your current readiness to quit (Prochaska et al., 2014).
* **Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT):** Using patches, gums, or lozenges to manage withdrawal symptoms during your hospital stay, with free supplies provided upon discharge (Brown et al., 2021).
* **Post-Discharge Support:** Continuing the momentum after you leave the hospital through phone counseling, text-based support, or web-based check-ins (Brown et al., 2021).

### The Impact on Mental Health Recovery

You might be wondering if quitting smoking right now will add too much stress or make your mental health worse. Historically, there has been a misconception that smoking helps manage psychiatric symptoms.

However, studies show that quitting does *not* harm mental health recovery. In fact, a major randomized controlled trial found that psychiatric patients who participated in a tailored smoking cessation program were actually **less likely to be rehospitalized** for psychiatric reasons over the next 18 months compared to those who received standard care (Prochaska et al., 2014). Treating the nicotine dependence may provide broader therapeutic benefits that actually support your overall recovery.
