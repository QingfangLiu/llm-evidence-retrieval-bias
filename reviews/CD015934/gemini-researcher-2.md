Evidence from primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrates that smoking cessation programs initiated during an acute inpatient psychiatric stay can significantly help adults quit smoking. Historically, some practitioners assumed that quitting smoking could disrupt mental health recovery, but clinical trials challenge this assumption and highlight the hospital ward as a critical intervention window.

Because most psychiatric units enforce a 100% smoking ban, the hospital stay provides an enforced period of abstinence. Primary studies show that capitalizing on this "teachable moment" improves long-term outcomes without harming mental health.

Key findings from the primary literature include:

* **Improved Long-Term Quit Rates:** Patients who begin a tailored cessation program while hospitalized have significantly higher rates of abstinence months after discharge compared to those receiving usual care. In one RCT, a sustained care intervention led to a 6-month abstinence rate of 8.9% compared to 3.5% for usual care (Brown et al., 2021). Another trial tracked patients for 18 months and found those in the motivationally-tailored intervention arm achieved a 20.0% quit rate versus 7.7% in the usual care arm (Prochaska et al., 2014).
* **Effective Pharmacotherapy Options:** Beyond behavioral interventions, the specific medications initiated on the ward matter. An open-label trial comparing cytisine (a plant-based alkaloid) to standard nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) in psychiatric inpatients found that cytisine yielded better abstinence outcomes (17.6% vs. 6.6%) at six months post-discharge (Tavakoli-Ardakani et al., 2023).
* **Continued Post-Discharge Engagement:** Interventions that successfully transition patients into long-term resources yield the best outcomes. Approaches that combine inpatient counseling with post-discharge resources—such as automated texts, quitlines, and supplies of transdermal nicotine patches—effectively sustain the motivation built during hospitalization (Brown et al., 2021).
* **No Harm to Mental Health Recovery:** Treating tobacco dependence does not worsen psychiatric symptoms. In fact, cessation support may provide broader therapeutic benefits. One study found that patients in the usual care group actually had a significantly higher likelihood of psychiatric rehospitalization than those assigned to the smoking cessation intervention group (Prochaska et al., 2014).
