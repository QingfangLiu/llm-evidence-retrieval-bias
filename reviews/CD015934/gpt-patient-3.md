## Bottom line

**Yes—quit-smoking programmes begun on psychiatric wards can help some adults stop smoking, but the average benefit is modest.** The clearest evidence comes from programmes that:

1. begin counseling and nicotine-dependence treatment during admission;
2. provide nicotine replacement therapy or another appropriate medication; and
3. continue active support for several months after discharge.

Results were not uniformly sustained. One trial found a benefit through 18 months, while others found an advantage at four or six months that was no longer clearly present at the final follow-up. Absolute quit rates generally remained low. ([PMC][1])

I identified **11 published randomized comparative primary studies** involving adult psychiatric inpatients. Two mainly studied smoking reduction rather than successful quitting, so they provide only indirect evidence for cessation.

## Interpretation

Across the better-quality trials, the **hospital admission appears to be a useful starting point, but inpatient counseling alone is probably insufficient**. The programmes with the clearest cessation effects arranged continued medication access, counseling, quitline contact, texts or other proactive follow-up after discharge. ([PMC][1])

Important uncertainties remain:

* Quit rates were generally low, even with intensive treatment.
* Studies used different definitions of abstinence; several reported seven-day point-prevalence abstinence rather than uninterrupted long-term abstinence.
* Durability was inconsistent.
* Several medication trials were very small or compared two active treatments without a usual-care control.
* Smoking reduction is potentially beneficial, but it is not the same outcome as stopping smoking completely.
