## Bottom line

Using a strict definition—**adult smokers admitted to an inpatient psychiatric ward, with the intervention commencing during that admission**—I identified **11 published randomized or cluster-randomized primary studies and one uncontrolled pilot study** through July 25, 2026.

My synthesis of the primary studies is:

* **Multicomponent programmes begun on the ward and actively continued after discharge can modestly increase short- to medium-term smoking abstinence.**
* The most persuasive evidence concerns programmes combining motivational or behavioral counseling, nicotine-replacement therapy, and repeated postdischarge contact. Prochaska and Brown found significantly greater biochemically verified abstinence at six months; Metse found greater seven-day point-prevalence abstinence at six months but not 12 months; Stockings found a benefit at four months that was no longer significant at six months. Hickman found numerically higher abstinence but was an underpowered feasibility trial. ([eScholarship][1])
* Evidence for **durable continuous or prolonged abstinence** is less convincing. Absolute quit rates were generally low, and favorable point-prevalence effects were not always maintained at 12 months.
* Small medication, nicotine-replacement, and behavioral-reduction studies do not establish which individual treatment component is most effective. Several measured smoking reduction or exhaled carbon monoxide rather than sustained abstinence.

### Uncontrolled primary study

**Kacmarek et al., 2026 — StayQuit:** A single-arm VA pilot began counseling during psychiatric hospitalization and continued telephone counseling and NRT support for 13 weeks. Twenty-six participants were analyzed; 15 completed at least one counseling session and the 12-week assessment. Cigarette consumption and abstinence self-efficacy improved among completers, but the absence of a comparison group and substantial attrition mean the study cannot demonstrate that StayQuit increased cessation. ([PMC][13])

## Interpretation for an evidence synthesis

The clinically relevant conclusion is narrower than “inpatient smoking treatment works”:

1. **Best-supported intervention:** A bundle of inpatient motivational or behavioral counseling, access to NRT, and proactive support extending for several months after discharge.
2. **Most consistent endpoint:** Seven-day point-prevalence abstinence at approximately four to six months.
3. **Uncertain endpoints:** Continuous/prolonged abstinence and maintenance beyond six to 12 months.
4. **Component attribution:** The studies generally tested treatment bundles, so they cannot determine whether counseling, NRT, digital contact or care coordination was the active ingredient.
5. **Generalizability:** Some studies involved acute mixed-diagnosis wards, while others were restricted to schizophrenia or long-stay facilities.
6. **Risk of imprecision:** Many individual trials were small, with few quit events. The larger trials provide the most credible estimates.
7. **Outcome heterogeneity:** Several smaller studies focused on cigarettes per day, carbon monoxide or “reduction,” which should not be pooled uncritically with biochemically verified abstinence.

### Relevant records not included as qualifying primary efficacy studies

* **Chou et al., 2004** evaluated nicotine patches among patients recruited from a psychiatric **day-care ward**, rather than an inpatient psychiatric admission, so it falls outside a strict inpatient-ward criterion. ([ScienceDirect][14])
* Secondary or pooled analyses of the Prochaska and Hickman trials should not be counted as separate studies.
* A large registered inpatient trial, NCT00968513, enrolled participants, but I did not find a peer-reviewed primary publication reporting its main smoking-cessation efficacy outcome.
* Chart reviews describing delivery of tobacco treatment during psychiatric hospitalization were excluded because they did not test cessation effectiveness.
