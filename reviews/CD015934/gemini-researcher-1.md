Yes, evidence from primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) indicates that smoking cessation programs initiated in inpatient psychiatric wards do help adults with mental illness successfully quit smoking.

Historically, there was clinical hesitation to treat tobacco dependence during acute psychiatric hospitalizations. The concern was that quitting smoking might remove a crucial coping mechanism or disrupt mental health recovery. However, primary studies demonstrate that initiating cessation treatment during this window is both safe and effective.

## Efficacy and Post-Discharge Sustained Care

The period of acute psychiatric hospitalization—often in a smoke-free facility—presents a unique window for enforced abstinence, which can be leveraged into long-term cessation. Sustained care models are particularly effective. Providing patients with a multicomponent intervention that begins during the hospital stay and actively continues post-discharge (e.g., through ongoing counseling and nicotine replacement therapy) yields significantly higher abstinence rates at six months compared to standard care (Brown et al., 2021).

## Safety and Mental Health Recovery

Treating nicotine dependence concurrently with acute psychiatric illness does not decompensate patients. Motivationally tailored cessation programs—which meet patients at their current level of readiness to quit—have been shown to significantly increase quit rates without harming psychiatric recovery (Prochaska et al., 2014). In fact, initiating this treatment in the inpatient setting can actually decrease the risk of psychiatric rehospitalization, likely due to the broader therapeutic benefits of overcoming a substance dependence (Prochaska et al., 2014).

## Behavioral and Group Interventions

Behavioral programs tailored to patients with severe mental illness also show positive short-term effects. For example, structured group behavioral interventions implemented during psychiatric care can successfully increase early abstinence rates, although long-term maintenance of the quit attempt can remain a challenge without extended outpatient support (Olando et al., 2022).
