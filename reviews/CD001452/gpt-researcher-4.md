## Bottom line

I identified **eight published primary comparative studies** of venepuncture versus heel lance/heel stick/heel prick in newborns. For routine newborn screening or tests requiring several drops or a larger volume of blood, most studies found that **venepuncture caused less observable pain and was often faster or more likely to succeed with one puncture**. ([PubMed][1])

The main exception was a study of **single-drop blood-glucose monitoring**, where pain scores did not differ significantly and heel prick was much faster. ([PubMed][2])

Sweet solutions appear to modify the comparison. In one factorial trial, venepuncture was less painful without oral glucose, but the difference disappeared when both groups received glucose. In another trial, sucrose reduced pain with both procedures, but venepuncture still generally produced lower pain scores. ([PubMed][3])

## Interpretation

### Pain

Seven studies reported findings that favored venepuncture in at least one pain outcome. However, two qualifications are important:

* In the Eriksson trial, oral glucose reduced pain enough that venepuncture and heel stick no longer differed significantly. ([PubMed][3])
* For single-drop glucose monitoring, Saththasivam et al. found no significant pain advantage for venepuncture. ([PubMed][2])

Thus, the apparent pain advantage is clearest for **routine metabolic screening or other samples requiring prolonged collection and heel squeezing**, rather than a rapid single-drop heel puncture.

### Sampling success and time

Venepuncture generally performed better when a meaningful blood volume was required. Larsson et al. found a large advantage in one-puncture success, while Ogawa et al. and Correcher Medina et al. found shorter procedures with venepuncture. ([AAP Publications][4])

That relationship reversed for a one-drop glucose test: heel prick was approximately 20 seconds faster in the Saththasivam study. ([PubMed][2])

### Important limitations for synthesis

The studies are clinically heterogeneous: they examined metabolic screening, bilirubin sampling, general diagnostic sampling, and single-drop glucose monitoring; used different lancets and needle gauges; differed in the use of oral glucose or sucrose; and involved operators with differing venepuncture experience. Pain was generally assessed behaviorally, and the sampling procedure itself could not be blinded. ([PubMed][3])

The Correcher Medina study is not strictly a full-term-only study and used non-concurrent calendar-period allocation. Larsson et al. described the population as healthy full-term newborns, but the published study has also been noted to include some infants around the late-preterm boundary. These studies should therefore be handled carefully in a strictly ≥37-week synthesis. ([Anales de Pediatría][8])
