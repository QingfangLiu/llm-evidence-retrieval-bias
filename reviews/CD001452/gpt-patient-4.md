## Bottom line

For a healthy full-term newborn who needs **more than a single drop of blood**, the individual comparative studies generally found that **venepuncture from a hand vein—when performed by someone experienced with newborns—caused less pain, required fewer repeat punctures, or collected the sample faster than heel lancing**. ([Nature][1])

The principal exception was a study of **bedside glucose monitoring**, where only one drop was required. In that study, pain measures did not differ significantly, while heel prick was much faster: median 7 seconds versus 27 seconds for venepuncture. ([PubMed][2])

### What “better” means in these studies

**Pain:** Most studies found lower behavioral pain scores, less crying, or smaller heart-rate/oxygen-saturation changes with venepuncture. For example:

* In 27 term infants, the mean NIPS pain score during sampling was 2.8 with venepuncture versus 5.2 with heel stick. Four heel-stick infants versus one venepuncture infant needed repeat sampling because of insufficient volume or hemolysis. ([Nature][1])
* In 120 full-term infants having newborn metabolic screening, venepuncture produced lower facial pain scores and less crying than either small- or large-lancet heel sampling. ([AAP Publications][3])
* In 200 term infants undergoing bilirubin or glucose testing, median NIPS scores were 3.5 with venepuncture and 7 with heel lance; the heel-lance group also had larger heart-rate increases and oxygen-saturation decreases. ([NepJol][4])

**First-attempt success and duration:** This advantage was clearest when several drops or a screening card had to be filled. In Larsson’s study, one puncture was sufficient in 86% of venepunctures, compared with 19% using a small heel lancet and 40% using a larger lancet. Median collection times were 191, 419, and 279 seconds, respectively. ([AAP Publications][3])

**Sweet solutions:** The results are not completely uniform. In one 120-infant trial, without glucose, heel sampling caused more crying and higher pain scores; after 1 mL of 30% oral glucose, the difference between methods was no longer statistically significant. ([PubMed][5]) In another 100-infant study, sucrose reduced pain responses, but venepuncture still generally produced lower pain and shorter-procedure outcomes than heel lance. ([PubMed][6])

**Technique matters:** A trial comparing two venepuncture needle sizes found that the small-caliber venepuncture group had less pain than heel lancing, but the larger-caliber venepuncture failed to obtain a sample in 33% of infants. This supports using appropriately sized equipment and an experienced neonatal operator. ([Sage Journals][7])

## Important limitations

These studies were generally small, and most were conducted between 1997 and 2012. Operators and bedside observers could not always be blinded to the procedure. Different studies required different amounts of blood and used different pain scales, lancets, needles, comfort measures, and operator experience.

The evidence is strongest for **routine laboratory or metabolic-screening samples requiring several drops**. It does not establish that venepuncture is preferable for every test. For a test requiring only one drop—particularly point-of-care glucose—a heel lance may be quicker and similarly tolerated. Rare complications were not studied adequately.

A useful question for the clinical team is: **“How much blood is needed, and is someone experienced in neonatal venepuncture available?”** The laboratory must also accept the type of specimen collected.

## Individual primary studies

### Studies explicitly involving term or full-term newborns

1. **Shah VS, Taddio A, Bennett S, Speidel BD.** Neonatal pain response to heel stick vs venepuncture for routine blood sampling. *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed.* 1997;77:F143–F144. Randomized study of 27 term infants; venepuncture produced lower pain scores and fewer inadequate or hemolyzed repeat samples. ([Nature][1])

2. **Larsson BA, Tannfeldt G, Lagercrantz H, Olsson GL.** Venipuncture is more effective and less painful than heel lancing for blood tests in neonates. *Pediatrics.* 1998;101:882–886. Randomized study of 120 healthy full-term infants; venepuncture caused less pain and crying and was more frequently successful with one puncture. ([AAP Publications][3])

3. **Eriksson M, Gradin M, Schollin J.** Oral glucose and venepuncture reduce blood sampling pain in newborns. *Early Hum Dev.* 1999;55:211–218. Randomized four-group study of 120 healthy full-term infants; venepuncture was less painful without oral glucose, while glucose substantially reduced the difference. ([PubMed][5])

4. **Kvist LJ, Jönsson K, Tornestrand BM, Edwinson Månsson M.** Can venepuncture reduce the pain of neonatal PKU-sampling? A randomised study. *Vård i Norden/Nordic Journal of Nursing Research.* 2002;22:27–30. Ninety healthy full-term infants; small-caliber venepuncture caused less pain than heel lancing, but large-caliber venepuncture had a high failure rate. ([Sage Journals][7])

5. **Ogawa S, Ogihara T, Fujiwara E, et al.** Venepuncture is preferable to heel lance for blood sampling in term neonates. *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed.* 2005;90:F432–F436. Randomized, double-blind sucrose/placebo study of 100 healthy full-term infants; venepuncture generally caused less pain and required less procedure time. ([PubMed][6])

6. **Saththasivam P, Umadevan D, Ramli N, et al.** Venipuncture versus heel prick for blood glucose monitoring in neonates. *Singapore Med J.* 2009;50:1004–1007. Sixty-six term infants requiring a one-drop glucose measurement; pain outcomes were not significantly different, and heel prick was faster. ([PubMed][2])

7. **Shrestha M, Adhikari RK.** Comparison of pain response to venepuncture versus heel lance blood sampling in term neonates. *J Nepal Paediatr Soc.* 2012;32:99–104. Two hundred term infants undergoing bilirubin or glucose testing; venepuncture produced substantially lower pain scores and smaller physiologic stress responses. ([NepJol][4])

### Additional direct comparative studies with population-design caveats

8. **Logan PW.** Venepuncture versus heel prick for the collection of the Newborn Screening Test. *Aust J Adv Nurs.* 1999;17:30–36. Controlled comparison of 36 newborns at two maternity centers; venepuncture was associated with shorter collection and crying times. Allocation was effectively by center rather than individual randomization, and the available abstract does not clearly state a full-term eligibility criterion. ([SWH Digital Repository][8])

9. **Correcher Medina P, Pedrón Marzal G, Rey Simón R, Calvo Rigual F.** Back-of-the-hand venepuncture: an alternative to heel puncture? *An Pediatr (Barc).* 2012;77:381–385. Randomized observational comparison of 607 newborns; venepuncture produced lower NIPS scores, less crying, shorter collection times, and fewer punctures. The gestational-age range was 35–42 weeks, so the cohort was predominantly term but included some late-preterm infants. ([analesdepediatria.org][9])
