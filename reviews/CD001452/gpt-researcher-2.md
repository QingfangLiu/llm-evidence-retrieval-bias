## Overall finding

For **healthy full-term newborns needing more than a single drop of blood**, the individual comparative studies generally found that **venepuncture by a trained practitioner caused less procedural pain and often required fewer punctures or less collection time than heel lancing**. The principal exception was blood-glucose monitoring, where only one drop was required: that study found no significant pain difference and heel prick was substantially faster. ([AAP Publications][1])

I found **seven primary comparative studies clearly described as enrolling term/full-term newborns**. I also found two relevant comparative studies with population-eligibility concerns: one did not state gestational-age eligibility in its accessible report, and one included newborns born at 35–42 weeks.

## Primary evidence in clearly term newborns

### 1. Shah et al., 1997

Randomized trial of 27 term neonates undergoing routine sampling: venepuncture, n=13; heel stick, n=14.

* Mean procedural NIPS: **2.8 with venepuncture versus 5.2 with heel stick**, p<0.001.
* An inadequate or hemolyzed sample necessitated repeat sampling in 1 venepuncture infant and 4 heel-stick infants.
* Mothers were more anxious about venepuncture beforehand, but afterward perceived less infant pain with venepuncture. ([Nature][2])

**Direction:** Favored venepuncture for pain and sample adequacy.

### 2. Larsson et al., 1998

Randomized trial of 120 healthy full-term infants having phenylketonuria screening: venepuncture, n=50; small heel lancet, n=50; large heel lancet, n=20.

* Facial pain responses were significantly lower with venepuncture.
* Crying during the first 60 seconds occurred in **44% with venepuncture, 72% with the small lancet, and 85% with the large lancet**.
* One puncture produced an adequate sample in **86%, 19%, and 40%**, respectively.
* Median collection time was **191 seconds with venepuncture, 419 seconds with the small lancet, and 279 seconds with the large lancet**. ([AAP Publications][1])

**Direction:** Strongly favored venepuncture for pain, first-attempt success, and collection time.

### 3. Eriksson et al., 1999

Randomized four-group trial of 120 healthy full-term newborns: venepuncture or heel stick, with or without 1 mL of 30% oral glucose.

Without glucose:

* Mean crying time: **26.8 seconds with venepuncture versus 57.3 seconds with heel stick**, p=0.0041.
* Mean PIPP: **6.0 versus 8.4**, p=0.0458.

With glucose:

* Crying time was 11.6 versus 12.9 seconds.
* PIPP was 3.3 versus 3.9.
* Neither difference was statistically significant. ([PubMed][3])

**Direction:** Favored venepuncture without glucose; oral glucose substantially reduced and eliminated the observed between-method difference.

### 4. Kvist et al., 2002

Randomized trial of 90 healthy, full-term, breastfed newborns undergoing newborn screening: small-caliber venepuncture, n=30; large-caliber venepuncture, n=30; heel lance, n=30.

* Small-caliber venepuncture produced significantly lower NIPS scores than heel lance at one and two minutes.
* The large-caliber venepuncture failed to produce a sample in **33%**, leaving that comparison underpowered.
* Higher birth weight was associated with more difficult venepuncture. ([Sage Journals][4])

**Direction:** Favored small-caliber venepuncture, but needle caliber and technical success mattered.

### 5. Ogawa et al., 2005

Randomized, placebo-controlled four-group trial of 100 healthy full-term newborns undergoing metabolic screening: venepuncture or heel lance after sucrose or water, n=25 per group.

* Without sucrose, median NFCS was **23 with venepuncture versus 58 with heel lance**, p<0.001.
* Sucrose reduced pain responses in both groups.
* Nevertheless, heel lance with sucrose still produced a higher score than venepuncture with water: **47 versus 23**, p<0.01.
* Crying and total procedure time followed the same direction. ([PMC][5])

**Direction:** Favored venepuncture, including when heel lance was accompanied by sucrose.

### 6. Saththasivam et al., 2009

Comparative study of 66 term neonates requiring blood-glucose monitoring.

* NFCS scores did not differ significantly.
* Median procedure time was **27 seconds for venepuncture versus 7 seconds for heel prick**, p<0.001.
* Cry duration and number of punctures were not significantly different, although their directions favored heel prick. ([PubMed][6])

**Direction:** Did not show a venepuncture pain advantage; favored heel prick operationally when only one drop of blood was required.

### 7. Shrestha and Adhikari, 2012

The article describes a “comparative observational study” in which 200 term newborns requiring bilirubin or glucose testing were randomly assigned to venepuncture or heel lance, n=100 per group.

* Median NIPS: **3.5 with venepuncture versus 7 with heel lance**, p=0.0001.
* The increase in heart rate and fall in oxygen saturation were significantly greater with heel lance. ([NepJol][7])

**Direction:** Favored venepuncture for behavioral and physiological pain responses.

## Relevant comparative studies with eligibility concerns

### Logan, 1999

Thirty-six newborns at two maternity centers underwent venepuncture or heel prick for newborn screening. Each center used only one of the two methods, so method was confounded with center and staff.

Venepuncture was associated with shorter collection time, less total crying, shorter first cry, a lower percentage of time crying, and longer cry latency; all reported as p<0.05. The accessible abstract does **not state that all infants were born at term**, so term-population eligibility needs confirmation from the complete article. ([SWH Digital Repository][8])

### Correcher Medina et al., 2012

Comparative cohort of 607 healthy newborns undergoing metabolic screening: venepuncture, n=280; heel puncture, n=327.

* NIPS: **2 versus 5**, p<0.001.
* Cry duration: **58 versus 104 seconds**.
* Procedure duration: **60 versus 120 seconds**.
* Venepuncture required fewer attempts.

However:

* Gestational age ranged from **35 to 42 weeks**, so this was not a strictly full-term population.
* Allocation was by birth period—March–June 2009 for venepuncture and July 2009–January 2010 for heel puncture—not individual randomization. ([Anales de Pediatría][9])

## Interpretation

The comparison is highly dependent on the **purpose and required blood volume**:

* For newborn screening, bilirubin testing, or other samples requiring several drops or approximately 0.4 mL, the studies consistently favored venepuncture for pain and usually for sampling efficiency.
* For bedside glucose testing requiring only one drop, heel prick was much faster and produced no significantly greater pain in the relevant study.
* Oral glucose or sucrose reduces pain with either method and may narrow the difference.
* The favorable venepuncture results generally came from trained or experienced practitioners. Results may not transfer directly to settings where neonatal venepuncture experience is limited.
* The studies are mostly small, operators could not be blinded, assessor blinding was frequently unclear, and devices, needle gauges, sample volumes, pain scales, and co-interventions differed considerably. I would therefore avoid treating their results as one homogeneous effect.
