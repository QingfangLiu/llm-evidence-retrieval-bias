## Clinical bottom line

For **healthy full-term newborns requiring a routine blood sample**, the individual comparative studies generally suggest that **dorsal-hand venepuncture is less painful and often more efficient than heel lancing**, provided the operator is experienced. This is most apparent when enough blood is needed for newborn screening, bilirubin, or other laboratory testing.

The main exception is **point-of-care glucose testing requiring only one drop**: one controlled study found no pain difference, while heel prick took about 7 seconds versus 28 seconds for venepuncture. ([ResearchGate][1])

## Interpretation for practice

**Pain:** Most of the directly applicable studies found less immediate behavioral pain or crying with venepuncture. Oral glucose or sucrose reduces pain with either method and may narrow the difference, although the two glucose/sucrose trials did not give completely identical results. ([ScienceDirect][4])

**Sampling efficiency:** For newborn screening or samples requiring several drops or a larger volume, venepuncture generally required fewer punctures and was faster. For a **single drop of blood glucose**, heel prick was faster and venepuncture offered no demonstrated pain advantage. ([AAP Publications][3])

**Operator dependence:** The favorable venepuncture results frequently came from settings using trained or experienced personnel. The large-needle failure rate in Kvist and the single-drop glucose findings illustrate that needle size, operator availability, blood volume, and test purpose matter. ([Sage Journals][5])

**Safety and sample quality:** The studies were mainly designed around short-term pain, crying, collection time, and repeat punctures. Adverse events and specimen-quality outcomes were inconsistently and incompletely reported, so the evidence does **not establish that either technique is generally safer** or produces more accurate laboratory results. ([Nature][2])

Overall, the primary evidence favors **venepuncture by a skilled clinician when more than a very small capillary sample is required**. It does not support routinely replacing a quick heel prick with venepuncture when only one drop is needed.
