It is completely understandable to want the gentlest option for your newborn. When a baby needs a blood test, it is entirely natural to assume that a quick prick on the heel would be less painful than using a needle in a vein (venepuncture). However, individual primary studies—specifically randomized controlled trials—have actually shown the opposite for full-term babies.

Here is what the primary research says about comparing the two methods:

### Pain and Discomfort

Studies using validated neonatal pain scales, which measure things like facial expressions and crying duration, have found that full-term babies experience significantly less pain during venepuncture than during a heel lance (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005). Babies in the venepuncture groups had lower pain scores, and fewer of them cried compared to those who had their heels pricked.

### Speed and Effectiveness

You might expect a heel prick to be faster, but research shows venepuncture is often much more efficient. In one trial, venepuncture successfully yielded the required amount of blood on the first attempt 86% of the time, compared to only 19% to 40% of the time for heel lances (Larsson et al., 1998). Because heel lances often require the heel to be squeezed repeatedly to get enough blood, the total procedure time actually ends up being longer, causing the baby more prolonged distress (Ogawa, 2005).

### Why are heel lances still common?

If venepuncture is less painful, you might wonder why heel lances are still widely used. Heel pricks are generally standard practice because they are technically easier for general medical staff to perform. Drawing blood directly from a tiny newborn's vein requires a clinician with specific training and specialized skill (such as a pediatric phlebotomist).

If your hospital has a skilled pediatric blood-draw team available, the primary research supports venepuncture as the gentler and more effective option for your baby.
