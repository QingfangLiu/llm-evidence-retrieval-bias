## Bottom line

For a **full-term newborn who needs more than a single drop of blood**, the individual comparative studies generally found that **venepuncture caused less observable pain, required fewer repeat punctures, and often took less total time than heel lancing**—provided the venepuncture was performed by someone experienced with newborns.

The main exception was a study of **blood-glucose monitoring**, where only one drop was needed: venepuncture was not less painful and took longer than an automatic heel prick. ([ResearchGate][1])

## How to interpret this as a parent

**For newborn screening or a laboratory sample requiring a meaningful volume:** the studies predominantly support venepuncture, particularly when a clinician or phlebotomist experienced with neonatal veins performs it.

**For a bedside glucose check requiring one drop:** an automatic heel lance may be faster, and the available study did not demonstrate greater pain than venepuncture when oral dextrose was used. ([ResearchGate][1])

**Sweet-tasting solutions:** glucose or sucrose reduced pain with both methods. In one trial it eliminated the detectable difference, while another still favored venepuncture, so it should be viewed as an additional comfort measure rather than proof that the procedures are equivalent. ([PubMed][4])

Important limitations are that most studies were small, single-center studies; devices and pain scales differed; several studies used specially trained or particularly experienced personnel; and serious adverse events and long-term outcomes were not adequately studied. The evidence is principally about **immediate pain, crying, collection time, and repeat punctures**.

A useful question for the baby’s clinical team is: **“What volume and specimen type does this test require, and who is most experienced at neonatal venepuncture?”**
