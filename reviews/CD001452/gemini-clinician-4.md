Based on individual primary studies comparing venepuncture (drawing blood from a vein) and heel lancing in full-term neonates, the evidence points quite clearly in one direction: **venepuncture is generally superior** both in terms of minimizing the baby's pain and maximizing clinical efficiency.

Here is a breakdown of what the primary clinical trials have found:

## Pain and Infant Distress

Venepuncture is consistently shown to be a less painful procedure than heel lancing.

* **Lower Pain Scores:** In randomized controlled trials using validated assessment tools—such as the Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS) and the Premature Infant Pain Profile (PIPP)—newborns undergoing venepuncture demonstrate significantly lower pain responses than those receiving a heel prick (Cavicchiolo et al., 2022; Larsson et al., 1998).
* **Less Crying:** Babies assigned to venepuncture groups cry significantly less frequently, and for shorter durations, than those who undergo heel lancing (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005).

## Clinical Effectiveness and Efficiency

Beyond the newborn's comfort, venepuncture is frequently more effective for the clinician, provided they are trained in neonatal phlebotomy.

* **Higher First-Attempt Success:** One foundational trial found that a single puncture successfully yielded the required blood volume in 86% of neonates in the venepuncture group. In contrast, heel lancing achieved single-puncture success in only 19% to 40% of cases, depending on the type of lancet used (Larsson et al., 1998).
* **Shorter Procedure Times:** Because blood typically flows more readily from a venous draw without the need for repeated squeezing of the tissue, the overall time required to complete the sample collection is significantly shorter with venepuncture (Larsson et al., 1998; Ogawa, 2005).

## Interaction with Pain Relief (Analgesia)

Giving an oral sweet solution (like sucrose) a few minutes before the procedure is a well-known standard of care for reducing procedural pain in neonates. However, the chosen method for the blood draw still heavily influences the outcome. One trial demonstrated that even when oral sucrose is administered before a heel lance, the infant's pain response remains higher than when venepuncture is performed entirely *without* sucrose (Ogawa, 2005).

In short, if you and your clinical staff have the proper training to perform it, prioritizing a venepuncture over a heel prick will likely be significantly faster for your workflow and noticeably less distressing for your full-term patients.
