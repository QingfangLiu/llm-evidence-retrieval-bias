## Bottom line

For **healthy full-term newborns requiring a screening-sized or diagnostic blood sample**, most individual comparative studies found that **dorsal-hand venepuncture was less painful than heel lance** and often required **less time or fewer punctures**. The findings are less clear when oral glucose/sucrose is used, and they may not apply when only a single drop of blood is needed—for example, bedside glucose monitoring.

Important qualifications:

* Venepuncture performance appears highly dependent on practitioner experience.
* The trials were generally small and procedural blinding was impossible.
* “Better” also depends on the intended laboratory test. Venous blood transferred onto newborn-screening filter paper may not be analytically interchangeable with blood spotted directly from a heel lance.

I treated reviews and guidelines solely as citation-finding aids; none are included as primary evidence below.

## Individual study findings

### Studies explicitly enrolling term/full-term newborns

**Shah et al., 1997 — UK; randomized trial, n=27**

Term newborns undergoing routine blood sampling were randomized to venepuncture or heel stick. Mean procedural NIPS scores were lower with venepuncture, approximately 2.8 versus 5.2. Four heel-stick infants versus one venepuncture infant required repeat sampling because of inadequate volume or hemolysis. ([PubMed][1])

**Larsson et al., 1998 — Sweden; randomized trial, n=120**

Healthy full-term infants undergoing PKU screening were assigned to venepuncture, a small heel lancet, or a large heel lancet. Venepuncture produced lower NFCS pain scores and less crying. A usable sample was obtained with one puncture in 86% of venepunctures, compared with 19% using the small heel lancet and 40% using the large lancet. Median collection time was 191 seconds with venepuncture, versus 419 and 279 seconds with the two heel-lance devices. ([AAP Publications][2])

**Eriksson et al., 1999 — Sweden; randomized 2×2 trial, n=120**

Healthy full-term newborns were allocated to venepuncture or heel stick, with or without 1 mL of 30% oral glucose. Without glucose, venepuncture reduced crying time—26.8 versus 57.3 seconds—and PIPP scores—6.0 versus 8.4. After glucose, the between-method differences were no longer statistically significant. ([PubMed][3])

**Kvist et al., 2002 — Sweden; randomized trial, n=90 enrolled**

Healthy full-term breastfed newborns undergoing PKU screening were randomized to heel lance, small-calibre venepuncture, or large-calibre venepuncture. Small-calibre venepuncture produced significantly lower NIPS scores than heel lance at one and two minutes. Sample acquisition failed in 33% of the large-calibre venepuncture group, leaving that comparison underpowered. ([Sage Journals][4])

**Ogawa et al., 2005 — Japan; randomized double-blind 2×2 trial, n=100**

Healthy full-term newborns were assigned to venepuncture or heel lance after sucrose or water. Without sucrose, median NFCS scores were 23 with venepuncture and 58 with heel lance. Sucrose reduced pain, particularly for heel lance, but heel lance plus sucrose remained more painful than venepuncture without sucrose. Crying and procedure time also favored venepuncture. ([PMC][5])

**Saththasivam et al., 2009 — Malaysia; comparative trial, n=66**

Term newborns requiring bedside blood-glucose monitoring underwent venepuncture or heel prick. NFCS scores, crying measures, and puncture numbers did not differ significantly. Venepuncture took substantially longer—median 27 versus 7 seconds—and nonsignificant trends favored heel prick. This is the principal study not favoring venepuncture and involved collection of only one drop of blood. ([PubMed][6])

**Shrestha and Adhikari, 2012 — Nepal; comparative observational study with random assignment, n=200**

Term newborns requiring bilirubin or glucose testing were assigned to venepuncture or heel lance. Median NIPS scores were 3.5 with venepuncture and 7 with heel lance. Heel lance also produced a larger increase in heart rate and a larger fall in oxygen saturation. ([NepJol][7])

### Direct studies with a gestational-age eligibility concern

**Logan, 1999 — Australia; controlled comparative study, n=36**

Midwives at two maternity centers used either dorsal-hand venepuncture or heel prick for the newborn screening test; the accessible report does not explicitly state that all infants were full-term. Venepuncture was associated with shorter collection time, less total crying, a shorter first cry, a lower percentage of time crying, and longer cry latency. Because each center used only one technique, method effects were potentially confounded by center and practitioner effects. ([SWH Digital Repository][8])

**Correcher Medina et al., 2012 — Spain; temporally allocated observational study, n=607**

Healthy newborns of **35–42 weeks’ gestation** were allocated by birth period rather than individual randomization: 280 underwent dorsal-hand venepuncture and 327 heel puncture. Venepuncture produced lower median NIPS scores, 2 versus 5; less frequent crying, 57.8% versus 90.2%; shorter crying, approximately 58 versus 104 seconds; and shorter collection time, 60 versus 120 seconds. It also required fewer punctures, with no observed difference in TSH false-positive frequency. Because late-preterm infants were eligible and term-only results were not separately reported in the publication, its full sample should not be pooled uncritically in a term-only synthesis. ([Anales de Pediatría][9])

### Additional primary evidence concerning screening-test validity

**Lorey and Cunningham, 1994 — US newborn-screening program; three specimen cohorts totaling 253,021**

This program-level study compared direct heel-stick spotting, capillary-tube transfer, and dorsal-hand venous-blood transfer onto filter paper for PKU screening. Mean phenylalanine concentrations were progressively lower across the three methods—2.63, 2.46, and 2.28 mg/dL—and presumptive-positive rates were 23, 11, and 0 per 10,000, respectively. The investigators warned that venous blood transferred onto filter paper could miss borderline cases when cutoffs were established using directly spotted heel blood. Gestational-age eligibility was not reported, so this is ancillary rather than strictly term-only evidence. ([ScienceDirect][10])

## Interpretation for an evidence synthesis

The individual studies suggest three clinically distinct conclusions:

1. **Pain:** Venepuncture generally causes less behavioral pain and crying than heel lance when no sweet solution is used. Shah, Larsson, Kvist, Ogawa, Shrestha, Logan, and Correcher all favored venepuncture. Eriksson also favored venepuncture without glucose, but found no important difference after glucose. ([PubMed][1])

2. **Sampling efficiency:** Venepuncture often produced a sufficient screening sample faster and with fewer punctures, but this was not universal. The Malaysian glucose-monitoring study favored heel prick when only one blood drop was required, and the Kvist study showed that needle calibre and practitioner technique can materially affect success. ([AAP Publications][2])

3. **Laboratory suitability:** A less painful collection method is not necessarily analytically equivalent. For dried-blood-spot screening, the collection method and transfer procedure may alter measured analyte concentrations. Direct venous sampling for a conventional laboratory tube and venous blood transferred onto filter paper should therefore be treated as separate interventions. ([ScienceDirect][10])
