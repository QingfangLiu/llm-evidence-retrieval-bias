## Bottom line

For **full-term newborns who need more than a single drop of blood**, the direct comparative studies generally favor **venepuncture—usually from a dorsal hand vein—when performed by a trained clinician**. Across several randomized trials, venepuncture produced lower procedural-pain scores, less crying, fewer repeat punctures, and often faster completion than heel lancing. ([PubMed][1])

That conclusion is **not universal for every sampling task**:

* For **newborn-screening cards or samples requiring appreciable volume**, venepuncture appears preferable.
* For a **single bedside glucose drop**, one study found no significant pain difference and found heel puncture substantially faster. ([PubMed][2])
* Oral glucose or sucrose can reduce pain from either method and may narrow the difference, although one trial still found heel lancing more painful even when sucrose was used. ([PubMed][3])

## What the individual studies found

### Pain and crying

Most studies showed less pain with venepuncture:

* **Shah et al., 1997:** In 27 term newborns, mean Neonatal Infant Pain Scale score was **2.8 with venepuncture versus 5.2 with heel stick**. Four heel-stick babies, versus one venepuncture baby, required another sample because of insufficient volume or hemolysis. ([Nature][4])
* **Larsson et al., 1998:** Among 120 healthy full-term infants undergoing phenylketonuria screening, venepuncture produced lower facial-pain scores and less crying than either of two heel-lancet sizes. ([AAP Publications][5])
* **Eriksson et al., 1999:** Without oral glucose, venepuncture caused less crying and lower Premature Infant Pain Profile scores than heel lancing. After 30% glucose, the difference between methods was no longer statistically significant. ([PubMed][3])
* **Kvist et al., 2002:** Small-caliber venepuncture caused significantly less pain than heel lancing in healthy term breastfed newborns. A larger-caliber venepuncture technique had a high failure rate. ([Sage Journals][6])
* **Ogawa et al., 2005:** In 100 full-term newborns, median Neonatal Facial Coding System score without sucrose was **23 with venepuncture versus 58 with heel lancing**. Heel lancing with sucrose remained more painful than venepuncture without sucrose. ([PubMed][7])
* **Shrestha and Adhikari, 2012:** In 200 term newborns, median Neonatal Infant Pain Scale score was **3.5 with venepuncture versus 7 with heel lance**; heart-rate and oxygen-saturation changes also favored venepuncture. ([NepJol][8])

The principal contrary finding was the bedside-glucose study by Saththasivam et al.: pain scores did not differ significantly, and several secondary measures numerically favored heel puncture, though without statistical significance. ([PubMed][2])

### Success and procedure time

The largest efficiency difference came from Larsson et al.:

* A usable sample was obtained with **one puncture in 86% of venepunctures**, compared with **19% using the smaller heel lancet and 40% using the larger heel lancet**.
* Median completion time was **191 seconds for venepuncture**, versus **419 and 279 seconds** for the two heel-lance groups. ([AAP Publications][5])

Logan also found shorter collection time and less crying with venepuncture during newborn screening. ([SWH Digital Repository][9])

Conversely, when only one glucose drop was required, Saththasivam et al. reported a median procedure time of **27 seconds for venepuncture versus 7 seconds for heel prick**. ([PubMed][2])

### Sample adequacy and screening validity

Evidence on laboratory quality is limited:

* Shah et al. reported fewer repeat samples caused by inadequate volume or hemolysis after venepuncture. ([Nature][4])
* In the large Spanish study, venepuncture did not produce a significant difference in false-positive thyroid-stimulating-hormone screening results compared with heel puncture. That study included newborns of 35–42 weeks’ gestation and therefore was not restricted entirely to full-term infants. ([ScienceDirect][10])

## Clinical interpretation

The studies support the following practical distinction:

**Favor venepuncture when:**

* A newborn-screening card or tube must be filled.
* Multiple analytes or more than a drop of blood are needed.
* Staff are experienced in neonatal venepuncture.
* Repeated squeezing of the heel or multiple lancet attempts would otherwise be likely.

**Heel lancing remains reasonable when:**

* Only one small point-of-care glucose sample is needed.
* Venous access would predictably take longer or require repeated attempts.
* Local staff are substantially more proficient with modern automated heel devices.

Confidence is limited because most studies are relatively old, were conducted in single centers, used different lancets, needles, pain scales and sweet solutions, and could not blind the person performing the procedure. Operator skill appears especially important: the favorable venepuncture results should not be assumed to apply where neonatal venous sampling is performed infrequently. Serious adverse events were too uncommon and inadequately reported for these studies to establish comparative safety with precision.
