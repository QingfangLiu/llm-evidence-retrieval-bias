## Bottom line

For **routine laboratory or newborn-screening samples requiring several drops of blood**, venepuncture generally appears better than heel lancing in full-term newborns—provided it is performed by someone experienced in neonatal venepuncture. Most comparative studies found:

* Lower behavioral pain scores and less crying.
* Greater first-attempt success.
* Shorter collection times and fewer repeat punctures.

The important exception is **point-of-care glucose testing requiring only one drop**: one study found no significant pain difference and a substantially shorter procedure with heel prick. ([PubMed][1])

## Clinical interpretation

For a **newborn metabolic screen, bilirubin measurement, or another sample requiring appreciable blood volume**, the evidence consistently favors venepuncture for pain and usually for collection efficiency. The finding that heel lancing can take longer may seem counterintuitive, but collecting several drops commonly requires repeated squeezing of the heel, whereas successful venous access permits continuous flow. ([AAP Publications][3])

For a **single-drop bedside glucose test**, the balance may reverse: heel lance is quicker, and the available comparative study did not demonstrate greater pain. ([PubMed][1])

Oral sucrose or glucose reduces procedural pain but should not be assumed to eliminate the effect of sampling technique. In Ogawa’s trial, heel lance with sucrose was still more painful than venepuncture without sucrose, while Eriksson’s smaller trial found that glucose reduced pain sufficiently that the difference was no longer statistically detectable. ([ScienceDirect][4])

The evidence is mainly about **immediate pain, crying and sampling performance**. It provides much less information about laboratory accuracy, hemolysis rates, infection, tissue injury or long-term outcomes. Many studies were small and operator blinding was generally impossible.
