## Clinical answer

For **healthy full-term newborns requiring enough blood for laboratory testing or newborn screening**, venepuncture—usually from a dorsal hand vein and performed by an experienced clinician—was generally **less painful, required fewer punctures, and was faster overall** than heel lancing.

The important exception is **single-drop bedside glucose testing**: one nonrandomized comparative study found no pain advantage for venepuncture and found heel prick substantially faster.

I searched for direct comparative primary studies through July 2026 and excluded reviews, meta-analyses, recommendations, guidelines, opinions, and studies comparing one heel-lance device with another.

## How I would interpret this evidence

### Pain

Six of the seven studies restricted to term infants favored venepuncture for pain or distress. The exception was the small blood-glucose study in which all infants received oral dextrose and only one drop was required. That study found no pain difference. ([PubMed][6])

The sweet-solution findings are not completely uniform. Eriksson et al. found that glucose largely eliminated the difference, whereas Ogawa et al. still found heel lance plus sucrose more painful than venepuncture without sucrose. Differences in lancets, needles, operators, pain scoring, and sample volume could explain some of this discrepancy. ([PubMed][3])

### Sampling efficiency

For screening-card or larger-volume samples, venepuncture generally performed better. The clearest example is Larsson et al.: one-puncture success was 86% with venepuncture, compared with 19% and 40% with two heel-lance sizes, and collection was substantially faster. ([AAP Publications][2])

For a **single capillary glucose measurement**, heel prick was faster in Saththasivam et al. The comparison is limited because assignment depended on which designated staff member was available, and different professions performed the two procedures. ([ResearchGate][9])

### Harms and specimen quality

Adverse events were reported inconsistently and studies were too small to establish comparative safety reliably. Shah et al. reported one heel bruise and more repeat collections after heel stick. Correcher Medina et al. found no increase in abnormal screening TSH results with venous sampling, but that does not establish analytic equivalence for every test or newborn-screening program. ([Nature][1])

### Applicability and limitations

The evidence applies mainly to **healthy newborns in the first few days of life**, often having metabolic-screening samples collected. It does not necessarily apply to sick newborns, difficult venous access, repeated NICU sampling, or premature infants.

The main methodological limitations are small samples, limited reporting of allocation concealment, inability to blind the person performing the procedure, dependence on operator skill, and nonrandom or unclear allocation in several later studies. Therefore, the evidence supports venepuncture when adequate expertise is available, but it does not show that every venepuncture performed by any operator will be preferable to a properly performed automated heel lance.

## Practical conclusion

For a healthy term infant needing a **multi-drop or laboratory-volume specimen**, the individual primary studies predominantly support **skilled venepuncture** as the less painful and often more efficient method.

For **one-drop point-of-care glucose testing**, heel lance remains a reasonable and faster approach; the only study specifically examining that situation did not demonstrate less pain with venepuncture.
