## Bottom line

Blue-light-assisted TURBT—usually white-light inspection followed by blue-light inspection after intravesical hexaminolevulinate/HAL or 5-aminolevulinic acid/5-ALA—**finds additional tumors, especially flat carcinoma in situ, more consistently than white light alone**. Whether this translates into fewer future recurrences is less certain: several older randomized trials reported substantial reductions, but several other trials—including the large, contemporary PHOTO trial—found no meaningful recurrence benefit. ([PubMed][1])

Evidence that blue light prevents progression to muscle-invasive or metastatic disease is **limited and conflicting**. Complication rates generally appear similar; blue light has not been shown to make TURBT itself safer, although serious reactions to the photosensitizing drug are uncommon. ([NCBI][2])

## Complications and adverse effects

The available primary safety data do not show that blue light reduces TURBT complications:

* In the large HAL trial, urinary adverse events such as hematuria, dysuria, bladder spasm and retention were similar between groups. Serious events occurred in 39 blue-light and 32 white-light patients; only one serious event—a bladder spasm requiring prolonged hospitalization—was considered possibly related to HAL. No phototoxicity was reported. ([PMC][17])
* In the double-blind 5-ALA trial, adverse events occurred in 32.6% of 5-ALA patients and 33.9% of placebo patients. Most were typical post-TURBT urinary symptoms. ([ACS Journals][18])
* O’Brien et al. reported no adverse events attributable to HAL. ([PubMed][12])
* In PHOTO, adverse events occurred in fewer than 2% of participants and were similar between groups. There were 26 serious adverse events in total, without a significant group difference. ([PubMed][16])

Thus, blue light appears **approximately as safe as white-light TURBT**, but there is no good evidence that it prevents perforation, bleeding, infection, urinary retention or other operative complications.

## How to interpret the conflicting recurrence results

The recurrence benefit appears sensitive to the setting. Older studies often had less sophisticated white-light equipment, no immediate postoperative chemotherapy, or included patients with multiple/recurrent tumors—circumstances in which finding additional lesions may produce a larger benefit. In contrast, O’Brien and PHOTO involved experienced teams, contemporary white-light TURBT and substantial use of postoperative or risk-adapted intravesical treatment, and neither showed improved recurrence outcomes. This is a plausible interpretation across the individual trials, rather than something directly proven by a head-to-head analysis. ([BJUI][11])

For an individual patient, the most relevant issues to discuss with the surgeon are whether carcinoma in situ, multifocal disease or an incompletely visualized tumor is suspected; how experienced the surgeon and center are with both techniques; whether immediate intravesical chemotherapy is planned; and whether a repeat TURBT will be required based on stage, grade and muscle sampling. The pathology and completeness of the resection are likely to matter more than the light source alone.
