## Bottom line

Blue-light-assisted TURBT finds additional tumors—especially flat carcinoma in situ—but the randomized primary studies **do not consistently show that this translates into fewer recurrences**. For someone undergoing TURBT for a **first suspected tumor**, the two most directly applicable modern trials found no clear recurrence advantage. Several older trials and trials including recurrent or multifocal disease did report fewer recurrences.

Evidence that blue light prevents **progression to muscle-invasive cancer** is much weaker and inconsistent. The larger studies generally found no statistically significant progression benefit, although one single-center factorial trial reported fewer progressions. Major complications appear similar with blue and white light.

HAL means hexaminolevulinate; several older studies instead used 5-aminolevulinic acid, or 5-ALA. They use the same general fluorescence principle but are not identical agents.

## Most relevant primary randomized studies

### Contemporary HAL/blue-light trials

**Heer et al., 2022 — PHOTO trial, NEJM Evidence**

This pragmatic UK trial randomized 538 people with a suspected first diagnosis of intermediate- or high-risk NMIBC; 426 with confirmed NMIBC and no early cystectomy were included in the main clinical analysis.

At approximately three years, recurrence-free survival was 57.8% with photodynamic/blue-light TURBT and 61.6% with white light. The recurrence hazard ratio was 0.94, 95% CI 0.69–1.28, showing no benefit. Progression occurred in 19 blue-light and 12 white-light participants, HR 1.41, 95% CI 0.67–2.96—not statistically significant. Grade 3 or higher adverse events were uncommon and similar: 1.4% versus 2.3%. ([PubMed][1])

**Stenzl et al., 2010 — international phase III HAL trial**

This study enrolled 814 people with suspected bladder cancer; the recurrence analysis included 551 participants with Ta/T1 tumors. Nine-month recurrence was 47% after HAL blue-light TURBT versus 56% after white-light TURBT, an absolute difference of 9 percentage points, or roughly one fewer recurrence for every 11 patients treated.

Progression to muscle-invasive disease occurred in five blue-light and seven white-light patients. Any adverse event occurred in 48.0% versus 50.7%, and serious adverse events in 9.3% versus 8.4%. Only one serious event—a bladder spasm with prolonged hospitalization—was considered possibly HAL-related. ([PMC][2])

**Grossman et al., 2012 — long-term follow-up of the same Stenzl cohort**

This is not an independent second trial. At median follow-up of approximately 4.5 years, median recurrence-free survival was 16.4 months with blue light and 9.6 months with white light, p=0.04. Cystectomy occurred in 4.8% versus 7.9%, but that difference was not statistically significant, p=0.16. ([PMC][3])

A relevant subgroup observation from the original trial was that the recurrence reduction was significant among patients with previously recurrent disease—50.6% versus 61.8%—but not among those presenting with an initial cancer, 41.6% versus 48.8%. This was a subgroup analysis rather than a separately powered result. ([PMC][2])

**O’Brien et al., 2013 — newly presenting NMIBC with postoperative mitomycin**

A total of 249 patients with a newly presenting suspected tumor were randomized; 185 had NMIBC. Recurrence was not significantly different:

* At three months: 20% with HAL versus 17% with white light.
* At 12 months: 16% versus 22%.

HAL detected more occult CIS, but no HAL-related adverse events were reported. One limitation is that postoperative mitomycin was actually given less often in the HAL group—63% versus 77%—despite being part of the planned intervention. ([PubMed][4])

**Hermann et al., 2011 — randomized two-center HAL trial**

Among 233 randomized patients, 145 were eligible for the recurrence analysis. At 12 months, recurrence occurred in 30.5% after HAL-guided TURBT versus 47.3% after white-light TURBT, p=0.05. After an apparently complete white-light resection, blue light found residual tumor in 49% of blue-light-arm patients who had cancer.

False-positive findings were more frequent with photodynamic diagnosis—25% versus 16% with white light—which may lead to additional biopsies without necessarily representing a serious surgical complication. ([PubMed][5])

**Gkritsios et al., 2014 — prospective HAL trial**

This trial randomized 130 patients. HAL identified additional cancer in about 30% of evaluable blue-light patients, but recurrence-free time was not significantly different between groups over follow-up of up to 40 months, p=0.202. ([PubMed][6])

**Geavlete et al., 2010 — high-risk NMIBC and six-week repeat TURBT**

The study randomized 446 suspected cases, but its principal report focused on the 136 patients found to have high-risk NMIBC. At planned repeat TURBT six weeks later, residual or recurrent tumor was present in 11.1% after blue-light resection versus 31.2% after white-light resection. This demonstrates improved initial clearance, but six-week residual disease is not equivalent to long-term recurrence or progression. ([PubMed][7])

### Primary randomized studies using 5-ALA

**Stenzl et al., 2011 — double-blind placebo-controlled multicenter trial**

Among 370 patients with NMIBC, 12-month recurrence-free survival was 64.0% with 5-ALA versus 72.8% with placebo, p=0.22. Progression-free survival was nearly identical: 89.4% versus 89.0%. Although fluorescence detected additional lesions, it did not improve these clinical outcomes. ([PubMed][8])

**Schumacher et al., 2010 — blinded multicenter phase III trial**

Three hundred patients were randomized. Fluorescence found additional lesions within the blue-light group, but there was no difference in recurrence-free or progression-free survival at 12 months. The investigators concluded that the added fluorescence examination produced no clinical advantage in this trial. ([PubMed][9])

**Babjuk et al., 2005 — prospective randomized trial**

This smaller trial included 122 patients with primary or recurrent Ta/T1 disease. At the first follow-up cystoscopy, recurrence was found in 8% after fluorescence-guided TURBT versus 37% after white light. Recurrence-free survival was:

* 12 months: 66% versus 39%.
* 24 months: 40% versus 28%.

The strongest reported benefit was among patients with multiple or previously recurrent tumors; the primary and solitary-tumor subgroups did not show statistically significant differences. ([PubMed][10])

**Filbeck et al., 2002; Denzinger et al., 2007 — initial and eight-year reports from the same trial**

Of 301 randomized patients, 191 were included in the efficacy analysis. Residual tumor at repeat TURBT was 4.5% with fluorescence versus 25.2% with white light. At 24 months, recurrence-free survival was 89.6% versus 65.9%; the adjusted recurrence hazard ratio was 0.33.

The later follow-up reported eight-year recurrence-free survival of 71% with fluorescence versus 45% with white light. These are unusually large benefits, but the trial was single-center and more than one-third of randomized participants were excluded from the efficacy analysis, mostly because pathology did not show eligible NMIBC or cystectomy was indicated. ([PubMed][11])

**Riedl et al., 2001; Daniltchenko et al., 2005 — early and five-year reports from the same trial**

After exclusion of 13 patients with muscle-invasive disease, 102 patients remained. At six-week repeat resection, residual tumor was present in 16% after fluorescence versus 39% after white light.

The five-year report found median time to first recurrence of 12 versus five months. Five-year recurrence was 59% versus 75%; progression occurred in four versus nine patients. The study was small, and the progression difference was not presented as a definitive statistically powered result. ([PubMed][12])

**Rolevich et al., 2017 — 2×2 factorial trial of 5-ALA and postoperative doxorubicin**

This single-center trial randomized 525 patients; 377 were eligible for the primary analysis. Over median follow-up of 54.8 months, fluorescence-guided TURBT was associated with lower recurrence, HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.39–0.80, and lower progression, HR 0.33, 95% CI 0.12–0.91. Overall and bladder-cancer-specific survival did not differ. Because the progression estimate was based on relatively few events and came from one center, it requires cautious interpretation. ([PubMed][13])

## How this applies to a first suspected tumor

For a person having a **first TURBT**, I would not describe blue light as conclusively superior for preventing recurrence. The large PHOTO trial and the O’Brien newly presenting-disease trial found no recurrence benefit, while several positive studies included recurrent tumors or found their clearest benefit among patients with recurrent or multifocal disease. ([PubMed][1])

Blue light may nevertheless be valuable when the immediate goal is to find otherwise occult lesions—particularly suspected CIS, multifocal disease or poorly defined tumor margins. That potential diagnostic advantage should be distinguished from a proven ability to prevent progression or avoid cystectomy, which the trials have not consistently demonstrated. ([PubMed][4])

Regarding harms, the best comparative data show **no meaningful increase in major perioperative complications**, although false-positive fluorescence can result in extra biopsies. The practical decision therefore tends to be less about avoiding surgical complications and more about whether the additional lesion detection is likely to matter in your particular tumor pattern and whether your surgeon and center routinely use the technique. ([PMC][2])
