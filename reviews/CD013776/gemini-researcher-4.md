To answer your question strictly through the lens of primary literature, we have to separate **tumor detection** from long-term **clinical outcomes** (like recurrence and progression).

While early primary cohort studies and registries clearly established that blue-light cystoscopy (BLC) finds more tumors than white-light cystoscopy (WLC), recent high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) suggest that this increased detection may not necessarily translate into a reduction in long-term recurrence rates.

Here is a breakdown of the primary evidence regarding recurrence, detection, and complications.

## Recurrence and Progression Outcomes

Historically, the rationale for blue-light-assisted transurethral resection of a bladder tumor (TURBT) was that finding and resecting more tumor tissue would naturally reduce the chances of the cancer returning. However, recent rigorous RCT data has challenged this assumption.

The most definitive recent primary study on this topic is the **PHOTO Trial**, a large, multi-center RCT involving 538 patients with first-diagnosis, medium-to-high risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) (Heer et al., 2022).

* **Recurrence:** The trial found no significant difference in recurrence-free survival between the blue-light and white-light groups. Over a follow-up period of 3 years, there were 86 recurrences in the photodynamic diagnosis group and 84 in the white-light group (a recurrence rate of roughly 42% for both).
* **Conclusion on Efficacy:** The authors concluded that despite better diagnostic accuracy, photodynamic-guided TURBT did not reduce recurrences, nor was it considered cost-effective compared to standard white light over a 3-year horizon (Heer et al., 2022).

## Tumor Detection and Upstaging

Even if the impact on long-term recurrence is debated, primary observational and registry studies consistently confirm that BLC is vastly superior to WLC at identifying elusive malignancies, particularly flat lesions like carcinoma in situ (CIS).

* **US Multicenter Registry:** A prospective study evaluating 641 BLC procedures in 533 patients found that adding BLC to standard WLC increased the overall detection rate by 12% for papillary lesions and a striking 43% for CIS (Daneshmand et al., 2018). Importantly, lesions detected *exclusively* via blue light led to a change in recommended clinical management in 14% of cases.
* **Racial Efficacy:** A 2024 multicenter registry study analyzing 1,292 patients confirmed that this detection advantage persists across different demographics. The addition of BLC to WLC increased the overall detection rate of malignant lesions by 10% irrespective of patient race, with the highest positive predictive value observed in Asian cohorts (Ladi-Seyedian et al., 2024).
* **Residual Cancer Identification:** A retrospective study of 83 patients evaluated the use of 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) blue-light diagnosis immediately *following* a standard white-light resection. The researchers found that 47% of patients still had fluorescent lesions after the white-light procedure was ostensibly "finished." Subsequent resection of those glowing areas confirmed residual cancer in over half (51.3%) of those cases, yielding a 90.9% sensitivity for detecting residual tumors (Watanabe et al., 2021).

## Complications and Safety

The intravesical photosensitizers used to induce fluorescence under blue light (such as hexaminolevulinate or 5-ALA) are well-tolerated, and the BLC procedure does not appear to increase surgical complications compared to standard WLC.

* In the **PHOTO RCT**, the frequency of adverse events was very low and virtually identical between the two arms: 5.7% in the blue-light group versus 5.5% in the white-light group (Heer et al., 2022).
* Similarly, the **US prospective registry** reported an excellent safety profile, noting only a single case of mild dermatologic hypersensitivity (0.2%) out of hundreds of instillations, concluding that the repeated use of the photoactive compound is highly safe (Daneshmand et al., 2018).
