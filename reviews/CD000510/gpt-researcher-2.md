## Scope

I treated **prophylactic surfactant** as surfactant routinely administered at or soon after birth, before clinically established RDS, and **selective/rescue surfactant** as administration only after prespecified respiratory criteria or established RDS. I included direct comparative primary studies and companion follow-up reports. I excluded pooled analyses, reviews, guidelines, opinions, protocols, and trials comparing two rescue techniques without a prophylactic arm.

An important source of heterogeneity is that older trials frequently delayed rescue until infants required mechanical ventilation, substantial oxygen—often FiO₂ 0.40–0.60—or elevated mean airway pressure. Modern studies instead began with CPAP and used earlier, protocol-driven selective surfactant.

## Longer-term outcomes

The available follow-up reports do not show a clear long-term neurodevelopmental advantage of prophylaxis:

* In the SUPPORT follow-up, death or neurodevelopmental impairment at 18–22 months occurred in 27.9% after initial CPAP/selective surfactant and 29.9% after routine intubation/surfactant, RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.78–1.10. ([New England Journal of Medicine][12])
* School-age follow-up of one center from the Kendig trial found fewer children with an abnormal pulmonary history after prophylaxis, 22% versus 39%, but no significant differences in formal pulmonary-function testing, neurologic, cognitive, behavioral, motor, or educational outcomes. ([PubMed][13])
* Twelve-month follow-up of infants from the Merritt trial found comparable growth and neurologic/neurosensory outcomes among prophylactic, rescue, and placebo groups. ([PubMed][14])

## Overall benefit–harm interpretation

**Benefits in older trials:** Routine prophylaxis consistently improved immediate oxygenation and often reduced RDS severity, retreatment, air leak, or early respiratory-support requirements. Some large trials reported lower mortality or mortality-related composites, particularly in the most immature infants. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

**Limitations of those apparent benefits:** The selective arms often received comparatively late rescue after substantial oxygen and ventilation requirements. Antenatal steroid exposure, delivery-room CPAP, and modern noninvasive support were also less established. Thus, these studies principally answer whether surfactant at birth is better than **delayed rescue under older respiratory-management practices**.

**Modern evidence:** When effective CPAP is started immediately and surfactant is administered selectively, routine prophylactic intubation/surfactant has not reduced death, BPD, mechanical ventilation, or major neonatal morbidity. The selective strategy avoids intubation and surfactant exposure in a substantial proportion of infants. ([PubMed][8])

**Harms or burdens:** Aside from the pneumothorax signal with oropharyngeal administration and the transient one-minute Apgar finding in one older trial, major individual complications were generally not consistently increased. The clearest modern disadvantage is procedural: routine prophylaxis requires intubation and exposes infants who might remain stable on CPAP and never require surfactant.

**Inference:** The apparent reversal between eras is most plausibly related to the comparator. Prophylaxis can outperform delayed rescue in mechanically ventilated infants, but it has not outperformed immediate CPAP with timely selective surfactant in contemporary trials.
