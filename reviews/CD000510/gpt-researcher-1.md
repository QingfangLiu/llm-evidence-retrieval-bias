## Scope

I treated **prophylactic surfactant** as routine administration at or shortly after birth, before clear clinical RDS, and **selective surfactant** as administration only after respiratory distress, oxygen/ventilator criteria, or CPAP-failure criteria were met.

I included randomized and nonrandomized primary comparative studies. I excluded:

* Trials comparing surfactant with placebo or no surfactant when rescue surfactant was unavailable.
* Early-versus-late surfactant trials in which all infants already had RDS.
* Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions, editorials, and quality-improvement studies that did not directly compare the two clinical strategies.

## Main interpretation

### Older intubation-era trials

The trials conducted primarily from 1991–1996 usually intubated infants at birth and sometimes delayed selective treatment until substantial respiratory failure—for example, FiO₂ thresholds of 0.40–0.60 and mean airway pressure ≥7 cm H₂O. In this setting, prophylaxis commonly produced better early oxygenation, less severe radiographic RDS, and less ventilatory support. Kendig and Bevilacqua also reported improved survival, while Kattwinkel found fewer infants dead or requiring oxygen at 28 days. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

However, the results were not uniform. Merritt found no selective advantage of prophylaxis over rescue treatment; Egberts found better early respiratory measures but no difference in mortality, ventilation duration, pneumothorax, or other major complications; and Walti did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in survival without BPD. ([ScienceDirect][2])

### CPAP-era trials

When selective treatment was embedded in an initial-CPAP strategy, routine prophylactic intubation and surfactant did **not** improve death/BPD or other major outcomes. Initial CPAP allowed substantial proportions of infants to avoid intubation, mechanical ventilation, or surfactant exposure. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][3])

The recent POPART trial tested a different prophylactic route—surfactant placed in the oropharynx during stabilization. It did not reduce intubation and was associated with more diagnosed and treated pneumothorax. ([PubMed][4])

## Longer-term outcomes

The longer-term evidence does not show a consistent neurodevelopmental advantage from either strategy.

* In the Merritt cohort, growth, neurosensory status, and neurologic outcomes were broadly similar at 1 year, but the prophylaxis group had lower mean Bayley mental and psychomotor scores than the rescue group, and chronic lung disease was more frequent. This was a follow-up subset and the finding was not consistently reproduced elsewhere. ([PubMed][17])
* At 4.5–8 years in a subset of the Kendig trial, prophylaxis was associated with fewer reported pulmonary problems, 22% versus 39%, but formal pulmonary testing, neurologic, cognitive, behavioral, and educational outcomes did not differ. ([PubMed][18])
* In SUPPORT, death or neurodevelopmental impairment at 18–22 months was 27.9% with initial CPAP/selective surfactant and 29.9% with routine early surfactant; RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.78–1.10. Individual neurodevelopmental components also did not differ. ([New England Journal of Medicine][19])

## Benefits and harms supported by the individual studies

**Potential benefits of prophylaxis in older care settings**

* Better immediate oxygenation and less severe RDS.
* Reduced early ventilatory and oxygen requirements.
* Possible reductions in pneumothorax or PIE.
* Improved survival in some trials, especially among extremely immature infants or where rescue treatment was relatively delayed. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

**Potential disadvantages**

* Intubation and surfactant exposure for infants who would not develop clinically important RDS. In Kendig, only 58% of the selective group ultimately received surfactant; in the Vermont Oxford trial, 54% of CPAP/selective infants avoided surfactant entirely. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])
* Greater exposure to invasive ventilation when prophylaxis is coupled with routine intubation rather than rapid extubation.
* No demonstrated improvement in death/BPD or neurodevelopment in contemporary CPAP-era trials. ([New England Journal of Medicine][11])
* Route-specific harm in POPART: increased pneumothorax after prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant. ([PubMed][4])

The most important source of heterogeneity is therefore not simply **timing**, but the complete respiratory strategy: antenatal steroid exposure, whether infants were routinely intubated, CPAP availability, rescue thresholds, surfactant preparation and dose, and whether extubation occurred promptly.
