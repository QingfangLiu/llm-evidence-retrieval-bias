## Bottom line

The answer depends heavily on **how the baby is breathing immediately after birth** and on the era in which the study was performed.

For very preterm infants who are **spontaneously breathing and can be supported with nasal CPAP**, modern randomized trials found **no clear improvement in survival, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, or neurodevelopment from routinely intubating every infant to give prophylactic surfactant**. Starting with CPAP and giving surfactant selectively allowed many infants to avoid surfactant, intubation, or prolonged mechanical ventilation without worsening major outcomes. ([PubMed][1])

Older studies often favored prophylaxis, particularly for reducing early severe RDS, oxygen requirements, air leak, and—in some trials—mortality. However, these trials were conducted when infants were commonly intubated routinely, antenatal corticosteroid use was lower, and “rescue” surfactant was sometimes delayed until the infant required very high oxygen or substantial ventilator pressure. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

### What prophylactic surfactant may provide

**Faster respiratory improvement.** Several older trials found better oxygenation and less severe radiographic RDS during the first hours after birth. In the Egberts trial, prophylaxis reduced severe RDS at six hours, although it did not improve mortality or duration of respiratory support. ([PubMed][3])

**Possible reductions in air leak and mortality under older care conditions.** In Kendig’s trial of infants below 30 weeks, survival to discharge was 88% with immediate prophylaxis versus 80% with rescue treatment; among infants of 26 weeks or less, survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax was 7% versus 18%. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2]) Bevilacqua’s trial also reported lower pulmonary interstitial emphysema and mortality with prophylaxis. ([PubMed][4])

**Benefits may be greatest when rescue treatment would otherwise be delayed.** In the Merritt trial, rescue surfactant was given at an average age of approximately 220 minutes and only after relatively severe oxygen and pressure criteria were reached. Despite this delay, the intention-to-treat analysis still showed no clear advantage of prophylaxis over rescue therapy. ([PubMed][5])

### Harms and disadvantages of prophylaxis

**Some infants receive an invasive procedure they may never need.** In the three-arm Dunn trial, 48% of infants assigned to initial CPAP avoided intubation and mechanical ventilation, and 54% avoided surfactant entirely. Major outcomes were not significantly worse than with prophylactic surfactant strategies. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][6])

**More exposure to mechanical ventilation.** In SUPPORT, death or BPD occurred in 47.8% with initial CPAP/selective treatment and 51.0% with routine intubation and early surfactant—a nonsignificant difference. Infants started on CPAP were less likely to require intubation or postnatal corticosteroids, spent fewer days mechanically ventilated, and were more likely to be alive without ventilation on day seven. Other major neonatal adverse outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][1])

**No demonstrated long-term neurodevelopmental advantage.** In the SUPPORT follow-up, death or neurodevelopmental impairment at 18–22 months occurred in 27.9% after initial CPAP versus 29.9% after early intubation/surfactant, with no statistically significant difference. ([New England Journal of Medicine][7]) The older Merritt trial follow-up similarly found comparable growth, neurologic, and neurosensory outcomes at one year. ([PubMed][8])

**Administration-related effects are possible.** Kattwinkel’s trial reported lower one-minute Apgar scores after prophylaxis, although the difference disappeared by five minutes and there was no increase in asphyxia-related complications. ([PubMed][9]) In the recent POPART trial, an experimental approach—placing surfactant in the oropharynx while providing CPAP—did not reduce intubation and was associated with more treated pneumothorax: 16.6% versus 6.4%. This result applies to that particular route and should not be generalized to standard intratracheal or thin-catheter administration. ([PubMed][10])

## How to interpret this for an individual baby

For a **spontaneously breathing infant who can be stabilized on CPAP**, the modern trials support beginning noninvasively and giving surfactant only if prespecified signs of worsening RDS develop. This does not appear to sacrifice survival or neurodevelopment and may avoid unnecessary intubation and ventilation.

For a baby who **cannot breathe adequately, needs extensive resuscitation, or is already intubated**, the major disadvantage of prophylaxis—performing an otherwise avoidable intubation—is no longer the same. The older prophylaxis trials may therefore be more relevant to that situation, although current surfactant products, administration techniques, antenatal steroid exposure, and respiratory care differ substantially from those trials.

The applicable evidence also changes with gestational age. A 24-week infant, a 28-week infant on increasing oxygen, and a 31-week infant breathing comfortably on CPAP have very different baseline probabilities of needing surfactant. The useful questions for the NICU team are the baby’s current oxygen requirement, work of breathing, blood gases, CPAP pressure, whether intubation is already required, and the unit’s criteria and technique for selective surfactant.

## Individual primary studies

### Direct randomized comparisons

1. **Kendig JW, et al.** *A Comparison of Surfactant as Immediate Prophylaxis and as Rescue Therapy in Newborns of Less Than 30 Weeks’ Gestation.* **N Engl J Med.** 1991;324:865–871. Immediate calf-lung surfactant versus rescue treatment; prophylaxis improved survival and reduced pneumothorax, particularly at 26 weeks or less. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

2. **Merritt TA, et al.** *Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Trial of Human Surfactant Given at Birth Versus Rescue Administration in Very Low Birth Weight Infants With Lung Immaturity.* **J Pediatr.** 1991;118:581–594. Three-arm trial; surfactant improved early respiratory physiology compared with placebo, but prophylaxis had no selective advantage over rescue therapy in intention-to-treat analysis. ([PubMed][5])

3. **Egberts J, et al.** *Comparison of Prophylaxis and Rescue Treatment With Curosurf in Neonates Less Than 30 Weeks’ Gestation: A Randomized Trial.* **Pediatrics.** 1993;92:768–774. Prophylaxis improved six-hour oxygenation and reduced severe RDS, but did not improve mortality, ventilation duration, oxygen duration, or major complications. ([PubMed][3])

4. **Kattwinkel J, et al.** *Prophylactic Administration of Calf Lung Surfactant Extract Is More Effective Than Early Treatment of Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates of 29 Through 32 Weeks’ Gestation.* **Pediatrics.** 1993;92:90–98. Among 1,248 analyzed infants, prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS, retreatment, early ventilation/oxygen exposure, and death or oxygen requirement at 28 days. ([PubMed][9])

5. **Bevilacqua G, Parmigiani S, Robertson B.** *Prophylaxis of Respiratory Distress Syndrome by Treatment With Modified Porcine Surfactant at Birth: A Multicentre Prospective Randomized Trial.* **J Perinat Med.** 1996;24:609–620. In 287 infants at 24–30 weeks, prophylactic poractant reduced severe RDS, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, and mortality under the treatment conditions of that era. ([PubMed][4])

6. **Tianjin study investigators.** *Protective and Curative Effects of Prophylactic Administration of Pulmonary Surfactant on Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome.* 2006. Randomized 100 infants below 32 weeks and 1,500 g to prophylactic versus treatment-after-RDS poractant; reported better early gas exchange with prophylaxis. ([PubMed][11])

7. **Sandri F, et al.** *Prophylactic or Early Selective Surfactant Combined With nCPAP in Very Preterm Infants.* **Pediatrics.** 2010;125:e1402–e1409. In 208 infants at 25–28 weeks who did not require immediate intubation, prophylaxis did not reduce mechanical ventilation, death, BPD, or major prematurity-related morbidity compared with CPAP and early selective surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][12])

8. **SUPPORT Study Group; Finer NN, et al.** *Early CPAP Versus Surfactant in Extremely Preterm Infants.* **N Engl J Med.** 2010;362:1970–1979. In 1,316 infants at 24–27 weeks, initial CPAP/selective treatment produced similar death-or-BPD outcomes and less invasive ventilation exposure than routine early intubation and surfactant. ([PubMed][1])

9. **Dunn MS, et al.** *Randomized Trial Comparing 3 Approaches to the Initial Respiratory Management of Preterm Neonates.* **Pediatrics.** 2011;128:e1069–e1076. Compared prophylactic surfactant plus ventilation, prophylactic surfactant with rapid extubation, and initial CPAP/selective surfactant; no significant major-outcome advantage for prophylaxis, while initial CPAP allowed many infants to avoid intubation or surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][6])

10. **Murphy MC, et al.** *Prophylactic Oropharyngeal Surfactant for Preterm Newborns at Birth: A Randomized Clinical Trial.* **JAMA Pediatr.** 2024. POPART trial of 251 infants below 29 weeks; oropharyngeal prophylaxis did not reduce intubation and was associated with more pneumothorax. ([PubMed][10])

### Primary follow-up studies

11. **Vaucher YE, et al.** *Outcome at Twelve Months of Adjusted Age in Very Low Birth Weight Infants With Lung Immaturity: A Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Trial of Human Surfactant.* **J Pediatr.** 1993;122:126–132. One-year growth and neurodevelopment were similar after prophylactic, rescue, or placebo treatment in the original Merritt trial. ([PubMed][8])

12. **Sinkin RA, et al.** *School-Age Follow-up of Prophylactic Versus Rescue Surfactant Trial: Pulmonary, Neurodevelopmental, and Educational Outcomes.* **Pediatrics.** 1998;101:E11. Fewer children in the prophylaxis group had an abnormal respiratory history, but formal lung function and neurodevelopmental and educational outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][13])

13. **Vaucher YE, et al.** *Neurodevelopmental Outcomes in the Early CPAP and Pulse Oximetry Trial.* **N Engl J Med.** 2012;367:2495–2504. SUPPORT follow-up showing no significant difference in death or neurodevelopmental impairment between initial CPAP/selective treatment and early intubation/surfactant. ([New England Journal of Medicine][7])

14. **SUPPORT respiratory follow-up investigators.** *Respiratory Outcomes of the Surfactant Positive Pressure and Oximetry Randomized Trial.* **J Pediatr.** 2014;165:240–249.e4. Primary follow-up found similar principal respiratory symptoms, with some secondary respiratory-health measures favoring the initial-CPAP group. ([sciencedirect.com][14])
