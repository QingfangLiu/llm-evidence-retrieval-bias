## What the individual trials show

The answer depends heavily on **how the baby is doing immediately after birth**.

For a preterm infant who is breathing and can be stabilized with continuous positive airway pressure—**CPAP**—the modern randomized trials found that routinely intubating every infant to give prophylactic surfactant did **not** improve survival or reduce bronchopulmonary dysplasia compared with CPAP followed by surfactant only when respiratory distress met treatment criteria. Selective treatment also allowed many infants to avoid intubation, mechanical ventilation, and surfactant altogether. ([PubMed][1])

Older trials sometimes found greater benefits from prophylaxis, including better early oxygenation, less severe RDS, fewer air leaks, and—in some trials—better survival. However, those studies were conducted when routine delivery-room intubation was common, antenatal corticosteroid use was lower, and “selective” surfactant was often withheld until RDS had become relatively severe. Their results therefore do not transfer directly to contemporary CPAP-based care. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

### Most applicable modern randomized trials

**SUPPORT trial, 2010 — 1,316 infants born at 24–27 weeks.** Infants were assigned either to intubation and surfactant within one hour or to CPAP begun in the delivery room, with intubation and surfactant subsequently used when required. Death or BPD occurred in 51.0% of the routine-surfactant group and 47.8% of the CPAP group; this was not a statistically significant difference. Infants assigned to CPAP had fewer days of mechanical ventilation and were less likely to receive postnatal corticosteroids. ([PubMed][1])

**CURPAP trial, Sandri et al., 2010 — 208 infants born at 25–28 weeks.** All were spontaneously breathing and initially supported with CPAP. Immediate prophylactic surfactant did not reduce mechanical ventilation during the first five days compared with CPAP and early selective surfactant: 31.4% versus 33.0%. Mortality and major neonatal complications were also similar. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][3])

**VON Delivery Room Management Trial, Dunn et al., 2011 — 648 infants born at 26–29 weeks.** Three strategies were compared: prophylactic surfactant followed by ventilation, prophylactic surfactant followed by rapid extubation to CPAP, and CPAP with selective surfactant. Death or BPD did not differ significantly. In the CPAP/selective group, approximately 48% avoided intubation and mechanical ventilation and 54% avoided surfactant. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][4])

**POPART trial, 2024 — 251 infants born before 29 weeks.** This tested a different prophylactic approach: surfactant placed in the oropharynx at birth while CPAP was provided, rather than routine endotracheal administration. It did not reduce intubation by 120 hours—63.5% versus 64.8%—and pneumothorax was more frequent with prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant, 16.6% versus 6.4%. ([PubMed][5])

### Findings from the older endotracheal-surfactant trials

Results were mixed:

* **Kendig et al., 1991:** Survival to discharge was 88% with prophylaxis versus 80% with rescue treatment. Among infants born at or before 26 weeks, survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax was less frequent, 7% versus 18%. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])
* **Merritt et al., 1991:** Prophylactic surfactant improved respiratory measurements shortly after birth, but the intention-to-treat analysis did not establish a significant overall advantage over rescue administration. Rescue treatment occurred at a mean age of about 220 minutes and required substantial oxygen and airway-pressure criteria, making it later than many contemporary selective-treatment protocols. ([PubMed][6])
* **Egberts et al., 1993:** Prophylaxis improved oxygenation and reduced severe RDS, but mortality, pneumothorax, brain injury, duration of ventilation, and duration of oxygen therapy were not significantly different. ([PubMed][7])
* **Kattwinkel et al., 1993:** Among infants born at 29–32 weeks, prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS, retreatment, early respiratory support, and the composite of death or oxygen use at 28 days. The benefit was concentrated among the smaller and less mature infants. One-minute Apgar scores were lower after prophylactic treatment, but the difference had disappeared by five minutes. ([PubMed][8])
* **Walti et al., 1995:** Prophylaxis reduced early oxygen and ventilatory support. Survival without BPD was numerically higher but not statistically conclusive. Severe intraventricular hemorrhage and retinopathy of prematurity were less common, although these were secondary outcomes in a relatively small trial. ([EBSCO OpenURL][9])
* **Bevilacqua et al., 1996:** Prophylaxis reduced severe RDS, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, oxygen requirements, and mortality in infants born at 24–30 weeks. ([PubMed][10])

## Practical interpretation

For a baby who is **spontaneously breathing and stable enough for CPAP**, the most applicable randomized evidence supports beginning with noninvasive respiratory support and giving surfactant **selectively and early if prespecified signs of RDS develop**, rather than intubating solely to administer prophylactic surfactant. “Selective” in contemporary care generally does not mean waiting until the baby is critically ill; the trials used respiratory-failure, oxygen-requirement, apnea, or ventilation criteria to trigger escalation. ([PubMed][1])

For a baby who **already requires intubation during resuscitation**, the trade-off is different: the major disadvantage of prophylaxis—performing an otherwise avoidable intubation—has already occurred. The prophylaxis-versus-CPAP trials do not directly answer whether surfactant should be withheld from an already-intubated, extremely preterm infant.

Gestational age, antenatal corticosteroid exposure, oxygen requirement, work of breathing, apnea, and whether the baby needs intubation are therefore central to applying this evidence to an individual infant.
