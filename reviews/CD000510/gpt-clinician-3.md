## Clinical bottom line

The apparent benefit of prophylactic surfactant depends heavily on what it is compared with.

In older trials, “selective” surfactant was often delayed until infants required mechanical ventilation, an FiO₂ of 0.40–0.60, or substantial mean airway pressure. Against that comparator, prophylaxis frequently reduced RDS severity and early respiratory support, and some trials reported lower mortality or air leak.

In contemporary trials using delivery-room CPAP and relatively early selective surfactant, routine prophylaxis has **not demonstrated a clear reduction in death, BPD, or mechanical ventilation**. The selective strategy allows many infants to avoid surfactant and endotracheal intubation altogether.

## Contemporary CPAP-era trials

### SUPPORT trial — extremely preterm infants

The SUPPORT trial randomized 1,316 infants born at 24+0 through 27+6 weeks to:

* Intubation and surfactant within 1 hour, or
* Delivery-room CPAP, with intubation and surfactant only when protocol criteria were met.

Death or BPD occurred in 51.0% of the prophylactic-surfactant group and 47.8% of the CPAP/selective group; adjusted relative risk for CPAP versus prophylactic surfactant was 0.95, 95% CI 0.85–1.05. CPAP-assigned infants were less likely to be intubated, received fewer postnatal corticosteroids, and had fewer days of mechanical ventilation. Other major neonatal outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][1])

**Interpretation:** Routine intubation and prophylactic surfactant did not improve survival without BPD over CPAP-first selective treatment.

### CURPAP trial

CURPAP included 208 spontaneously breathing infants born at 25–28 weeks who had not required intubation during initial stabilization. Infants received either prophylactic surfactant followed by nasal CPAP or nasal CPAP with early selective surfactant.

Mechanical ventilation within five days occurred in 31.4% with prophylaxis versus 33.0% with selective treatment, RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.64–1.41. Mortality, major morbidity, and survival in room air at 36 weeks were also similar. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][2])

**Interpretation:** Prophylaxis added to CPAP did not reduce subsequent ventilation.

### Vermont Oxford Network delivery-room trial

This 648-infant trial at 26+0 through 29+6 weeks compared:

1. Prophylactic surfactant followed by ventilation
2. Prophylactic surfactant with rapid extubation to bubble CPAP
3. Initial bubble CPAP with selective surfactant

Compared with prophylactic surfactant and ventilation, the relative risk of death or BPD was 0.78, 95% CI 0.59–1.03, with prophylactic surfactant/rapid extubation and 0.83, 95% CI 0.64–1.09, with initial CPAP. Neither difference was statistically significant. In the CPAP-first arm, 48% avoided intubation and mechanical ventilation, and 54% avoided surfactant. The study ended early because enrollment declined. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][3])

**Interpretation:** CPAP-first did not worsen death/BPD and prevented substantial exposure to intubation and surfactant.

### POPART trial — prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant

POPART randomized 251 infants born before 29 weeks to prophylactic oropharyngeal surfactant plus CPAP or CPAP alone.

Intubation within 120 hours occurred in 63.5% versus 64.8%, RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.81–1.18. Treated pneumothorax was more frequent after oropharyngeal surfactant: 16.6% versus 6.4%. Other respiratory and neonatal outcomes were generally similar. ([JAMA Network][4])

**Interpretation:** This non-intubation prophylactic technique did not prevent intubation and produced a pneumothorax signal. Because the route was oropharyngeal rather than standard intratracheal or thin-catheter administration, this harm should not automatically be generalized to every surfactant technique.

## Earlier trials comparing prophylaxis with delayed rescue

### Kendig trial

Among 479 infants born before 30 weeks, immediate calf-lung surfactant was compared with rescue surfactant once oxygen or ventilator thresholds were reached.

Survival to hospital discharge was 88% with prophylaxis versus 80% with rescue. Among infants at or below 26 weeks, survival was 75% versus 54%, and pneumothorax was 7% versus 18%. Only 58% of infants assigned to rescue ultimately received surfactant. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

This is one of the strongest older studies favoring prophylaxis, particularly among the most immature infants. Its rescue threshold was considerably later than CPAP-first selective treatment in modern studies.

### Merritt trial

This trial enrolled 246 infants at 24–29 weeks and compared prophylactic human surfactant, rescue surfactant, and—in singleton infants—an air placebo. Rescue treatment was given at a mean age of approximately 220 minutes after fairly severe oxygen and ventilation criteria developed.

Surfactant improved oxygenation and ventilator requirements, but the intention-to-treat comparison did not demonstrate a significant clinical advantage of prophylaxis over rescue treatment. At one year, growth, neurosensory outcomes, and neurologic outcomes were similar between groups. ([PubMed][6])

### Egberts trial

In 147 infants born at 26–29 weeks, prophylactic poractant alfa within 10 minutes was compared with rescue treatment when infants had established RDS requiring mechanical ventilation and an FiO₂ of at least 0.60.

Prophylaxis improved early oxygenation, reduced severe RDS from 32% to 19%, and reduced subsequent qualification for rescue surfactant from 32% to 11%. It did not significantly reduce mortality, pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, IVH, PVL, PDA, or duration of ventilation or oxygen. In subgroup analysis, antenatal corticosteroid exposure reduced RDS at least as effectively as prophylactic surfactant. ([PubMed][7])

### Kattwinkel trial

This large trial randomized 1,398 infants born at 29–32 weeks; 1,248 were included in the reported analysis. Prophylaxis reduced moderate RDS from 12% to 7%, repeat surfactant treatment from 9% to 5%, and the composite of death or oxygen use at 28 days from 9% to 5%. Benefits were concentrated mainly among infants at or below 30 weeks or under 1,500 g. In the selective arm, 43% received surfactant, generally at a median age of approximately 1.5 hours. ([PubMed][8])

### Walti trial

Among 256 infants born at 25–31 weeks, prophylaxis reduced early oxygen and ventilator requirements. Survival without BPD was 60% with prophylaxis versus 46% with rescue, but the confidence interval included no effect: OR 1.53, 95% CI 0.90–2.61. Severe peri/intraventricular hemorrhage and retinopathy of prematurity were less frequent with prophylaxis, although these secondary findings have not been consistently reproduced in later trials. ([EBSCO OpenURL][9])

### Bevilacqua trial

This multicenter trial enrolled 287 infants born at 24–30 weeks. Prophylaxis reduced severe RDS by approximately 32%, pulmonary interstitial emphysema from 14% to 7%, mortality from 35% to 21%, and a composite unfavorable outcome from 58% to 41%. ([PubMed][10])

Again, the selective strategy required clinical RDS and mechanical ventilation before treatment, making it more delayed than modern selective administration.

## Benefits and harms by strategy

### Potential benefits of prophylaxis

Prophylaxis can establish surfactant activity before substantial atelectasis and lung injury occur. In older trials with delayed rescue, it reduced severe RDS, early oxygen exposure, ventilator requirements, and sometimes pneumothorax or mortality. The largest apparent benefit was generally in extremely immature infants and in settings where rescue treatment was delayed. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

### Potential harms or disadvantages of prophylaxis

The most consistent disadvantage is **overtreatment**. Depending on the study, approximately half of selectively managed infants never needed surfactant. Prophylaxis may therefore expose infants who would stabilize on CPAP to surfactant administration and, with traditional delivery, endotracheal intubation. ([American Academy of Pediatrics][3])

Routine intubation also increases exposure to positive-pressure ventilation. In SUPPORT, the CPAP/selective group had fewer days of ventilation without worse death/BPD outcomes. ([PubMed][1])

POPART identified increased pneumothorax with prophylactic oropharyngeal administration, although this finding is route-specific and requires replication. ([JAMA Network][4])

Available trials have not shown a consistent long-term neurodevelopmental advantage of prophylaxis; the Merritt follow-up found similar one-year outcomes. ([PubMed][11])

### Potential harm of selective treatment

Selective treatment can be harmful when the threshold is so high that a genuinely surfactant-deficient infant remains untreated through prolonged oxygen exposure, atelectasis, or mechanical ventilation. The older studies favoring prophylaxis frequently used rescue thresholds of FiO₂ 0.40–0.60 plus ventilator-pressure requirements. It is therefore reasonable to infer that part of the historical prophylaxis benefit represented a benefit of **earlier surfactant**, rather than a requirement to treat every at-risk infant before RDS could be assessed. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

## Overall interpretation

For an infant who is breathing spontaneously and can be stabilized with CPAP, the contemporary individual trials do not show a clinically important advantage from routine prophylactic surfactant over close monitoring with early selective administration. Selective treatment substantially decreases unnecessary intubation and surfactant exposure.

The older evidence remains relevant when early CPAP, rapid RDS assessment, or prompt surfactant administration is unavailable. In that situation, “selective” treatment can effectively become late rescue, and prophylaxis may outperform it.

A directly relevant trial of prophylactic versus selective surfactant administered by LISA—pro.LISA—has been published as a protocol, but I found no posted outcome results in the trial registry, so it is not included as completed efficacy evidence. ([PubMed][12])
