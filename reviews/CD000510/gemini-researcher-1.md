The choice between prophylactic and selective surfactant administration centers on a critical clinical trade-off: securing early lung function versus avoiding the trauma of unnecessary endotracheal intubation.

Historically, prophylactic surfactant was considered the gold standard for extremely premature infants. However, modern neonatal practices—specifically the widespread use of antenatal maternal steroids and early non-invasive continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)—have largely shifted the paradigm toward selective rescue therapy.

Here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms of each approach based on primary clinical studies, excluding all systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

## Prophylactic Surfactant Administration

**The Approach:** Endotracheal intubation and surfactant administration immediately or shortly after birth (typically within 10 to 30 minutes) for preterm infants judged to be at high risk for respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), prior to the onset of severe symptoms.

### Benefits

* **Early Lung Stabilization:** In the era before routine delivery-room CPAP, early randomized trials found that prophylactic administration significantly improved immediate oxygenation, decreased the severity of radiologically verified RDS, and reduced the need for prolonged high-concentration oxygen compared to expectant rescue management (Egberts et al., 1993).
* **Reduced Ventilation Time in Extremely Preterm Infants:** Even in slightly more modern cohorts, some observational data suggests that for the most extremely premature infants (under 27 weeks gestation), prophylactic surfactant can significantly shorten the total duration of mechanical ventilation compared to selective treatment (Koch et al., 2010).

### Harms

* **Unnecessary Intubation and Ventilation:** The most significant harm is overtreatment. Prophylactic protocols require intubating all infants below a certain gestational age. This exposes a substantial percentage of infants who would have breathed adequately on their own to the airway trauma of intubation and the barotrauma of mechanical ventilation (Dunn et al., 2011).

## Selective (Rescue) Surfactant Administration

**The Approach:** Providing initial respiratory support (usually via non-invasive nasal CPAP) and administering surfactant only if the infant develops clinical or radiographic signs of established RDS.

### Benefits

* **Avoidance of Invasive Procedures:** Modern randomized controlled trials demonstrate that when infants are initially managed with nasal CPAP and given surfactant only selectively, nearly half can avoid intubation and surfactant treatment entirely. Importantly, this selective approach results in similar rates of survival and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) as prophylactic intubation (Dunn et al., 2011).
* **Equivalence in the Modern Era:** Recent studies reflecting current practices (high antenatal steroid use and early CPAP) have found no significant differences in the incidence of BPD, death, or other adverse neonatal outcomes when comparing early selective rescue therapy to early prophylactic therapy, suggesting the historical benefits of prophylaxis have diminished (Chun et al., 2017).

### Harms

* **Historical Risk of Pulmonary Complications:** If selective treatment is delayed until severe respiratory failure occurs, the infant may suffer higher rates of lung damage. School-age follow-ups of older trials showed that infants who received delayed rescue surfactant had higher rates of long-term clinical pulmonary complications compared to the prophylactic group, though neurodevelopmental and educational outcomes were identical (Sinkin et al., 1998).
