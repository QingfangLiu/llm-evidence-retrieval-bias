When synthesizing the primary literature on surfactant administration in preterm infants at risk of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), the evidence splits cleanly into two distinct eras: the period before early nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP) became the standard of care, and the modern era where early non-invasive stabilization is preferred.

Because you are excluding aggregate and secondary literature, the benefits and harms discussed below are drawn exclusively from foundational and modern primary randomized controlled trials and cohort studies.

## The Case for Prophylactic Administration

Historically, prophylactic administration—intubating and delivering surfactant within the first minutes of life before the onset of respiratory insufficiency—was shown to be superior to waiting for clinical signs of RDS.

**Benefits:**
The theoretical advantage of prophylaxis is that surfactant distributes more uniformly into lungs that are still fluid-filled, preventing the initial atelectasis (alveolar collapse) and epithelial damage caused by the first mechanical breaths.

* **Improved Survival & Fewer Air Leaks:** A foundational multicenter trial by Kendig et al. (1991) demonstrated that prophylactic surfactant significantly improved survival to discharge compared to selective rescue therapy (88% vs. 80%). This benefit was particularly pronounced in extremely preterm infants (≤ 26 weeks’ gestation), who also experienced a significantly lower rate of pneumothorax (7% vs. 18%).
* **Reduced Ventilation Duration:** Some center-specific observational data supported this trend even later on. A cohort study by Koch et al. (2010) examining infants under 27 weeks’ gestation found that prophylactic administration reduced the average duration of mechanical ventilation by nearly 100 hours per patient compared to a historical selective-treatment cohort.

**Harms:**
The primary harm of the prophylactic approach is the requirement for universal endotracheal intubation. This exposes infants to immediate barotrauma and the mechanical risks of the intubation procedure itself, effectively over-treating neonates who might never have progressed to severe RDS. Interestingly, an early trial by Dunn et al. (1991) noted that while prophylactic surfactant lowered the incidence of severe chronic lung disease compared to a placebo control, the early-treatment group actually experienced a *higher* incidence of mild chronic lung disease compared to the selective rescue group.

## The Case for Selective (Rescue) Administration

As delivery room stabilization techniques evolved to favor early nCPAP, the risk-benefit calculus shifted. Modern primary trials tested whether the benefits of prophylactic surfactant still held when the selective group was supported non-invasively from birth.

**Benefits:**
The defining benefit of a selective strategy is that it spares a substantial proportion of preterm infants from intubation entirely.

* **Avoidance of Intubation:** In the Dunn et al. (1991) trial, nearly half (48%) of the neonates randomized to the late/selective group never met the respiratory failure criteria to require surfactant, successfully avoiding the intervention altogether. These un-intubated infants achieved earlier hospital discharge.
* **Equivalency Under nCPAP:** The CURAPAP trial (Sandri et al., 2010) explicitly compared prophylactic surfactant followed by nCPAP versus early nCPAP alone with selective rescue surfactant. They found no significant difference in the need for mechanical ventilation in the first five days of life (31.4% in the prophylactic group vs. 33.0% in the selective group). Survival rates in room air at 36 weeks were nearly identical (78.1% vs. 78.6%).

**Harms:**
The potential harm of selective administration is that if an infant *does* fail nCPAP, their lungs may have already suffered sheer stress and early atelectrauma before the rescue surfactant is finally administered, potentially reducing the efficacy of the drug compared to a pristine, unventilated lung.

---

## Summary of Clinical Trade-offs

| Strategy | Primary Benefits | Primary Harms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Prophylactic** | Maximizes uniform drug distribution; historically lowered mortality and pneumothorax rates in highly premature cohorts. | Forces universal endotracheal intubation; guarantees over-treatment of infants who would not develop severe RDS. |
| **Selective** | Prevents unnecessary intubation in up to 50% of at-risk infants; highly synergistic with early nCPAP protocols. | Delays therapeutic intervention for infants who eventually fail non-invasive support. |
