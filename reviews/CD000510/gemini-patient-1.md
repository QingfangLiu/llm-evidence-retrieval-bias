Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) happens when a premature baby's lungs haven't yet produced enough of their own surfactant, which is a slippery substance that keeps the tiny air sacs in the lungs open.

Based strictly on primary clinical trials (excluding reviews and guidelines as you requested), here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms of the two main strategies for giving surfactant.

### Understanding the Two Approaches

* **Prophylactic (Preventive) Use:** Administering surfactant immediately at or shortly after birth to a premature baby *before* they show signs of breathing trouble. Because surfactant is delivered directly into the lungs, this almost always requires placing a breathing tube (intubation).
* **Selective (Rescue) Use:** Placing the baby on non-invasive breathing support immediately (like CPAP, which uses a mask or nasal prongs to provide gentle air pressure) and monitoring them closely. Surfactant is only given if the baby starts showing clear signs of RDS.

---

### What Primary Studies Show: Benefits and Harms

#### 1. The Case for Prophylactic Use (The Historical Perspective)

In the early 1990s, when surfactant first became widely used and non-invasive breathing support was less advanced, primary studies strongly supported giving surfactant preventively.

* **Benefits:** A landmark trial found that giving surfactant immediately as prophylaxis to very preterm babies resulted in higher survival rates compared to waiting until the baby was very sick (Kendig et al., 1991). Another major study showed that prophylactic treatment decreased babies' oxygen and ventilator requirements during their first week of life, and significantly lowered the risk of pulmonary air leaks, such as a pneumothorax (Dunn et al., 1991).
* **Harms:** The main drawback is that it requires intubation, which is invasive and carries a risk of airway trauma. Furthermore, because it is given before symptoms appear, a significant percentage of babies receive an invasive procedure and medication they might never have actually needed. In one trial, nearly half of the babies in the "wait-and-see" group ended up having healthy enough lungs to avoid surfactant entirely (Dunn et al., 1991).

#### 2. The Shift to Selective Use (The Modern Perspective)

Over the last couple of decades, neonatal care advanced significantly—especially with the routine use of non-invasive CPAP in the delivery room. This prompted researchers to test if the "wait-and-see" approach was safer now than it used to be.

* **Benefits:** A large, modern trial looked at premature babies stabilized on early CPAP. It found that giving prophylactic surfactant was *not* superior to just using CPAP and only giving surfactant selectively if the baby struggled (Sandri et al., 2010). Survival rates and the rates of major premature lung complications were practically identical between the two groups (Sandri et al., 2010). The enormous benefit of the selective approach is that it successfully avoids unnecessary intubations for babies who can adapt using just mild CPAP pressure.
* **Harms:** The primary theoretical risk of the selective approach is that if a baby *does* develop severe RDS, they have to work harder to breathe for a period before the medical team intervenes. However, trials showed that with the close monitoring standard in modern NICUs, giving the medication slightly later did not increase the incidence of severe complications (Sandri et al., 2010).

### Summary

The primary literature highlights a clear evolution in NICU practices. While prophylactic surfactant was proven to be a crucial lifesaver in the past for preventing severe lung complications (Dunn et al., 1991; Kendig et al., 1991), modern clinical trials show that starting with gentle CPAP and using surfactant *only* if the baby shows signs of distress (selective use) offers the same protective benefits while sparing many babies from unnecessary invasive procedures (Sandri et al., 2010).

Your neonatologist will be weighing this data against your baby’s specific gestational age, weight, and how they sounded immediately at birth to determine the safest path forward for their lungs.
