Here's what the primary-study literature shows on prophylactic vs. selective surfactant for RDS. This spans two "generations" of trials — older ones (1990s) from before routine antenatal steroids and CPAP-first management, and newer ones (2009–2023) conducted in the CPAP/LISA era — and the conclusions differ somewhat between eras.

**Early-era trials (pre-antenatal-steroid-era, mechanical ventilation as default)**

These RCTs randomized intubated preterm infants to receive surfactant either immediately at birth (prophylaxis) or only after RDS was diagnosed clinically/radiographically (rescue/selective). Pooled across this generation, prophylaxis was generally associated with lower rates of pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, and mortality, and less bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) or death, with no major offsetting harms identified. Individual trials (e.g., Kattwinkel 1993) reported less moderate RDS, less need for retreatment, and less need for ventilatory support with prophylaxis, though 1-minute Apgar scores were lower (a difference that resolved by 5 minutes).

**Modern-era trials (CPAP-first management, high antenatal steroid use)**

Once nasal CPAP became standard initial support and antenatal corticosteroid use was near-universal, the calculus changed. Trials like CURPAP (Sandri 2010) and the NICHD SUPPORT trial (2010) found that prophylactic surfactant was *not* superior to an early-CPAP-with-selective-surfactant strategy for reducing mechanical ventilation, air leak, BPD, or death — and prophylactic surfactant necessarily requires intubating some infants who would never have needed it. The Rojas 2009 trial similarly supports "surfactant without mandatory ventilation" (give it, then extubate quickly to CPAP) rather than true prophylaxis in all comers. The very recent pro.LISA trial extends this question to prophylactic vs. selective *less-invasive* surfactant administration (LISA), avoiding intubation in both arms.

**Net picture:** In infants managed with early mechanical ventilation, prophylaxis reduced mortality and air-leak complications. In infants managed with early CPAP and minimally-invasive techniques, selective/early-rescue surfactant achieves similar outcomes without the downside of unnecessary intubation — so most contemporary practice has shifted toward selective (early rescue) surfactant guided by FiO₂/CPAP failure thresholds, reserving prophylaxis mainly for infants requiring intubation for other reasons (e.g., resuscitation).

A network meta-analysis (not included below since it's a review) found no clear mortality difference between plausible FiO₂ thresholds for triggering selective surfactant, which is consistent with this shift.
