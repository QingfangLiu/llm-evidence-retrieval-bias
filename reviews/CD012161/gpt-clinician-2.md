## Scope

I searched through **July 19, 2026** for directly comparative primary studies in nonpregnant adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus multiple daily injections. I excluded systematic and narrative reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, editorials, pump/AID-only studies, premixed-insulin studies, and pediatric-only studies. Longer randomized trials are emphasized; selected short mechanistic trials are listed separately.

## What the primary studies show

### 1. Rapid-acting analogues versus regular human insulin

**Postprandial glucose is the clearest benefit.** In large lispro and aspart trials, administering the analogue immediately before eating produced substantially lower 1- and 2-hour postprandial excursions than regular insulin given 30–45 minutes before eating:

* In 1,008 adults with type 1 diabetes, lispro lowered the postmeal glucose rise by **1.3 mmol/L at 1 hour and 2.0 mmol/L at 2 hours**. ([PubMed][1])
* In 882 participants, aspart lowered postmeal glucose after each major meal; for example, breakfast glucose was **156 versus 185 mg/dL**. ([PubMed][2])
* A separate 1,070-person aspart trial also found consistently lower postprandial glucose with aspart. ([PubMed][3])

**HbA1c improvement is small and inconsistent.** The larger aspart studies found differences of approximately **−0.1% to −0.15%**, while several lispro trials found no significant HbA1c advantage:

* Aspart: 7.78% versus 7.93% at 6 months in one trial. ([PubMed][2])
* In a 12-month study, lispro produced HbA1c of 8.1% versus 8.3% with regular insulin. ([PubMed][4])
* In a blinded MDI crossover trial, HbA1c was 7.5% with lispro and 7.4% with regular insulin, with no significant difference. ([PubMed][5])
* In newly diagnosed adults, lispro and regular insulin produced similar HbA1c and beta-cell outcomes over 12 months. ([PubMed][6])

**Overall hypoglycemia is generally similar, although its timing may change.**

* The large 1,008-person lispro crossover trial reported **12% fewer total hypoglycemic episodes**, with the largest relative reduction at night. ([PubMed][1])
* The blinded UK trial found no difference in total symptomatic hypoglycemia: lispro caused fewer episodes from midnight to 6 a.m., but more between 6 a.m. and noon. ([PubMed][5])
* Among 33 adults with impaired awareness, severe events were numerically lower with lispro—55 versus 84—but the difference was not statistically significant. ([PubMed][7])
* Aspart trials generally found similar overall or major hypoglycemia, with some evidence of fewer late-postmeal or nocturnal episodes. ([PubMed][2])

**Convenience and satisfaction findings are mixed.** Immediate premeal dosing is inherently more flexible than taking regular insulin 30–45 minutes beforehand. One large aspart study found improved treatment-satisfaction scores, whereas the blinded UK lispro study found no clear patient preference when participants were already accustomed to injecting close to meals. ([PubMed][3])

### 2. One conventional rapid analogue versus another

There is little evidence of clinically important differences among aspart, lispro, and glulisine when used properly.

* In 672 adults treated for 26 weeks, glulisine and lispro produced the same HbA1c reduction, **−0.14% in each group**, with no relevant difference in overall, nocturnal, or severe symptomatic hypoglycemia. ([PubMed][8])
* In 267 Japanese adults, glulisine was noninferior to lispro for HbA1c; 2-hour postprandial glucose and serious hypoglycemia-related adverse events were not significantly different. ([PubMed][9])
* A small pharmacokinetic crossover study found aspart and lispro essentially indistinguishable in serum profiles and metabolic effects. ([PubMed][10])

Thus, switching between conventional rapid analogues is unlikely to produce a major HbA1c or hypoglycemia benefit by itself.

### 3. Faster aspart versus standard insulin aspart

**Mealtime faster aspart improves early postmeal control, but produces only a very small HbA1c difference.**

In the 1,143-person onset 1 trial:

* Mealtime faster aspart reduced HbA1c approximately **0.15 percentage points more** than standard aspart at 26 weeks.
* The 1-hour postmeal increment was lower by **1.18 mmol/L**, and the 2-hour increment by approximately **0.67 mmol/L**.
* Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia and general adverse events were similar. ([Diabetes Journals][11])

At 52 weeks, the same trial showed:

* HbA1c difference **−0.10%**.
* 1-hour postmeal increment difference **−0.91 mmol/L**, or about −16 mg/dL.
* No difference in overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia or treatment-emergent adverse events. ([PMC][12])

The 1,025-person onset 8 trial, using degludec as basal insulin, found essentially identical HbA1c with mealtime faster aspart versus aspart: **−0.02% treatment difference**. Postmeal faster aspart met the study’s noninferiority criterion but had HbA1c approximately **0.10% higher** than premeal standard aspart. ([PMC][13])

The findings suggest that postmeal dosing offers useful flexibility, but it should not be assumed to be metabolically identical to appropriately timed mealtime dosing.

### 4. Ultra-rapid lispro versus standard lispro

PRONTO-T1D randomized 1,222 adults:

* Mealtime ultra-rapid lispro reduced the 1-hour postmeal excursion by **1.55 mmol/L** and the 2-hour excursion by **1.73 mmol/L** compared with lispro.
* HbA1c difference was only **−0.08%**.
* Postmeal ultra-rapid lispro was noninferior under the trial’s relatively wide margin, but HbA1c was **0.13% higher than with premeal lispro**, a statistically significant difference.
* Overall severe, documented, and postprandial hypoglycemia rates were similar. Hypoglycemia occurring more than 4 hours after meals was 37% lower with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro.
* Injection-site reactions occurred in **2.9% with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro, 2.4% with postmeal ultra-rapid lispro, and 0.2% with lispro**. ([PubMed][14])

At 52 weeks:

* HbA1c was 7.47% versus 7.54%, an insignificant adjusted difference of −0.06%.
* One- and 2-hour postmeal glucose remained lower with ultra-rapid lispro.
* Overall hypoglycemia remained similar, while events more than 4 hours after meals were 31% lower.
* Injection-site reactions remained more frequent: **3.3% versus 0.9%**. ([PubMed][15])

The CGM substudy confirmed lower postmeal glucose exposure with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro, but it was a subset of the same PRONTO-T1D population rather than an independent trial. ([PubMed][16])

## Clinical interpretation

For adults using MDI, the expected advantages of faster prandial insulin are primarily:

* Lower early postprandial glucose excursions.
* Less need to inject 30–45 minutes before meals compared with regular insulin.
* Potentially less late-postmeal or nocturnal hypoglycemia in some patients.
* Greater flexibility when meal timing or intake is uncertain.

The trade-offs are:

* **Little average HbA1c improvement**—usually zero to approximately 0.15 percentage points.
* No consistent reduction in overall or severe hypoglycemia.
* Hypoglycemia may shift earlier after eating, so timing and correction-dose stacking remain important.
* Postmeal dosing is convenient but can produce slightly worse HbA1c than correctly timed premeal dosing.
* Ultra-rapid lispro causes more injection-site reactions than standard lispro.
* There is no evidence from these trials that one conventional rapid analogue is meaningfully superior to another.

None of the identified comparative MDI trials lasted longer than 52 weeks or was designed to establish effects on retinopathy, nephropathy, cardiovascular events, mortality, or other long-term complications. The principal established benefit is therefore **postprandial control and dosing convenience**, rather than demonstrated improvement in long-term clinical outcomes.
