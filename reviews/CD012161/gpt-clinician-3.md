## Scope

I focused on randomized comparative studies in nonpregnant adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus multiple daily injections. I excluded pump-only studies, pediatric studies, pregnancy, single-dose clamp/meal experiments, and noncomparative cohorts. Follow-up in the studies summarized below was generally 12–52 weeks.

## Main findings

### 1. Rapid-acting analogues versus regular human insulin

**Postprandial glucose is consistently better with lispro, aspart, or glulisine.**

* In a 1,008-person crossover trial, lispro lowered the postmeal rise by **1.3 mmol/L (23 mg/dL) at 1 hour** and **2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) at 2 hours** versus regular insulin. ([PubMed][1])
* In the 1,070-person European aspart trial, postprandial glucose was approximately **0.6–1.2 mmol/L (11–22 mg/dL) lower** after the principal meals. ([DOI][2])
* In Raskin et al., postmeal glucose after breakfast was **156 versus 185 mg/dL**, after lunch **137 versus 162 mg/dL**, and after dinner **153 versus 168 mg/dL** with aspart versus regular insulin. ([PubMed][3])

**The average HbA1c improvement is small.**

Individual trials generally reported differences of about **0–0.2 percentage points**:

* Lispro: HbA1c **8.1% versus 8.3%** in a 12-month study. ([PubMed][4])
* Aspart: baseline-adjusted difference **−0.12%** in Home et al. and endpoint HbA1c **7.78% versus 7.93%** in Raskin et al. ([DOI][2])
* Other studies found no meaningful HbA1c difference, including the blinded UK lispro crossover trial and the newly diagnosed cohort studied by Recasens et al. ([PubMed][5])

**Hypoglycemia findings are mixed, but nocturnal or late postmeal hypoglycemia may be reduced.**

* Anderson et al. reported **12% fewer overall hypoglycemic episodes** with lispro, with the largest relative reduction at night. ([PubMed][1])
* Home et al. found no significant difference in total major hypoglycemia, but fewer nocturnal events requiring parenteral treatment (**1.3% versus 3.4%**) and fewer late postmeal events. ([DOI][2])
* When both insulins were deliberately injected immediately before meals, Heller et al. found a **72% lower rate of major nocturnal hypoglycemia** with aspart, while total major hypoglycemia was not significantly different. Minor events were reduced by 7%. ([PubMed][6])
* In adults with impaired hypoglycemia awareness, lispro produced numerically fewer severe events than regular insulin—**55 versus 84 episodes**—but the difference was not statistically significant in this small study. ([PubMed][7])
* Several other trials found similar overall hypoglycemia rates. ([PubMed][3])

**Convenience is a genuine benefit, although patient preference is not universal.**

Most comparisons administered the analogue immediately before eating but required regular insulin 30–45 minutes before eating. Treatment satisfaction improved in the large European aspart trial, while participants in the blinded UK study—who already injected shortly before meals—expressed no clear preference. ([DOI][2])

### 2. One conventional rapid analogue versus another

In the 26-week trial of **glulisine versus lispro**, HbA1c fell by **0.14% in both groups**. Overall, nocturnal, and severe symptomatic hypoglycemia were similar. A statistically lower total insulin requirement with glulisine was reported, but its clinical importance was uncertain. ([PubMed][8])

Thus, there is little evidence that switching among lispro, aspart, and glulisine produces an important difference in HbA1c or safety for most adults.

### 3. Faster aspart versus conventional aspart

**onset 1:** At 52 weeks, faster aspart produced a small HbA1c advantage of **−0.10%** and reduced the 1-hour meal-test glucose increment by **0.91 mmol/L (16.5 mg/dL)**. Severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia and overall adverse events were similar. ([PMC][9])

**onset 8:** Mealtime faster aspart and conventional aspart had essentially identical HbA1c effects—difference **−0.02%**. The 1-hour postmeal increment was **0.90 mmol/L (16.2 mg/dL) lower** with mealtime faster aspart. Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was similar, with less hypoglycemia 3–4 hours after meals. ([PMC][10])

**Postmeal faster aspart:** Administration after the meal remained noninferior for HbA1c in the trials, but HbA1c was approximately **0.10% higher** than with premeal conventional aspart in onset 8, and early postmeal glucose control was less favorable. ([PMC][10])

### 4. Ultra-rapid lispro versus conventional lispro

**PRONTO-T1D:** Mealtime ultra-rapid lispro produced:

* HbA1c difference: **−0.08%**
* 1-hour postmeal excursion: **−1.55 mmol/L (−27.9 mg/dL)**
* 2-hour postmeal excursion: **−1.73 mmol/L (−31.1 mg/dL)**

Severe, documented, and overall postprandial hypoglycemia were similar, but hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was **37% lower**. Injection-site reactions occurred in **2.9% with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro versus 0.2% with lispro**. ([PubMed][11])

At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference was no longer statistically significant (**−0.06%**), although lower postmeal glucose and a 31% reduction in hypoglycemia more than four hours after meals persisted. Injection-site reactions occurred in **3.3% versus 0.9%**. ([PubMed][12])

The PRONTO CGM substudy found approximately **44 additional daytime minutes in range per day** and 11.5 fewer nighttime minutes at or below 70 mg/dL with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro. ([PubMed][13])

**Predominantly Chinese phase 3 trial:** Ultra-rapid lispro was noninferior to lispro for HbA1c; the difference was **+0.07%**. The 1- and 2-hour postmeal excursions were lower by approximately **17.8 and 25.5 mg/dL**, respectively. Severe and other hypoglycemia categories were similar. Early postmeal events were numerically more frequent and events after four hours numerically less frequent with ultra-rapid lispro, but neither difference was statistically significant. ([Wiley Online Library][14])

## Clinical interpretation

For an adult changing from **regular human insulin to a rapid analogue**, the best-supported advantages are:

* Lower 1- and 2-hour postmeal glucose.
* Injection at or near mealtime rather than 30–45 minutes beforehand.
* A possible reduction in nocturnal or late postmeal hypoglycemia.
* At most, a small average HbA1c improvement.

For an adult already using **lispro or aspart**, changing to faster aspart or ultra-rapid lispro primarily targets **early postmeal excursions**. The expected HbA1c improvement is usually only **0–0.1 percentage points**, and overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia is generally unchanged. Ultra-rapid lispro causes more injection-site reactions than conventional lispro.

Postmeal administration offers flexibility when meal intake is uncertain, but it is not metabolically equivalent to optimal premeal administration: early postmeal glucose is generally higher, and some trials found a slightly worse HbA1c.

None of these trials was designed or sufficiently long to establish effects on retinopathy, nephropathy, cardiovascular disease, mortality, or other long-term complications; the longest randomized follow-up was 52 weeks. ([PMC][9])
