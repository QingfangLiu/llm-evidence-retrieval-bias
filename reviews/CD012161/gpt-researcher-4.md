## Scope

I interpreted **faster-acting insulin analogues** broadly as:

* Rapid-acting analogues: insulin lispro, insulin aspart and insulin glulisine.
* Ultra-rapid formulations: faster-acting insulin aspart and ultra-rapid lispro.

The main synthesis includes randomized comparative treatment studies in nonpregnant adults with type 1 diabetes using basal–bolus **multiple daily injections**, where the prandial insulin differed between groups. I excluded pump-only studies, pediatric-only studies, pregnancy studies, observational comparisons, and trials that changed both basal and prandial insulin because the effect of the mealtime insulin could not be isolated. Single-dose clamp and meal-challenge studies are identified separately.

## Overall conclusions

### Faster analogues versus regular human insulin

The clearest benefit is **lower early postprandial glucose**, generally when the analogue is given immediately before eating while regular human insulin is given approximately 30–45 minutes beforehand. Across the larger aspart studies, postprandial differences were commonly approximately 0.6–1.6 mmol/L, while older lispro studies sometimes reported differences approaching 2 mmol/L. ([PubMed][1])

The effect on **HbA1c is much smaller and less consistent**. Large aspart trials found advantages of approximately 0.12–0.16 percentage points, whereas several lispro trials found no material difference. Premeal glulisine produced a 0.13-percentage-point greater HbA1c reduction than regular insulin over 12 weeks, but postmeal glulisine did not. ([PubMed][1])

Overall and severe hypoglycaemia were generally **similar**, although individual studies reported less nocturnal or late-postprandial hypoglycaemia with an analogue. These signals were not completely consistent, and an aspart extension found more minor hypoglycaemia despite no difference in major episodes. ([PubMed][1])

### One rapid analogue versus another

Insulin glulisine and lispro produced essentially equivalent HbA1c control. Neither the large multinational trial nor the Japanese trial demonstrated a clear difference in overall or severe hypoglycaemia. ([PubMed][2])

### Ultra-rapid versus conventional rapid analogues

Mealtime faster aspart and ultra-rapid lispro consistently reduced **1- and 2-hour postprandial glucose**. The typical advantage was approximately 0.9–1.7 mmol/L, or about 16–31 mg/dL. HbA1c differences were usually between zero and 0.15 percentage points. ([Diabetes Journals][3])

Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycaemia was generally similar, but its **timing shifted**: faster aspart produced more events within the first hour after meals in onset 1 but fewer events 3–4 hours after meals in onset 8; ultra-rapid lispro produced fewer events more than four hours after meals. ([PMC][4])

A reproducible harm signal with ultra-rapid lispro was **more injection-site reactions**, generally mild or moderate. Rates were approximately 2.8–3.3%, compared with 0–0.9% with conventional lispro in the principal trials. ([PubMed][5])

Postmeal administration offers flexibility, but its glycaemic performance is usually less favorable than mealtime administration. Postmeal ultra-rapid lispro had a 0.13-percentage-point higher HbA1c than lispro in PRONTO-T1D, and postmeal faster aspart had a 0.10-percentage-point higher HbA1c than aspart in onset 8, although both met their respective noninferiority criteria. ([PubMed][5])

## Outcomes for which evidence is insufficient

These trials do **not establish differences in mortality, cardiovascular events, retinopathy, nephropathy or other long-term diabetes complications**. Most were 12–52 weeks; the longest identified direct extension followed aspart versus regular insulin for approximately three years. Events such as diabetic ketoacidosis and serious adverse events were too uncommon or inconsistently reported to support a dependable comparative conclusion. ([PubMed][6])

Patient-reported evidence is limited and inconsistent. Treatment satisfaction favored aspart in the large European study, whereas the blinded UK lispro study found no clear treatment preference. Much of the practical benefit therefore comes from the ability to inject closer to the meal rather than a demonstrated improvement in quality of life or adherence. ([DOI][18])

## Important methodological considerations

* Many older studies were open-label, and several newer trials used treat-to-target noninferiority designs. These designs are suitable for showing comparable HbA1c but can reduce the likelihood of observing large between-group HbA1c differences.
* Hypoglycaemia definitions, meal timing and basal insulin regimens differed substantially.
* Some older lispro publications pooled or re-reported participants from multinational development trials. They should not automatically be treated as independent studies in a meta-analysis. ([PubMed][9])
* Standardized meal-test PPG improvements may not translate directly into the same difference during unrestricted daily living.
* Studies comparing a complete analogue regimen with a complete human-insulin regimen—for example, changing both basal NPH and prandial regular insulin—were not included in the principal synthesis because they do not isolate the contribution of the faster prandial insulin.
