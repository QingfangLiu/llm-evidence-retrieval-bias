## Faster-Acting Insulin Analogues in Adults with T1D on MDI: Primary Studies

I found a substantial body of individual randomized controlled trials (RCTs) — no reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines — directly addressing your question. They fall into a few useful groups.

**1. Classic rapid-acting analogues (aspart, lispro) vs. regular human insulin**
These established the original case for "faster-acting" analogues over regular human insulin, generally showing modest HbA1c improvements and fewer hypoglycemic episodes requiring assistance, with a trade-off of needing careful basal insulin optimization since nighttime control could be more variable.

**2. Second-generation "ultra-fast" analogues (faster aspart / Fiasp, ultra-rapid lispro / Lyumjev) vs. their older analogue counterparts**
These more recent trials ask whether the *newest* faster-acting analogues outperform the standard rapid analogues (not regular human insulin), typically finding non-inferior HbA1c with modestly better postprandial glucose control, sometimes at the cost of more injection-site reactions.

**3. Comparisons within combined basal+bolus analogue regimens**
Trials where both the older regimen (NPH + regular insulin) and newer regimen (long-acting analogue + rapid analogue) were varied together, which still speaks to the bolus insulin's contribution.

A general pattern across these studies: faster-acting insulins tend to reduce postprandial glucose excursions and sometimes reduce hypoglycemia, with HbA1c differences that are often small (frequently within 0.1–0.2 percentage points), and safety profiles that are broadly similar aside from occasional signals like increased injection-site reactions.

One important caveat: many of the newer faster-aspart/URLi trials pool type 1 and type 2 populations or report combined "onset" trial programs — I've focused on the T1D-specific reports and MDI-specific results.
