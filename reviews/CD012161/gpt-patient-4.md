## Bottom line

For adults with type 1 diabetes using multiple daily injections, faster mealtime insulins **consistently improve glucose during the first one to two hours after eating**. Their effect on HbA1c is much smaller—usually no difference to about a **0.1–0.2 percentage-point improvement**.

Across the trials, **overall severe or documented hypoglycemia was generally similar**, but its timing sometimes changed: less late postmeal or nocturnal hypoglycemia, but potentially more early postmeal hypoglycemia. Ultra-rapid lispro caused more injection-site reactions than conventional lispro. None of these trials was long enough to determine effects on microvascular complications, cardiovascular outcomes, mortality, or multi-year safety.

I treated the comparison in three groups:

* Rapid analogues—lispro, aspart or glulisine—versus regular human insulin.
* Ultra-rapid formulations—faster aspart or ultra-rapid lispro—versus conventional rapid analogues.
* One conventional rapid analogue versus another.

I excluded pump-only, pediatric-only, pregnancy and single-dose glucose-clamp studies from the main synthesis.

## Rapid analogues versus regular human insulin

### Benefits

The most reproducible benefit was lower early postprandial glucose:

* In 1,070 adults, insulin aspart reduced postmeal glucose by approximately **0.6–1.2 mmol/L, or 11–22 mg/dL**, and HbA1c by 0.12 percentage points relative to regular insulin over six months. ([PubMed][1])
* In another 882-person trial, aspart given immediately before meals produced lower postmeal glucose and an HbA1c of 7.78% versus 7.93% with regular insulin given 30 minutes before meals. ([PubMed][2])
* In the large multicenter lispro program, the postmeal rise was approximately **23 mg/dL lower at one hour and 36 mg/dL lower at two hours** with lispro; reported hypoglycemia was 12% less frequent, particularly overnight. ([PubMed][3])
* Smaller trials were less consistent for HbA1c. A blinded crossover trial found better post-breakfast and post-lunch glucose with lispro but essentially identical HbA1c, 7.5% versus 7.4%. ([PubMed][4])

An important practical benefit in these trials was that rapid analogues were administered at or immediately before eating, whereas regular insulin was generally administered 30–45 minutes beforehand. ([PubMed][2])

### Harms and limitations

Severe hypoglycemia was not consistently reduced. In the 1,070-person aspart trial, the relative risk of major hypoglycemia was 0.83, but the confidence interval included no difference; nocturnal events requiring assistance and late postmeal events were less frequent. ([PubMed][1]) In 33 adults with impaired hypoglycemia awareness, lispro produced numerically fewer severe events than regular insulin—55 versus 84—but the difference was not statistically significant. ([PubMed][5])

The three-year extension of the large aspart study found a persistent 0.16-percentage-point HbA1c advantage and no difference in major hypoglycemia, but **minor hypoglycemia was more frequent with aspart**, relative risk 1.24. ([PubMed][6])

One favorable lispro study changed the basal regimen substantially by adding NPH at meals in the lispro arm. Its lower HbA1c and hypoglycemia therefore cannot be attributed solely to the faster bolus insulin. ([PubMed][7])

## Ultra-rapid versus conventional rapid analogues

### Faster aspart versus insulin aspart

In onset 1, involving 1,143 adults:

* Mealtime faster aspart improved HbA1c by an additional **0.15 percentage points**.
* One-hour postmeal glucose excursion was approximately **21 mg/dL lower**.
* Two-hour excursion was approximately **12 mg/dL lower**.
* Overall severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia was similar. ([Diabetes Journals][8])

At 52 weeks, the HbA1c difference was 0.10 percentage points and the one-hour postmeal difference approximately 16.5 mg/dL, with no overall difference in severe or glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia. ([PMC][9])

In onset 8, using insulin degludec as basal insulin, mealtime faster aspart produced essentially the same HbA1c as conventional aspart—difference −0.02 percentage points—but approximately **16 mg/dL less one-hour postmeal excursion**. Postmeal faster-aspart dosing was less effective than mealtime dosing, with an HbA1c difference of +0.10 percentage points versus conventional mealtime aspart. ([PMC][10])

### Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro

In the 1,222-person PRONTO-T1D trial:

* Mealtime ultra-rapid lispro lowered one-hour postmeal excursion by approximately **28 mg/dL** and two-hour excursion by approximately **31 mg/dL**.
* The HbA1c difference was only −0.08 percentage points.
* Overall severe, documented and postmeal hypoglycemia was similar.
* Hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was 37% less frequent.
* Injection-site reactions occurred in 2.9% with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro versus 0.2% with lispro. ([PubMed][11])

The CGM substudy found approximately **44 additional minutes per day of daytime time in range** and 11.5 fewer minutes of nighttime glucose ≤70 mg/dL with mealtime ultra-rapid lispro. ([PubMed][12]) At 52 weeks, postmeal glucose benefits persisted, but injection-site reactions remained more frequent—3.3% versus 0.9%. ([PubMed][13])

A separate 354-person trial, conducted predominantly in China, found no HbA1c advantage—treatment difference +0.07 percentage points—but reductions of approximately **18 mg/dL at one hour and 26 mg/dL at two hours**. Severe and other hypoglycemia rates were similar, although early postmeal events were numerically more frequent and events after four hours numerically less frequent. ([Wiley Online Library][14])

**Postmeal dosing deserves caution:** in PRONTO-T1D, ultra-rapid lispro administered 20 minutes after starting the meal met a generous noninferiority margin but resulted in an HbA1c 0.13 percentage points higher than mealtime lispro. ([PubMed][11])

## One conventional rapid analogue versus another

Insulin glulisine and lispro performed similarly:

* A 672-person, 26-week trial found an identical mean HbA1c reduction of 0.14 percentage points, with no relevant differences in overall, nocturnal or severe symptomatic hypoglycemia. ([PubMed][15])
* A 267-person Japanese trial found glulisine noninferior to lispro for HbA1c, with no statistically significant difference in two-hour postmeal glucose or serious hypoglycemia-related events. ([PubMed][16])

I did not identify a long-term MDI outcome trial directly comparing faster aspart with ultra-rapid lispro; the major studies compared each ultra-rapid formulation with its corresponding conventional rapid analogue.

## How this could inform a personal decision

A faster formulation is most likely to offer a noticeable benefit when CGM shows a reproducible **early postmeal spike**, especially when taking insulin 15–30 minutes before eating is impractical. The evidence is less compelling when the principal objective is simply lowering HbA1c, because average improvements were small.

Issues worth reviewing with the prescribing clinician include:

* Whether current excursions occur during the first two hours or later after meals.
* Whether early postmeal hypoglycemia already occurs.
* How often boluses are taken after eating.
* Whether injection-site discomfort would be problematic.
* Coverage and out-of-pocket cost, which these efficacy trials generally did not evaluate.

A switch should include review of bolus timing, insulin-to-carbohydrate ratios, correction factors and CGM patterns rather than assuming the existing timing will remain optimal.
