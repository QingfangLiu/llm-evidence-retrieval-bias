## Scope used

I focused on randomized treatment studies in nonpregnant adults with type 1 diabetes receiving basal–bolus **multiple daily injections**. I excluded pump-only studies, single-dose pharmacokinetic/clamp experiments, pediatric-only studies, pregnancy studies, and nonrandomized evidence.

For the main evidence set, I used **at least 24 weeks of randomized treatment per intervention**, which is a reasonable minimum for sustained HbA1c and safety assessment. This produced **15 trial records**. Three older insulin-lispro trial records—Z011, Z013, and Z015—were reported in overlapping or pooled publications, so publications and trials should not be counted interchangeably.

## What the primary studies show

### Ultra-rapid analogue versus conventional rapid analogue

**Faster aspart versus insulin aspart**

In onset 1, mealtime faster aspart produced a small HbA1c advantage over aspart at 26 weeks: estimated treatment difference −0.15 percentage points. One- and two-hour postprandial glucose excursions were lower by 1.18 and 0.67 mmol/L, respectively. Overall severe or blood-glucose-confirmed hypoglycemia and general safety were similar. Postmeal faster aspart was noninferior for HbA1c but did not provide the same postprandial benefit as mealtime dosing. ([Diabetes Journals][1])

In onset 8, conducted with basal degludec, there was essentially no HbA1c difference between mealtime faster aspart and aspart: −0.02 percentage points. The one-hour meal-test excursion was 0.90 mmol/L lower with faster aspart. Overall severe or confirmed hypoglycemia was similar, although hypoglycemia three to four hours after meals occurred less frequently with mealtime faster aspart. Postmeal faster aspart had an HbA1c difference of +0.10 percentage points versus mealtime aspart. ([PMC][2])

**Ultra-rapid lispro versus lispro**

In the global PRONTO-T1D trial, mealtime ultra-rapid lispro was noninferior to lispro for HbA1c, with an estimated difference of −0.08 percentage points. One- and two-hour postprandial excursions were lower by 1.55 and 1.73 mmol/L. Severe, documented, and postprandial hypoglycemia rates were similar, but hypoglycemia occurring more than four hours after meals was 37% lower. Injection-site reactions were more frequent with ultra-rapid lispro: 2.9% with mealtime and 2.4% with postmeal ultra-rapid lispro versus 0.2% with lispro. Postmeal ultra-rapid lispro remained noninferior but produced a statistically higher HbA1c than mealtime lispro, difference +0.13 percentage points. ([PubMed][3])

At 52 weeks, PRONTO-T1D continued to show little HbA1c difference between mealtime ultra-rapid lispro and lispro, −0.06 percentage points, while the postprandial advantage was maintained. ([PubMed][4])

The predominantly Chinese PRONTO-T1D study similarly found noninferior HbA1c with ultra-rapid lispro: difference +0.07 percentage points. One- and two-hour postprandial excursions were approximately 1.0 and 1.4 mmol/L lower, respectively. Overall safety was described as similar, although injection-site reactions occurred in approximately 2.8% of ultra-rapid lispro recipients. ([Wiley Online Library][5])

**Interpretation:** the consistent benefit of ultra-rapid formulations is better early postprandial control when administered at mealtime. Average HbA1c improvements are small or absent. Postmeal administration offers dosing flexibility but usually sacrifices some of the postprandial or HbA1c advantage.

### Rapid analogue versus another rapid analogue

In the 26-week Dreyer trial, insulin glulisine and lispro both reduced HbA1c by 0.14 percentage points. Overall, nocturnal, and severe symptomatic hypoglycemia did not differ meaningfully. Glulisine required a slightly smaller increase in basal and total insulin dose, but the investigators considered the clinical importance uncertain. ([PubMed][6])

In the 28-week Japanese study, glulisine was noninferior to lispro for HbA1c. Two-hour postprandial glucose did not differ significantly. Serious hypoglycemia-related events occurred in 6.8% versus 4.4%, respectively, but this difference was not statistically significant; treatment satisfaction and insulin-antibody outcomes were also similar. ([PubMed][7])

Thus, there is no persuasive evidence that glulisine is clinically superior to lispro in adults using MDI.

### Rapid analogue versus regular human insulin

The larger insulin-aspart trials consistently found better postprandial control and only modest HbA1c differences.

In the 1,070-participant European trial, insulin aspart lowered HbA1c by approximately 0.12 percentage points relative to human insulin and lowered postmeal glucose by about 0.6–1.2 mmol/L. Treatment satisfaction was better. Major hypoglycemia was not significantly reduced, although parenterally treated nocturnal events and late postprandial events were less frequent. ([PubMed][8])

In Raskin and colleagues’ 882-participant trial, HbA1c at six months was 7.78% with aspart versus 7.93% with regular human insulin. Postprandial glucose was consistently lower, while adverse events and overall hypoglycemia were similar. Findings persisted during the extension to 12 months. ([PubMed][9])

The Japanese 24-week aspart trial found noninferiority for HbA1c, difference −0.18 percentage points, better glucose 90 minutes after breakfast, and comparable overall safety. ([J-STAGE][10])

The lispro evidence is less uniform. The pooled multicenter reports found better one- and two-hour postprandial control and a small HbA1c advantage—approximately 8.1% versus 8.3% in the insulin-dependent population. Another large crossover report found approximately 12% fewer recorded hypoglycemic episodes with lispro. However, the reports include pooled and overlapping trial populations, requiring care during extraction. ([PubMed][11])

In a high-risk population with impaired hypoglycemia awareness, severe hypoglycemia was numerically less frequent with lispro than regular insulin—55 versus 84 episodes—but the difference was not statistically significant. Nocturnal severe events were also numerically lower. The study included only 33 participants and was underpowered for this outcome. ([PubMed][12])

Among 45 adults with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes, lispro and regular insulin produced similar HbA1c, mild hypoglycemia, antibody, and residual beta-cell-function outcomes over 12 months. ([PubMed][13])

### Patient-important harms and evidence limitations

Across the modern active-comparator trials, **overall severe or confirmed hypoglycemia was usually similar**. Some studies found reductions during specific postmeal time windows, but these analyses do not establish a major reduction in total severe hypoglycemia.

Ultra-rapid lispro has the clearest formulation-specific adverse effect: more injection-site reactions than conventional lispro. No comparably consistent new harm signal appeared with faster aspart in the MDI trials. ([PubMed][3])

The identified studies were not large or long enough to determine effects on diabetic ketoacidosis, mortality, retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, or cardiovascular outcomes. Quality-of-life and treatment-satisfaction evidence was sparse. Older studies also used NPH or other older basal regimens and pre-CGM definitions of hypoglycemia, limiting direct applicability to contemporary care.

I did **not** identify an eligible direct MDI trial comparing ultra-rapid lispro with faster aspart.
