## Bottom line

Topical cyclosporine A provides a **real but generally modest benefit over its vehicle**, most consistently by improving objective dry-eye signs such as corneal/conjunctival staining and, in some studies, tear production. Improvements in dryness, discomfort, OSDI, or other patient-reported symptoms are less consistent; several large trials found no meaningful symptom difference despite improved ocular-surface findings. ([PubMed][1])

The principal harm is **transient instillation-site burning, stinging, irritation, or pain**. Rates vary substantially by formulation and study—from near vehicle rates with some water-free formulations to approximately 24%–29% with nanomicellar or cationic formulations. Treatment discontinuation because of adverse events was usually uncommon. Large and longer-term studies have not identified a consistent treatment-related systemic toxicity, intraocular-pressure effect, infection signal, or serious ocular safety signal, although they cannot rule out very rare harms. ([AAO Journal][2])

## Major comparative studies

### Conventional oil-in-water emulsion, 0.05% or 0.1%

**Stevenson et al., phase 2 dose-ranging trial:** Among 162 participants treated for 12 weeks, 0.05%–0.4% CsA improved several staining and symptom measures compared with vehicle, without a clear dose-response relationship. Higher concentrations did not appear to add benefit. Blood concentrations were extremely low, and no increased infection or microbial-overgrowth signal was found. ([PubMed][3])

**Sall et al., two pivotal phase 3 trials:** In 877 participants treated for six months, both 0.05% and 0.1% CsA improved corneal staining and categorized Schirmer results versus vehicle. The 0.05% group also had benefits in blurred vision, artificial-tear use, and physician global assessment, but not across all symptom measures. There was no meaningful dose-response advantage for 0.1%. ([PubMed][1])

**Chen et al., Chinese phase 3 trial:** In 240 participants receiving CsA 0.05% or vehicle plus lubricants for 12 weeks, the reported composite “overall effective rate” at three months was 70.6% versus 27.8%, and OSDI separation appeared by days 56–84. The unusually defined composite response makes this large effect difficult to compare directly with other trials. ([PubMed][4])

### Cationic emulsion, 0.1%

**SICCANOVE:** In 489 participants treated once daily for six months, the mean improvement in corneal staining was statistically greater with CsA, but the between-group difference was small: −1.05 versus −0.82 grades. Mean ocular discomfort improved similarly in both groups, although a ≥25% discomfort-response threshold favored CsA, 50.2% versus 41.9%. ([PubMed][5])

**SANSIKA:** In 246 people with severe keratitis, the prespecified combined response of a ≥2-grade staining improvement plus ≥30% OSDI improvement was not significantly different—28.6% with CsA versus 23.1% with vehicle. Corneal staining and an inflammatory biomarker improved, but OSDI did not separate from vehicle. Instillation-site pain occurred in 29.2% versus 8.9%, mostly mild—an absolute excess of about 20 percentage points. ([Sage Journals][6])

**Three-year severe-keratitis trial, published June 2026:** All 336 participants initially received open-label CsA for 12 months; 245 staining responders were then randomized to continue CsA or switch to vehicle for another two years. Improvements in signs were maintained, while symptom improvement tended to lag sign improvement by approximately three to six months. Ocular complications were uncommon during the randomized period—4.7% with CsA and 3.1% with vehicle. Because only initial responders entered the randomized phase, these results principally describe maintenance among responders rather than the probability that a newly treated patient will respond. ([Springer][7])

### Nanomicellar aqueous solution, 0.09%

**Tauber et al., phase 2/3:** In 455 participants treated for 12 weeks, both 0.05% and 0.09% formulations improved conjunctival staining, corneal staining, and Schirmer results versus vehicle. The patient-reported global symptom coprimary endpoint was not met; all three groups improved by approximately 30%. ([PMC][8])

**Goldberg et al., phase 3:** In 744 participants, 16.6% of eyes receiving CsA 0.09% achieved a ≥10-mm Schirmer increase at day 84 versus 9.2% with vehicle—an absolute difference of 7.4 percentage points, or roughly one additional responder per 14 treated eyes. Corneal and conjunctival staining improved, but global symptoms did not separate from vehicle. Instillation-site pain occurred in 24.2% versus 4.3%; 2.4% discontinued because of this pain. ([AAO Journal][2])

A 2026 single-arm phase 4 study switched patients whose disease was inadequately controlled on 0.05% emulsion to 0.09% solution. Signs and symptoms improved from baseline, and 69.4% preferred the new formulation, but there was no concurrent control group, so the study cannot establish that 0.09% is superior. Instillation-site irritation occurred in 12.7%, and 6.7% discontinued because of an adverse event. ([PMC][9])

### Water-free solution, 0.1%

**Exploratory phase 2 trial:** A 207-participant study compared two water-free concentrations with vehicle and with an open-label conventional 0.05% emulsion group. Staining improvement appeared by day 14. Ocular adverse events occurred in 13.7% with water-free 0.1%, 15.4% with vehicle, and 20.8% in the open-label conventional-emulsion group. Because the conventional-emulsion arm was not masked and the study was exploratory, this is not definitive head-to-head evidence. ([PMC][10])

**ESSENCE-1:** Among 328 participants, total corneal staining favored CsA by 0.8 points at week 4, with separation beginning at week 2. The hierarchical OSDI endpoint was not significant, although one secondary dryness rating favored CsA by approximately 4.8 points on a 100-point scale. Ocular treatment-emergent events occurred in 12.3% versus 8.4% with vehicle. ([PubMed][11])

**ESSENCE-2:** Among 834 participants, mean corneal-staining improvement at day 29 was −4.0 with CsA and −3.6 with vehicle—a 0.4-point difference on a 15-point scale. A ≥3-grade staining improvement occurred in 71.6% versus 59.7%, approximately one additional staining responder per eight treated participants. Dryness scores did not favor CsA. Instillation-site reactions occurred in 10.2% versus 8.8%, and overall adverse-event rates were similar. ([JAMA Network][12])

**Chinese phase 3 trial:** Among 206 participants, total corneal staining improved by −4.8 with CsA versus −3.0 with vehicle at day 29. The dryness-score difference was not significant. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 14.6% and 10.7%, respectively. ([PMC][13])

## Longer-term safety findings

In a 412-participant open-label extension of the conventional emulsion studies, mean treatment duration was approximately 20 months, with some participants treated for up to three years. Treatment-related burning occurred in 10.9%, stinging in 3.9%, and conjunctival hyperemia in 3.4%; no serious treatment-related events or systemic side effects were identified. Because everyone received CsA and participants had already tolerated earlier treatment, this design may underestimate intolerance among new users. ([ScienceDirect][14])

In the 52-week ESSENCE-2 water-free extension, 27.5% experienced an ocular treatment-emergent event; most were mild. Instillation-site pain occurred in 6.5%, three patients discontinued because of adverse events, and serious events were judged unrelated to treatment. Without a long-term vehicle control, efficacy changes cannot be separated fully from natural variation, regression to the mean, or continued vehicle effects. ([PMC][15])

The one-year SANSIKA extension found continued staining and symptom improvement. Instillation-site pain remained the most common treatment-related event, and systemic CsA concentrations were generally undetectable or negligible. A later discontinuation follow-up found that 39% of selected patients who had achieved marked staining improvement relapsed over 24 months. ([PubMed][16])

## Practical interpretation

**Benefits you are most likely to notice clinically:** better corneal/conjunctival epithelial staining and, for a minority, a meaningful increase in tear production. Symptom relief is less predictable. A patient may have demonstrably healthier staining without feeling substantially better, particularly during the first several weeks. ([PubMed][17])

**Time course:** newer water-free and nanomicellar formulations have shown improvement in ocular-surface signs within approximately two to four weeks, but symptom improvement may take longer. Conventional emulsion trials generally assessed their clearest benefit after several months. The 2026 long-term cationic-emulsion study supports the possibility that signs may improve three to six months before symptoms. ([PMC][18])

**Most likely harm:** brief burning, stinging, pain, or irritation after administration. The risk appears formulation-dependent, but comparisons across separate trials are unreliable because populations, vehicles, adverse-event definitions, and follow-up periods differed. ([AAO Journal][2])

**What remains uncertain:** there is no strong, fully masked, adequately powered head-to-head trial proving that 0.05% emulsion, 0.09% nanomicellar solution, 0.1% cationic emulsion, or 0.1% water-free solution is clinically superior for both symptoms and signs. Most pivotal studies were manufacturer-sponsored and used active vehicles that themselves improved dry-eye outcomes, reducing apparent between-group differences. ([PMC][10])
