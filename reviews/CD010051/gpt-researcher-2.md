## Scope

I identified human primary clinical studies of topical cyclosporine A ophthalmic solutions, emulsions, nanoemulsions, gels, and water-free solutions in clinically diagnosed dry eye disease. I excluded animal studies, reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, editorials, and studies treating allergic keratoconjunctivitis rather than dry eye.

This is a structured evidence map rather than a fully reproducible systematic review. I prioritized comparative efficacy trials and longer-term safety studies; biomarker-only secondary analyses are not listed separately.

## Overall findings

### Benefits

**Ocular-surface staining improves fairly consistently.** Across the pivotal trials, CsA generally produced greater reductions in corneal and/or conjunctival staining than its vehicle. The absolute between-group differences were often modest because emulsion and water-free vehicles themselves improved dry-eye signs substantially. ([PubMed][1])

Examples include:

* In the original 877-participant CsA 0.05%/0.1% phase III program, both concentrations improved corneal staining and categorized Schirmer results more than vehicle; there was no clear dose-response relationship. ([PubMed][1])
* SICCANOVE found a mean 6-month corneal-staining reduction of 1.05 grades with CsA 0.1% cationic emulsion versus 0.82 with vehicle, a between-group difference of approximately 0.23 grade. ([PubMed][2])
* SANSIKA’s prespecified composite sign-and-symptom endpoint was negative, but corneal staining improved more with CsA than vehicle: adjusted changes of −1.764 versus −1.418 grades. ([Sage Journals][3])
* ESSENCE-2 found a 0.4-grade advantage in total corneal fluorescein staining at 29 days; 71.6% versus 59.7% had a reduction of at least three grades, corresponding to a trial-specific number needed to treat of about 8 for that responder definition. ([PubMed][4])
* A Chinese water-free CsA trial found a larger total-staining difference of −1.8 grades at 29 days. ([PubMed][5])

**Tear production sometimes improves, but not in every study.** The clearest responder result comes from the 744-participant OTX-101/Cequa phase III trial: 16.6% receiving CsA 0.09% versus 9.2% receiving vehicle had a Schirmer increase of at least 10 mm at day 84. This is an absolute difference of 7.4%, or a trial-specific number needed to treat of approximately 14. ([PubMed][6])

**Symptom improvement is substantially less consistent than improvement in clinical signs.**

* The original CsA 0.05% phase III studies found benefits for blurred vision, artificial-tear use, and physician global assessment, but not consistently across all symptom measures. ([PubMed][1])
* OTX-101 improved conjunctival and corneal staining, but global symptoms improved by similar amounts with CsA and vehicle in both the phase II/III and phase III trials. ([PubMed][7])
* In SANSIKA, mean OSDI change was −13.6 with CsA and −14.1 with vehicle. ([Sage Journals][3])
* In ESSENCE-2, dryness improved by −12.2 points with CsA and −13.6 with vehicle, with no significant treatment difference. ([PubMed][4])
* The Chinese water-free trial similarly improved staining but not the primary dryness score. ([PubMed][5])
* SICCANOVE did not show a meaningful difference in mean ocular discomfort, although a slightly larger proportion achieved at least 25% symptom improvement: 50.2% versus 41.9%. ([PubMed][2])

Thus, **better staining does not reliably translate into a detectable average symptom advantage**, particularly in short trials or when the vehicle is itself lubricating.

**Newer formulations may produce earlier objective effects.** The CyclASol phase II study reported staining separation as early as day 14, and ESSENCE-1 and ESSENCE-2 showed corneal-staining effects by four weeks. These early findings mainly concern signs rather than consistent symptom relief. ([PMC][8])

**Benefits in evaporative/MGD-associated dry eye are possible but less certain.** Small trials of CsA 0.05% in meibomian gland dysfunction reported improvements in tear-film breakup time, lid-margin inflammation, meibomian-gland expressibility, and staining. Symptom differences were inconsistent, and the studies were much smaller than the registration trials. ([PubMed][9])

## Harms

### Local ocular discomfort

Burning, stinging, instillation-site pain, irritation, redness, and transient blurred vision are the principal harms.

* In SANSIKA, instillation-site pain occurred in 29.2% with CsA 0.1% cationic emulsion versus 8.9% with vehicle; events were mainly mild. ([Sage Journals][3])
* In the one-to-three-year CsA 0.1% emulsion extension, treatment-related burning occurred in 10.9%, stinging in 3.9%, and conjunctival hyperemia in 3.4%. ([ScienceDirect][10])
* In the large French early-access cohort, instillation-site pain occurred in 10.2% and eye irritation in 7.8%. ([PubMed][11])
* Water-free CsA appeared better tolerated in ESSENCE-2: instillation reactions occurred in 10.2% with CsA and 8.8% with vehicle, nearly all mild. Only one participant discontinued because of instillation-site burning. ([PMC][12])
* During the 52-week ESSENCE-2 extension, instillation-site pain occurred in 6.5%, was mild, and became less frequently reported over time. ([PMC][13])

Formulation therefore appears important: local discomfort rates cannot safely be generalized from one concentration or vehicle to another.

### Treatment discontinuation

Discontinuation because of adverse effects was generally uncommon in the newer water-free trials, but real-world intolerance can be clinically important. In the retrospective PERSIST cohort, burning or stinging was the reason for 60% of previous CsA treatment discontinuations among patients attempting treatment for a second time. ([PubMed][14])

### Serious and systemic harms

Serious treatment-related ocular or systemic harms were uncommon.

* The dose-ranging emulsion study found no microbial overgrowth, increased ocular infection, clinically important systemic toxicity, or meaningful blood CsA exposure; the highest measured blood concentration was 0.16 ng/mL. ([PubMed][15])
* SANSIKA and its extension reported undetectable or negligible systemic levels in nearly all patients. ([PubMed][16])
* The long-term conventional-emulsion study found no serious treatment-related events or systemic safety signal over one to three years. ([ScienceDirect][10])
* ESSENCE-2 and its 52-week extension found no treatment-related pattern in visual acuity, intraocular pressure, slit-lamp findings, or fundoscopy; reported serious events were considered unrelated. ([PMC][12])

## Interpretation for evidence synthesis

The most defensible conclusions are:

1. **CsA probably improves corneal and conjunctival staining**, with some evidence of increased tear production.
2. **Average symptom benefit is uncertain and formulation-dependent.** Many large studies failed to separate from vehicle on their principal symptom endpoint.
3. **The magnitude of benefit over vehicle is usually small to moderate**, despite sometimes large within-group improvements.
4. **Benefits may require weeks to months**, although newer water-free formulations demonstrate objective staining effects within two to four weeks.
5. **Local instillation discomfort is the main harm** and an important adherence issue.
6. **Serious ocular, infectious, and systemic toxicity appears uncommon**, including during longer follow-up.
7. Comparisons across “0.05%,” “0.09%,” and “0.1%” products should not be interpreted as simple concentration comparisons: vehicles, droplet characteristics, dosing frequency, bioavailability, eligibility criteria, and outcome scales differ considerably.

Major limitations include strong vehicle responses, sign–symptom discordance, heterogeneous dry-eye phenotypes, different staining scales and symptom instruments, short primary follow-up in several recent trials, and frequent manufacturer involvement in pivotal formulation studies.
