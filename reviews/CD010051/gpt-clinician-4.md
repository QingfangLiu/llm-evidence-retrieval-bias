## Evidence summary

I conducted a targeted search for **human primary clinical studies of topical cyclosporine A for dry eye disease**, current through **July 25, 2026**. I prioritized randomized vehicle-controlled trials, then examined extension, active-comparator, withdrawal and observational studies. I excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, consensus statements and editorials.

### Bottom line

**Benefits**

* The most reproducible benefit is improvement in **objective ocular-surface signs**, especially corneal or conjunctival staining. Improvements in Schirmer testing occur in some studies but are less consistent.
* The average placebo-adjusted improvements are often **statistically significant but modest**. For example, the treatment difference in corneal staining was about 0.22 grade in SICCANOVE, 0.35 grade in SANSIKA, 0.4 grade in ESSENCE-2 and 0.8 grade in ESSENCE-1, although the studies used different populations, scales and formulations and therefore should not be directly compared. ([PubMed][1])
* **Patient-reported symptoms are less consistently improved than signs.** Large trials of 0.09% nanomicellar cyclosporine, 0.1% cationic emulsion and 0.1% water-free cyclosporine frequently found no significant separation from vehicle for global symptoms, OSDI or dryness, despite improvements in staining. ([PubMed][2])
* Some patients have clinically meaningful responses. In the 0.09% OTX-101 phase 3 trial, a ≥10-mm Schirmer response occurred in 16.6% versus 9.2% with vehicle—an absolute difference of 7.4%, corresponding to approximately **one additional responder for every 14 treated**. In ESSENCE-2, a ≥3-grade corneal-staining response occurred in 71.6% versus 59.7%, an adjusted difference of 12.6%, or approximately **one additional responder for every eight treated**. ([PubMed][3])
* Some newer formulations produced detectable staining differences within **2–4 weeks**, but this should not be interpreted as reliable rapid symptom relief. ([PubMed][4])

**Harms**

* The dominant adverse effect is **transient instillation pain, burning, stinging or irritation**. Rates vary substantially with formulation and trial methodology.
* Examples include instillation pain of 29.2% versus 8.9% with vehicle in SANSIKA and 24.2% versus 4.3% with vehicle in the OTX-101 phase 3 trial. In contrast, ESSENCE-2 found similar overall adverse-event rates and instillation reactions of 10.2% versus 8.8%. ([Sage Journals][5])
* Serious treatment-related ocular or systemic toxicity has not emerged in the trials. Systemic cyclosporine levels were generally negligible or undetectable, and clinically important changes in visual acuity, intraocular pressure or fundus examination were not identified. However, the trials were not large enough to reliably exclude very rare harms. ([PubMed][6])

## Long-term and maintenance findings

Long-term evidence is mainly open-label and therefore much less reliable for efficacy than the randomized phases:

* In a 412-patient extension using CsA 0.1% emulsion for up to three years, burning occurred in 10.9%, stinging in 3.9% and conjunctival hyperemia in 3.4%; no serious treatment-related or systemic toxicity was identified. ([ScienceDirect][13])
* In the SANSIKA open-label extension, continued improvement occurred through 12 months; systemic levels were negligible or undetectable. Instillation pain was more frequent among patients newly switched from vehicle than among those already receiving CsA, suggesting some accommodation over time. ([PubMed][14])
* In a small randomized withdrawal extension, half of the patients switched from CsA to artificial tears progressed, whereas patients continuing or starting CsA improved. The sample was small—only eight patients were in the withdrawal group—so this should be considered suggestive rather than definitive evidence that ongoing maintenance is necessary. ([PubMed][15])
* A 52-week water-free CsA extension found ocular adverse events in 27.5%; the most frequent was mild instillation pain at 6.5%. Without a control group, its efficacy changes cannot establish treatment effect. ([PubMed][16])

## Clinical interpretation

For counseling, the most defensible expectation is:

> **CsA increases the probability of improving ocular-surface staining and, in some patients, tear production. It may improve symptoms, but substantial symptom improvement is not assured even when the ocular surface objectively improves.**

The formulation and vehicle matter for tolerability, but the available trials do **not** establish that one CsA formulation is universally superior. Cross-trial comparisons are particularly unreliable because disease severity, staining scales, dosing frequency, run-in procedures and primary endpoints differed. Small active-comparator trials have sometimes found alternative lubricants or CsA formulations noninferior to one another, but these do not overturn the more reliable vehicle-controlled findings. ([PubMed][17])

When judging benefit versus harm, the central tradeoff is therefore **a modest increase in objective ocular-surface improvement versus a formulation-dependent risk of burning or instillation pain**, with little evidence of systemic toxicity.
