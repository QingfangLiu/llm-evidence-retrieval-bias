## Clinical bottom line

Across formulations, topical cyclosporine A consistently produces **modest improvements in objective ocular-surface signs**, particularly corneal/conjunctival staining and, in some studies, tear production. Benefits for **patient-reported dryness, discomfort, and OSDI are less consistent**: several large trials improved signs without separating from vehicle on their principal symptom outcome.

The principal harm is **transient instillation-site burning, stinging, pain, or irritation**. Its incidence varies substantially by formulation and vehicle. Serious treatment-related ocular events and systemic cyclosporine toxicity were rare in the trials, but controlled follow-up was generally only 1–6 months, so uncommon or very delayed harms cannot be excluded.

These formulations should be considered separately rather than assuming that 0.05% emulsion, 0.09% nanomicellar solution, 0.1% cationic emulsion, and water-free 0.1% solution are therapeutically interchangeable.

## Evidence from the major randomized studies

### Conventional oil-in-water emulsion: 0.05% and 0.1%

In the early dose-ranging trial, 162 participants received cyclosporine 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.4%, or vehicle twice daily for 12 weeks. Several staining, Schirmer, discomfort, and OSDI outcomes favored cyclosporine, but higher concentrations did not produce a clear incremental benefit; 0.05% and 0.1% were judged the most useful balance of efficacy and tolerability. ([PubMed][1])

The pivotal publication combined two identically designed, double-masked, six-month trials totaling 877 participants. Both 0.05% and 0.1% improved corneal staining and categorized Schirmer results more than vehicle. The 0.05% formulation also improved blurred vision, artificial-tear use, and physician global assessment, but did not consistently outperform vehicle on the broader symptom measures. There was no dose-response advantage for 0.1% over 0.05%. ([PubMed][2])

A later Chinese phase III trial randomized 240 participants to 0.05% emulsion or vehicle, with both groups also using hypromellose. At three months, its composite “overall effective rate” was 70.6% versus 27.8%; OSDI and several tear-film and staining measures also favored cyclosporine. Drug-related adverse events occurred in 5.0% versus 2.5%, chiefly mild-to-moderate ocular pain. The composite primary outcome is not a standard endpoint, which limits comparison with the US and European trials. ([PMC][3])

Long-term, uncontrolled extension data included 412 participants treated with 0.1% emulsion for up to three years. Treatment-related burning occurred in 10.9%, stinging in 3.9%, and conjunctival hyperemia in 3.4%; there were no serious treatment-related events or identified systemic adverse effects. Because everyone received active treatment, this study is reassuring for common longer-term harms but cannot establish their comparative incidence. ([ScienceDirect][4])

### Once-daily 0.1% cationic emulsion

In SICCANOVE, 492 treated participants with moderate-to-severe DED received cationic cyclosporine or vehicle for six months. Corneal fluorescein staining improved slightly more with cyclosporine: mean change −1.05 versus −0.82, an adjusted difference of −0.22 on the modified Oxford scale. Mean ocular discomfort did not clearly separate, although 50.2% versus 41.9% achieved at least 25% improvement in discomfort. Any ocular adverse event occurred in 42.6% versus 26.8%; treatment-related eye irritation occurred in 16.1% versus 2.4%, instillation-site irritation in 9.1% versus 1.6%, and eye pain in 7.0% versus 2.8%. One severe corneal epithelial erosion considered related to treatment resolved without sequelae. ([PMC][5])

SANSIKA enrolled 246 participants with severe keratitis. Its prespecified composite primary outcome—at least two grades of staining improvement plus at least 30% OSDI improvement—was not statistically significant: 28.6% versus 23.1%. Corneal staining nevertheless improved more with cyclosporine, while mean OSDI improvement was virtually identical to vehicle. Instillation-site pain occurred in 29.2% versus 8.9%, predominantly mild. ([Sage Journals][6])

During SANSIKA’s subsequent open-label year, continued improvements in staining and symptoms were reported. Instillation-site pain remained the most frequent treatment-related event, occurring in 7.8% of participants continuing cyclosporine and 19.0% of those newly switched from vehicle. Systemic levels were undetectable or negligible except in two people with previous systemic cyclosporine exposure. ([PMC][7])

### Nanomicellar cyclosporine 0.09%

The 455-participant phase II/III trial found greater improvement in conjunctival staining with both 0.05% and 0.09% nanomicellar cyclosporine than with vehicle. Neither concentration improved the coprimary global symptom score relative to vehicle. Instillation-site pain occurred in 15.1% with 0.09%, 13.2% with 0.05%, and 3.3% with vehicle; no serious ocular adverse events were reported. ([PMC][8])

The subsequent phase III trial randomized 744 participants. At day 84, an increase of at least 10 mm on unanesthetized Schirmer testing occurred in 16.6% with cyclosporine versus 9.2% with vehicle—an absolute difference of 7.4 percentage points. Corneal and conjunctival staining also favored treatment, but the global symptom score improved by approximately 30% in both groups without significant separation. Instillation-site pain occurred in 24.2% versus 4.3%; 2.4% discontinued because of pain. Serious events were nonocular and considered unrelated. ([PubMed][9])

### Water-free cyclosporine 0.1%

A 207-participant exploratory phase II trial found early improvements, especially in central corneal and conjunctival staining, with water-free cyclosporine compared with vehicle. An open-label Restasis arm was included, but because that comparison was not masked, it should not be treated as definitive evidence of superiority between formulations. ([ScienceDirect][10])

ESSENCE-1 randomized 328 participants. At four weeks, the between-group difference in total corneal fluorescein staining was −0.8 points favoring cyclosporine. The hierarchical OSDI endpoint was negative, but the dryness visual-analog score favored cyclosporine by about 4.9 points. Instillation-site pain was reported in 2.5% versus 1.2%; three cyclosporine participants discontinued because of ocular adverse events. ([PMC][11])

ESSENCE-2 randomized 834 participants for 29 days. Total corneal staining improved by −4.0 versus −3.6, a between-group difference of −0.4. Dryness did not improve more than vehicle. A reduction of at least three staining grades occurred in 71.6% versus 59.7%, although the relationship between staining response and symptom improvement came from responder analyses rather than the randomized symptom comparison. Overall and ocular adverse-event rates were similar; mild instillation reactions occurred in 10.2% versus 8.8%. ([PMC][12])

A Chinese phase III study of the same general water-free approach randomized 206 participants. At day 29, total corneal staining favored cyclosporine by −1.8 points, but dryness did not: between-group difference −3.8 on a 100-point scale, 95% CI −9.2 to 1.6. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 14.6% versus 10.7%. ([PubMed][13])

In the 52-week ESSENCE-2 open-label extension, 27.5% experienced an ocular treatment-emergent event; instillation-site pain occurred in 6.5% and was mild in every case. Serious events were nonocular and considered unrelated. ([PMC][14])

## What the individual trials collectively suggest

**Objective benefit is more dependable than symptomatic benefit.** Corneal or conjunctival staining favored cyclosporine in nearly all pivotal development programs. Tear production improved in selected studies, most clearly in the nanomicellar phase III trial. In contrast, OSDI, dryness, or global discomfort frequently improved substantially in both groups without a statistically significant cyclosporine–vehicle difference. ([PubMed][2])

**The average between-group sign differences are often small.** Examples include −0.22 on the modified Oxford scale in SICCANOVE and −0.4 on the 15-point NEI scale in ESSENCE-2. Responder outcomes sometimes look more favorable, but they depend heavily on the chosen threshold and should be interpreted alongside the continuous outcome and symptom results. ([PMC][5])

**Onset differs by formulation and endpoint.** The water-free trials demonstrated staining separation within two to four weeks, whereas benefits with the conventional emulsion were generally evaluated over three to six months. This does not prove one product is superior because the populations, vehicles, entry criteria, scales, and environmental challenge procedures differed. ([PMC][11])

**Local tolerability is the central tradeoff.** Instillation pain ranged from little more than vehicle with water-free formulations to approximately 24% with nanomicellar 0.09% and 29% with cationic 0.1%. Vehicle composition clearly matters: some vehicles themselves caused appreciable discomfort and improved both signs and symptoms. ([AAO Journal][15])

**Systemic toxicity has not emerged as a meaningful signal in these trials.** Studies generally found no clinically important changes in IOP, visual acuity, examination findings, or systemic cyclosporine exposure. However, trial sizes and follow-up are inadequate to rule out very rare adverse effects. ([ScienceDirect][16])

**Phenotype matters.** Most pivotal vehicle-controlled studies recruited predominantly female adults with moderate-to-severe staining or aqueous-deficient features. In a 345-participant MGD-enriched trial, both 0.05% cyclosporine and TearCare improved OSDI, but TearCare produced greater TBUT and meibomian-gland improvements, illustrating that an MGD-predominant phenotype may respond better to directly targeted therapy. ([PubMed][17])

## Practical interpretation for counseling

A reasonable description to patients is:

> Cyclosporine is more likely to improve corneal surface damage and sometimes tear production than to produce a large or rapid improvement in how the eyes feel. Symptom improvement may take weeks to months and is not guaranteed. Burning or stinging after dosing is common with some formulations, but serious eye or systemic complications were uncommon in the clinical trials.

The formulation-specific tolerability data may be as clinically important as the nominal concentration. Lack of symptomatic improvement despite improved staining is not unusual, and conversely, vehicle or lubricant effects can improve symptoms without a cyclosporine-specific effect.
