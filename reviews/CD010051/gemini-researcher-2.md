Based on the primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating topical cyclosporine A (CsA) for dry eye disease (DED), the data shows a clear divergence between clinical signs (which consistently improve) and subjective symptoms (which are sometimes masked by a strong placebo effect from the vehicle drops).

Here is the breakdown of the clinical benefits and harms demonstrated in these primary efficacy and safety studies.

## Benefits

**1. Increased Tear Production**
CsA consistently demonstrates the ability to increase aqueous tear production compared to vehicle (placebo) drops. In pivotal phase 3 trials, the standard primary endpoint is often the proportion of eyes achieving a clinically meaningful increase in Schirmer test scores (typically defined as an increase of $\ge$ 10 mm). High-concentration nanomicellar formulations (0.09%) have shown that a significantly greater percentage of patients hit this mark by day 84 compared to those using only the vehicle (Goldberg et al., 2019; Tauber et al., 2018).

**2. Improved Ocular Surface Integrity**
By modulating local inflammation and inhibiting T-cell activation, CsA helps heal the damaged ocular surface. Across various formulations—from the classic 0.05% emulsion to the 0.09% clear solution and 0.1% cationic emulsions—trials routinely report significant reductions in both corneal and conjunctival staining. These improvements indicate that the epithelial surface of the eye is physically repairing itself more effectively than it does with standard artificial tears or vehicle drops (Goldberg et al., 2019; Tauber et al., 2018).

**3. Symptom Relief (With Caveats)**
Patients generally report notable reductions in ocular discomfort, foreign body sensation, and global symptom scores over the course of treatment. For patients with severe ocular surface damage, higher concentrations (like the 0.1% cationic emulsion) have yielded statistically significant improvements in Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) discomfort scores compared to vehicle (Baudouin et al., 2017).

However, in the broader DED population, it is common for subjective symptoms to improve equally in both the treatment and the vehicle groups. For example, some phase 3 trials observed a ~30% reduction in global symptom scores from baseline across both groups, meaning the treatment did not always statistically separate from the vehicle in subjective symptom relief despite clear superiority in objective clinical signs (Goldberg et al., 2019).

## Harms and Tolerability

**1. Instillation Site Reactions**
The most prevalent adverse event across all primary studies is instillation site pain—typically described by patients as a burning or stinging sensation immediately upon applying the drops. In the foundational phase 3 trials for the 0.05% emulsion, ocular burning was reported in approximately 17% of patients in the first year (Sall et al., 2000).

**2. Localized Ocular Adverse Events**
Other less common but consistently documented local adverse reactions include conjunctival hyperemia (redness of the eye), eye irritation, discharge, and foreign body sensation.

**3. Systemic Safety**
Because CsA drops are administered topically at very low concentrations, the drug has a highly favorable systemic safety profile. The primary literature notes that treatment-emergent adverse events are overwhelmingly localized to the eye, mild to moderate in severity, and do not carry the systemic immunosuppressive risks associated with oral cyclosporine therapy (Goldberg et al., 2019; Sall et al., 2000).
