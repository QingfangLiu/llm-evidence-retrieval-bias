# Cyclosporine A eye drops for dry eye disease

I searched for human primary clinical studies available through **July 25, 2026**, emphasizing randomized controlled trials and longer-term safety extensions. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions, and editorials. I also excluded studies where the principal disease was allergic/vernal keratoconjunctivitis rather than dry eye disease.

## Overall interpretation

**Topical cyclosporine more consistently improves objective signs than symptoms.** Across formulations, the most reproducible benefits are reductions in corneal or conjunctival staining and, in some studies, increased Schirmer tear production. The incremental effects over vehicle are often statistically significant but modest because vehicle drops themselves produce substantial improvements. ([PubMed][1])

**Symptom benefits are inconsistent.** Some studies found improvements in particular symptoms, artificial-tear use, OSDI, or responder outcomes, but several large trials found no significant difference from vehicle for their prespecified symptom endpoint despite clear improvements in staining or tear production. ([PubMed][1])

**The principal harm is transient instillation discomfort—burning, stinging, or pain.** Its frequency depends strongly on the formulation: it was substantially higher than vehicle with nanomicellar 0.09% CsA, but close to vehicle with the water-free 0.1% formulation in ESSENCE-2. Serious treatment-related ocular or systemic events were not evident in the large trials, although the studies were not powered to exclude very rare harms. ([AAO Journal][2])

## Longer-term harms and durability

A 412-patient open-label extension of conventional 0.1% emulsion followed patients for up to three additional years. Burning occurred in **10.9%**, stinging in **3.9%**, and conjunctival hyperemia in **3.4%**; there were no serious treatment-related adverse events or identified systemic toxicity. Because participants had already tolerated and completed earlier CsA trials, this probably underestimates intolerance among new users. ([ScienceDirect][14])

In the 52-week ESSENCE-2 open-label extension of water-free 0.1% CsA, 27.5% reported an ocular TEAE and 6.5% reported instillation-site pain, all mild. Signs and symptoms continued to improve from baseline, but there was no concurrent control group. ([PubMed][15])

A three-year cationic-emulsion study published in 2026 enrolled 336 patients for an initial open-label year and randomized 245 responders during years 2–3. Ocular complications occurred in 4.7% with continued CsA and 3.1% with vehicle during the randomized period. The investigators observed that symptom improvement could lag staining improvement by approximately three to six months. The responder-enriched randomized phase limits applicability to early nonresponders or intolerant patients. ([PubMed][16])

## Practical conclusions for counseling

The most defensible expected benefit is **improvement in ocular-surface staining**, sometimes accompanied by increased tear production. The absolute incremental benefit over the formulation’s vehicle is generally modest, although a clinically useful subset responds substantially.

Improvement in burning, dryness, visual fluctuation, or OSDI is **less predictable**. A patient may show improved staining without reporting meaningful symptom relief, particularly during the first several weeks.

Instillation burning or pain is the principal tolerability issue. The observed rate ranges from approximately 2% in a small water-free phase II study to 15%–24% with nanomicellar 0.09% CsA. Formulation-specific data are therefore more useful than treating all topical-CsA products as equivalent. ([AAO Journal][9])

The available controlled trials are reassuring regarding visual acuity, IOP, infection, systemic absorption, and serious ocular toxicity, but most randomized periods lasted only one to six months. Long-term studies are predominantly open-label or responder-enriched and cannot reliably exclude rare harms. ([PubMed][3])
