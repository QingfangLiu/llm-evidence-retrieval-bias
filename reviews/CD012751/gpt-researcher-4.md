## Scope

This is a **focused study-level evidence map**, not a formal exhaustive systematic review. I checked primary randomized trial reports available through **July 18, 2026**, concentrating on adults with moderate-to-severe luminal Crohn’s disease and established or late-stage advanced therapies. I excluded systematic reviews, guidelines, committee statements, editorials, and routine observational cohorts.

Most maintenance trials used a **randomized-responder or withdrawal design**: only patients who responded to induction entered maintenance randomization. Their remission percentages therefore apply to an enriched responder population and should not be compared directly with treat-through trials or across drugs.

## Findings by treatment

### Infliximab

The initial placebo-controlled trial established rapid induction activity. In **ACCENT I**, among patients responding to an initial infliximab infusion, week-30 remission occurred in 39% with scheduled 5 mg/kg and 45% with 10 mg/kg, versus 21% with episodic/placebo maintenance. Median time to loss of response was 38 weeks and more than 54 weeks in the scheduled groups, versus 19 weeks in the comparison group. ([PubMed][1])

In **SONIC**, conducted in patients naïve to immunosuppressants and biologics, corticosteroid-free remission at week 26 was:

* Infliximab plus azathioprine: **56.8%**
* Infliximab alone: **44.4%**
* Azathioprine alone: **30.0%**

Mucosal healing followed the same hierarchy. Serious infection rates were 3.9%, 4.9%, and 5.6%, respectively. ([PubMed][2])

Separate randomized trials support infliximab for fistulizing disease, including induction of fistula closure and maintenance of fistula response. These outcomes are not equivalent to luminal CDAI remission. ([PubMed][3])

### Adalimumab

In anti-TNF–naïve patients in **CLASSIC I**, week-4 remission was 36% with the 160/80-mg induction regimen versus 12% with placebo. Injection-site reactions were more frequent with adalimumab, but overall adverse-event rates were similar. ([PubMed][4])

In **GAIN**, which enrolled patients with previous infliximab loss of response or intolerance, week-4 remission was approximately 21% with adalimumab versus 7% with placebo. ([ClinicalTrials.gov][5])

In the responder-enriched **CHARM** maintenance trial, remission was:

| Time    | Adalimumab every other week | Weekly adalimumab | Placebo |
| ------- | --------------------------: | ----------------: | ------: |
| Week 26 |                         40% |               47% |     17% |
| Week 56 |                         36% |               41% |     12% |

Discontinuation because of adverse events was more frequent with placebo than with either scheduled adalimumab regimen. ([PubMed][6])

### Certolizumab pegol

The two pivotal trials produced somewhat different results.

In **PRECiSE 1**, certolizumab produced a modest improvement in clinical response, but remission was not significantly different from placebo at the principal time points. Serious infection occurred in approximately 2% versus less than 1% with placebo, and anti-certolizumab antibodies developed in about 8%. ([PubMed][7])

In **PRECiSE 2**, induction responders were randomized to continued certolizumab or placebo. Week-26 remission was 48% versus 29%. Serious infectious adverse events occurred in approximately 3% versus less than 1%; one certolizumab recipient developed pulmonary tuberculosis. ([PubMed][8])

### Natalizumab

In **ENACT-1**, natalizumab did not produce a convincing overall induction benefit. In **ENACT-2**, among induction responders, continued natalizumab maintained response in 61% versus 28% and remission in 44% versus 26%. A fatal case of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy occurred during open-label extension treatment. ([PubMed][9])

**ENCORE**, which selected patients with elevated C-reactive protein, found sustained remission during weeks 8–12 in 26% with natalizumab versus 16% with placebo. Overall adverse-event frequencies were similar during the controlled period. ([PubMed][10])

The PML risk distinguishes natalizumab from gut-selective vedolizumab and substantially constrains its use.

### Vedolizumab

In **GEMINI 2**, week-6 clinical remission was 14.5% with vedolizumab versus 6.8% with placebo. Among induction responders entering maintenance, week-52 remission was 39.0% with dosing every eight weeks and 36.4% every four weeks, versus 21.6% with placebo. ([PubMed][11])

In **GEMINI 3**, which largely enrolled patients with previous anti-TNF failure, the week-6 remission difference was not significant—15.2% versus 12.1%—but a delayed benefit was evident at week 10, 26.6% versus 12.1%. This supports a slower onset in some refractory patients. Adverse-event rates were similar. ([PubMed][12])

In **VISIBLE 2**, intravenous vedolizumab induction responders were randomized to subcutaneous maintenance or placebo. Week-52 remission was 48.0% versus 34.3%. Injection-site reactions occurred in 2.9% with subcutaneous vedolizumab and were the principal new formulation-specific finding. ([PMC][13])

### Ustekinumab

In **UNITI-1**, involving patients with anti-TNF failure, week-8 remission was approximately 19%–21% with ustekinumab versus 8.9% with placebo. In **UNITI-2**, which included patients failing conventional treatment but not necessarily anti-TNF therapy, remission was approximately 32%–40% versus 19.6%.

Among induction responders in **IM-UNITI**, week-44 remission was:

* Ustekinumab every eight weeks: **53.1%**
* Every 12 weeks: **48.8%**
* Placebo withdrawal: **35.9%**

Overall adverse-event and serious adverse-event rates were similar across groups. ([PubMed][14])

A five-year extension found sustained remission in a subset remaining in follow-up, with low immunogenicity, but the later extension was no longer a fully placebo-controlled comparison and was affected by attrition. ([PubMed][15])

### Risankizumab

A phase 2 placebo-controlled trial first demonstrated induction efficacy in a population in which most participants had previous anti-TNF exposure: week-12 remission was 31% across risankizumab groups versus 15% with placebo and 37% with the 600-mg dose. ([PubMed][16])

In the phase 3 **ADVANCE** trial, week-12 CDAI remission was 45% with 600 mg and 42% with 1200 mg versus 25% with placebo; endoscopic response was 40%, 32%, and 12%. In the more treatment-refractory **MOTIVATE** trial, remission was 42% and 40% versus 20%, and endoscopic response was 29% and 34% versus 11%. Adverse-event frequencies were broadly similar to placebo. ([PubMed][17])

In the responder-withdrawal **FORTIFY** maintenance trial, week-52 CDAI remission was 52% with 360 mg and 55% with 180 mg versus 41% after withdrawal to placebo. Endoscopic response was 47% in both risankizumab groups versus 22%. ([PubMed][18])

### Guselkumab

In the phase 2 **GALAXI-1** induction study, week-12 remission ranged from approximately 46% to 57% across guselkumab doses versus 16.4% with placebo. ([PubMed][19])

The GALAXI-1 maintenance report found week-48 CDAI remission rates of approximately 57%–73% across guselkumab regimens. Endoscopic response was approximately 44%–46%. No active tuberculosis, opportunistic infection, or death was reported in the controlled study period. ([PubMed][20])

The phase 3 **GRAVITI** treat-through trial evaluated entirely subcutaneous treatment. At week 12:

* Clinical remission: **56.1% versus 21.4%**
* Endoscopic response: **41.3% versus 21.4%**

At week 48, remission was 60.0% and 66.1% with the two guselkumab maintenance regimens versus 17.1% in the placebo-policy group; endoscopic response was 44.3% and 51.3% versus 6.8%. Placebo participants could receive rescue guselkumab from week 16, with nonresponder methods used in the efficacy analysis. Adverse-event rates were not higher with guselkumab. ([ScienceDirect][21])

The confirmatory **GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3** trials also showed superiority to placebo for combined induction and week-48 clinical and endoscopic outcomes, with an ustekinumab active-control arm. ([PubMed][22])

### Mirikizumab

The phase 2 **SERENITY** study demonstrated dose-dependent endoscopic induction activity and maintenance of endoscopic response through one year. Overall adverse-event rates were similar to placebo during the randomized portions, although the nonrandomized maintenance cohort had more serious events and discontinuations. ([PubMed][23])

In phase 3 **VIVID-1**, the co-primary treat-through outcomes were:

* Week-12 patient-reported clinical response plus week-52 endoscopic response: **38.0% versus 9.0%**
* Week-12 response plus week-52 CDAI remission: **45.4% versus 19.6%**

Serious adverse events occurred in 10.3% with mirikizumab, 10.7% with ustekinumab, and 17.1% in the placebo-policy group. Three deaths occurred, none considered treatment-related. ([ScienceDirect][24])

### Upadacitinib

The phase 2 **CELEST** trial showed clearer dose-dependent effects on endoscopic outcomes than on CDAI remission. Endoscopic remission reached 22% with 24 mg twice daily versus 0% with placebo. Infections, serious infections, and lipid elevations were more frequent at some upadacitinib doses. ([Mayo Clinic][25])

In phase 3:

* **U-EXCEL:** week-12 remission 49.5% versus 29.1%; endoscopic response 45.5% versus 13.1%.
* **U-EXCEED:** remission 38.9% versus 21.1%; endoscopic response 34.6% versus 3.5%.
* **U-ENDURE:** week-52 remission 37.3% with 15 mg and 47.6% with 30 mg versus 15.1% after placebo withdrawal; endoscopic response 27.6%, 40.1%, and 7.3%. ([PubMed][26])

Herpes zoster was more frequent with upadacitinib, particularly with 30-mg maintenance. Hepatic abnormalities and neutropenia were also more frequent at the higher dose. Gastrointestinal perforations occurred in four patients receiving 45-mg induction and one patient in each active maintenance group. The final U-ENDURE report did not observe deaths, active tuberculosis, lymphoma, nonmelanoma skin cancer, major cardiovascular events, or venous thromboembolism during the controlled period, but the study was not large enough to exclude rare risks. ([PubMed][27])

## Direct randomized comparisons

**Infliximab plus azathioprine versus either alone:** SONIC supports combination treatment in biologic- and immunosuppressant-naïve patients, with higher corticosteroid-free remission and mucosal healing than either monotherapy. ([PubMed][2])

**Ustekinumab versus adalimumab:** In biologic-naïve patients in **SEAVUE**, week-52 remission was 65% with ustekinumab and 61% with adalimumab; the difference was not statistically significant. Serious infection occurred in 2% and 3%, respectively. ([PubMed][28])

**Risankizumab versus ustekinumab:** In anti-TNF–experienced patients in **SEQUENCE**, week-24 clinical remission was 58.6% versus 39.5%, satisfying noninferiority. Week-48 endoscopic remission was 31.8% versus 16.2%, demonstrating superiority for risankizumab. Overall adverse-event rates were similar. ([PubMed][29])

**Mirikizumab versus ustekinumab:** VIVID-1 met noninferiority criteria for week-52 CDAI remission, but mirikizumab did not establish superiority for endoscopic response in the overall active-control comparison. ([ScienceDirect][24])

## Overall interpretation

The most consistently replicated benefits are:

* Anti-TNF agents, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, selective IL-23 inhibitors, and upadacitinib all have randomized evidence of clinically meaningful remission induction or maintenance.
* Selective IL-23 trials and upadacitinib trials generally show larger placebo-adjusted effects on newer combined clinical and endoscopic outcomes than many older trials, but this **does not establish a cross-trial efficacy ranking**. Populations, prior biologic exposure, placebo rates, outcome definitions, rescue rules, and randomization designs differ substantially.
* Infliximab plus azathioprine has direct evidence of benefit over either monotherapy in appropriately selected treatment-naïve patients.
* Risankizumab has the clearest completed superiority evidence against another biologic—ustekinumab—for endoscopic remission after anti-TNF failure.
* Ustekinumab and adalimumab performed similarly in the biologic-naïve SEAVUE population.
* CDAI remission alone should not be interpreted as mucosal healing. Newer trials requiring endoscopic response or remission provide more objective evidence of disease control.

For harms, the RCT programs generally suggest low absolute rates of serious events, but they are inadequate for uncommon, delayed outcomes. Important study-level signals include serious infection and tuberculosis with anti-TNF/certolizumab treatment, natalizumab-associated PML, herpes zoster and laboratory abnormalities with upadacitinib, and gastrointestinal perforations in the upadacitinib program. Rare malignancy, cardiovascular, thrombotic, pregnancy, and long-latency infection outcomes require evidence beyond randomized trials.
