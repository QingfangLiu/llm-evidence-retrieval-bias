## Scope and how to interpret the evidence

The studies below principally enrolled **adults with moderate-to-severe active Crohn’s disease**. I prioritized randomized controlled trials and supplemented them with primary observational safety studies because randomized trials are usually too small and short to detect uncommon infections or malignancies.

Two cautions matter when comparing the numbers:

* “Clinical remission” usually meant a **Crohn’s Disease Activity Index below 150**, a symptom-based outcome. Endoscopic healing is a separate and generally more stringent outcome.
* Most maintenance studies used a **responder-enriched withdrawal design**: only patients who improved during induction were randomized to continue treatment or switch to placebo. Their remission percentages therefore cannot be directly compared with induction percentages—or with maintenance trials using a treat-through design.

## Benefits demonstrated in individual trials

### Anti-TNF therapy

**Infliximab**

In the original induction trial, remission at week 4 occurred in **33% with infliximab versus 4% with placebo**, an absolute difference of 29 percentage points. Among initial responders in ACCENT I, remission at week 30 occurred in **39% with infliximab 5 mg/kg every eight weeks and 45% with 10 mg/kg, versus 21% with placebo/episodic treatment**. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

In biologic- and immunomodulator-naive patients in SONIC, corticosteroid-free remission at week 26 was:

* **56.8%:** infliximab plus azathioprine
* **44.4%:** infliximab alone
* **30.0%:** azathioprine alone

Corresponding mucosal-healing rates were 43.9%, 30.1%, and 16.5%. Serious infections occurred in 3.9%, 4.9%, and 5.6%, respectively, during the trial. Thus, combination therapy improved efficacy, although its longer-term immunosuppression and lymphoma trade-off is important. ([PubMed][2])

For draining abdominal or perianal fistulas, a separate randomized trial found complete fistula closure in **55% with infliximab 5 mg/kg versus 13% with placebo**. This remains unusually direct randomized evidence for fistulizing Crohn’s disease. ([PubMed][3])

**Adalimumab**

In CLASSIC-I, week-4 remission occurred in **36% receiving the 160/80-mg induction regimen versus 12% receiving placebo**. Injection-site reactions were more frequent, but overall adverse-event rates were otherwise broadly similar. ([PubMed][4])

In CHARM, among patients entering the maintenance phase, remission at week 56 occurred in **36% with every-other-week adalimumab and 41% with weekly treatment, versus 12% with placebo**. Weekly treatment did not show a statistically significant efficacy advantage over every-other-week dosing in the randomized comparison. ([PubMed][5])

**Certolizumab pegol**

PRECiSE-1 showed a modest improvement in clinical response, but **induction remission was not significantly better than placebo**. Serious adverse events occurred in 10% with certolizumab and 7% with placebo; serious infections occurred in approximately 2% and under 1%, respectively. ([PubMed][6])

Among week-6 responders in PRECiSE-2, remission at week 26 was **48% with continued certolizumab versus 29% after withdrawal to placebo**. Serious infectious events occurred in 3% versus under 1%, including one case of pulmonary tuberculosis. ([PubMed][7])

### Anti-integrin therapy

**Vedolizumab**

In GEMINI 2, week-6 remission occurred in **14.5% with vedolizumab versus 6.8% with placebo**. Among induction responders, week-52 remission was **39.0% with dosing every eight weeks and 36.4% every four weeks, versus 21.6% with placebo withdrawal**. No clear benefit of every-four-week over every-eight-week dosing was demonstrated in that randomized maintenance comparison. ([PubMed][8])

In GEMINI 3, which largely involved patients in whom anti-TNF treatment had failed, week-6 remission was not significantly different—15.2% versus 12.1%—but by week 10 it was **26.6% versus 12.1%**. This suggests that vedolizumab may have a slower onset, particularly after prior anti-TNF failure. ([ScienceDirect][9])

**Natalizumab**

ENACT-1 did not show a significant induction-remission advantage at week 10—37% versus 30%—but among responders in ENACT-2, sustained remission through week 36 was **44% with natalizumab versus 26% after placebo withdrawal**. ENCORE subsequently showed sustained remission during weeks 8–12 in **26% versus 16%** among patients with active inflammation and elevated C-reactive protein. ([PubMed][10])

The major distinguishing harm is **progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy**, including a fatal case reported in a patient with Crohn’s disease exposed to natalizumab. ([PubMed][11])

### IL-12/23 blockade

**Ustekinumab**

UNITI-1 enrolled patients with anti-TNF failure; UNITI-2 enrolled those with failure of conventional treatment. Week-6 clinical response occurred in:

* UNITI-1: **33.7%–34.3% with ustekinumab versus 21.5% with placebo**
* UNITI-2: **51.7%–55.5% versus 28.7%**

Among ustekinumab induction responders, week-44 remission was **53.1% with every-eight-week dosing and 48.8% every 12 weeks, versus 35.9% after withdrawal to placebo**. Adverse-event rates were similar among treatment groups. ([PubMed][12])

In SEAVUE, a direct monotherapy comparison in biologic-naive patients, week-52 remission was **65% with ustekinumab versus 61% with adalimumab**, without a statistically significant difference. Serious infections occurred in 2% and 3%, respectively. ([PubMed][13])

Long-term extension data suggest sustained benefit in selected patients remaining in follow-up, but attrition limits interpretation. In an intention-to-treat analysis, remission at five years was **34.4% with every-eight-week and 28.7% with every-12-week ustekinumab**; serious infection rates were similar to the comparator exposure. ([PubMed][14])

### Selective IL-23 blockade

**Risankizumab**

In the ADVANCE induction trial, week-12 remission was **42%–45% with risankizumab versus 25% with placebo**. In MOTIVATE, which enrolled patients with prior biologic failure, remission was **40%–42% versus 20%**. Endoscopic response was also significantly improved in both trials. Overall treatment-emergent adverse-event rates were similar among groups. ([PubMed][15])

Among induction responders in FORTIFY, week-52 remission was approximately **52%–55% with continued risankizumab versus 41% after placebo withdrawal**; endoscopic response was 47% with either maintenance dose versus 22% with placebo withdrawal. ([PubMed][16])

SEQUENCE directly compared risankizumab with ustekinumab after anti-TNF failure. Clinical remission at week 24 was **58.6% versus 39.5%**, meeting the noninferiority criterion, while endoscopic remission at week 48 was **31.8% versus 16.2%**, demonstrating superiority for risankizumab. Serious adverse events occurred in 10.3% versus 17.4%, with much of the difference attributed to worsening Crohn’s disease. ([New England Journal of Medicine][17])

**Mirikizumab**

VIVID-1 used composite endpoints incorporating both early response and one-year outcomes. Clinical response at week 12 followed by CDAI remission at week 52 occurred in **45.4% with mirikizumab versus 19.6% with placebo**. Clinical response followed by endoscopic response occurred in 38.0% versus 9.0%.

Serious adverse events occurred in **10.3% with mirikizumab, 10.7% with ustekinumab, and 17.1% with placebo**. None of the three deaths in the study was considered treatment-related. The active-comparator component was useful, but the trial’s formally controlled primary superiority comparisons were against placebo rather than a simple mirikizumab-versus-ustekinumab remission comparison. ([PubMed][18])

**Guselkumab**

GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3 also used composite treat-through endpoints. Clinical response at week 12 followed by remission at week 48 occurred in:

* GALAXI-2: **49%–55% with guselkumab versus 12% with placebo**
* GALAXI-3: **47%–48% versus 13%**

Clinical response followed by endoscopic response occurred in 34%–39% versus 5%–6%. Serious adverse events occurred in 7%–11% with guselkumab, 12% with ustekinumab, and 15% with placebo; no deaths were reported. Guselkumab also outperformed ustekinumab across multiple prespecified week-48 outcomes, although the abstract’s primary numerical comparisons were presented against placebo. ([PubMed][19])

### Oral selective JAK1 inhibition

**Upadacitinib**

In the U-EXCEL and U-EXCEED induction trials, week-12 remission occurred in:

* U-EXCEL: **49.5% with upadacitinib versus 29.1% with placebo**
* U-EXCEED: **38.9% versus 21.1%**

Endoscopic response was **45.5% versus 13.1%** and **34.6% versus 3.5%**, respectively. ([PubMed][20])

In the final U-ENDURE maintenance report, among induction responders, week-52 remission occurred in **36.2% with 15 mg and 51.5% with 30 mg, versus 15.2% with placebo withdrawal**. Endoscopic response occurred in 27.3%, 40.7%, and 7.2%, respectively. ([PubMed][21])

Important observed harms included:

* More herpes zoster with the higher induction and maintenance doses
* More hepatic laboratory disorders and neutropenia with 30-mg maintenance
* Several gastrointestinal perforations during the development program

The final maintenance trial reported no deaths, tuberculosis, lymphoma, major cardiovascular events, or venous thromboembolism, but its size and duration cannot exclude uncommon risks. ([PubMed][20])

## What the evidence suggests overall

**For inducing symptomatic remission**, substantial placebo-adjusted benefits were demonstrated for infliximab, adalimumab, ustekinumab, selective IL-23 inhibitors, and upadacitinib. Vedolizumab’s early induction difference was smaller and its onset appeared slower after anti-TNF failure. Certolizumab’s first induction trial did not demonstrate a significant remission advantage.

**For maintaining remission**, continued treatment consistently reduced relapse among initial responders. The largest maintenance percentages should not automatically be interpreted as proof that one drug is superior because trial populations, prior biologic exposure, endpoints, rescue rules, and withdrawal designs differed.

The most informative direct comparisons were:

* Infliximab plus azathioprine was more effective than either alone in SONIC.
* Ustekinumab and adalimumab had similar one-year clinical remission in biologic-naive patients in SEAVUE.
* Risankizumab produced better endoscopic remission than ustekinumab after anti-TNF failure in SEQUENCE.
* Guselkumab outperformed ustekinumab across multiple prespecified one-year outcomes in GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3. ([PubMed][2])

## Harms that may not be fully captured in the randomized trials

Randomized Crohn’s trials generally found similar overall adverse-event rates between biologic and placebo groups, but they were not large enough to exclude rare events.

A prospective TREAT registry analysis involving more than 6,000 patients found similar overall mortality with and without infliximab. After adjustment, infliximab exposure was associated with an increased serious-infection risk, with a hazard ratio of **1.43**; prednisone, narcotic use, and greater Crohn’s severity were also independent risk factors. Because treatment was not randomized, residual confounding by disease severity remains possible. ([PubMed][22])

A nationwide primary cohort study found that lymphoma risk was elevated with either thiopurine or anti-TNF monotherapy and was **highest with combination anti-TNF plus thiopurine therapy**. The absolute risk remained low, but this is an important counterweight to the greater efficacy seen with infliximab–azathioprine combination treatment in SONIC. ([PubMed][23])

An evidence-based interpretation—not an individualized recommendation—is therefore:

* **Infliximab** has particularly direct evidence for fistulizing disease and strong efficacy when combined with azathioprine, but combination immunosuppression increases the importance of infection and lymphoma discussions.
* **Vedolizumab** offers a gut-selective mechanism and reassuring trial safety, but can act more slowly.
* **Ustekinumab and selective IL-23 agents** produced favorable short-term safety results and meaningful clinical/endoscopic benefits; rare long-term risks remain less precisely estimated for the newer agents.
* **Upadacitinib** produced rapid and relatively large induction and maintenance effects, including after prior treatment failure, but had more evident dose-related infectious and laboratory toxicity and observed gastrointestinal perforations.
* **Natalizumab** is distinguished by its potentially fatal PML risk.

Your own decision also depends on prior biologic exposure, active fistulas or abscesses, disease location and endoscopic severity, infection history, age, malignancy history, pregnancy plans, vaccination status, thrombotic or cardiovascular risk, and whether rapid onset or oral therapy is especially important.
