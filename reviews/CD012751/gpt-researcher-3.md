## Scope

This is a targeted, trial-level evidence map of randomized primary studies published through July 18, 2026. I focused on adults with moderate-to-severe active luminal Crohn’s disease, with fistulizing disease considered separately. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, editorials, comments, and post-hoc analyses from the evidence synthesis.

Important comparability issues:

* Older trials generally defined clinical remission as **CDAI <150**; newer studies frequently use symptom-based **PRO2 remission**, endoscopic response, or composite endpoints.
* Most maintenance trials enrolled or randomized **only patients who responded to induction**. Their remission percentages therefore apply to enriched responder populations, not to everyone starting treatment.
* Prior biologic exposure differs substantially between trials. Cross-trial ranking based on crude remission percentages would be misleading.

## Benefits

### Anti-TNF therapy

**Infliximab** has convincing induction and maintenance evidence. In the original induction study, week-4 clinical remission occurred in approximately 33% receiving infliximab versus 4% receiving placebo. In ACCENT I, among initial responders, week-30 remission was 39%–45% with scheduled infliximab versus 21% with placebo withdrawal. ([New England Journal of Medicine][1])

SONIC provides important comparative evidence in immunomodulator- and biologic-naive patients: week-26 corticosteroid-free remission was 56.8% with infliximab plus azathioprine, 44.4% with infliximab alone, and 30.0% with azathioprine alone. Combination therapy therefore produced greater benefit than either monotherapy in that population. ([PubMed][2])

For fistulizing Crohn’s disease, ACCENT II found complete absence of draining fistulas at week 54 in 36% continuing infliximab versus 19% switched to placebo. ([PubMed][3])

**Adalimumab** induced remission in biologic-naive patients in CLASSIC-I: 36% with the 160/80-mg regimen versus 12% with placebo at week 4. In GAIN, involving patients with infliximab intolerance or loss of response, week-4 remission was 21% versus 7%. ([PubMed][4])

Among induction responders in CHARM, remission at week 56 was 36% with every-other-week adalimumab and 41% with weekly adalimumab versus 12% with placebo withdrawal. EXTEND also demonstrated endoscopic benefit: mucosal healing at week 52 was 24% with continued adalimumab versus 0% after withdrawal. ([PubMed][5])

**Certolizumab pegol** produced more modest and endpoint-dependent results. In PRECISE 1, response rates were statistically higher, but remission was not significantly improved at weeks 6 or 26. Among open-label induction responders in PRECISE 2, however, week-26 remission was 48% with continued certolizumab versus 29% with withdrawal. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

### Integrin-directed therapy

**Vedolizumab** produced a relatively modest early induction effect in GEMINI 2: week-6 clinical remission was 14.5% versus 6.8% with placebo, while the CDAI-100 response endpoint was not statistically significant. Among induction responders, week-52 remission was 39.0% with dosing every eight weeks and 36.4% every four weeks versus 21.6% with placebo withdrawal. ([PubMed][7])

In GEMINI 3, which concentrated on patients with prior anti-TNF failure, the week-6 remission endpoint was negative—15.2% versus 12.1%—but a later week-10 difference emerged, 26.6% versus 12.1%. This supports a slower induction effect in treatment-refractory patients. ([PubMed][8])

**Natalizumab** did not significantly improve remission in the broad ENACT-1 induction population at week 10, but continuation among induction responders in ENACT-2 increased sustained remission through week 36, 44% versus 26%. ENCORE, restricted to patients with elevated C-reactive protein, found sustained remission in 26% versus 16%. ([New England Journal of Medicine][9])

### IL-12/23 and selective IL-23 inhibition

**Ustekinumab** improved induction response in both treatment-refractory and less heavily treated populations. In UNITI-1, week-6 response was approximately 34% with ustekinumab versus 21.5% with placebo; in UNITI-2 it was approximately 52%–56% versus 28.7%. Among induction responders in IM-UNITI, week-44 remission was 53.1% with eight-week dosing and 48.8% with twelve-week dosing versus 35.9% after withdrawal. ([PubMed][10])

In the biologic-naive SEAVUE head-to-head trial, ustekinumab did **not** demonstrate superiority to adalimumab: week-52 clinical remission was 65% versus 61%, respectively. ([PubMed][11])

**Risankizumab** showed consistent clinical and endoscopic effects. In ADVANCE, CDAI remission at week 12 was 42%–45% versus 25% with placebo, and endoscopic response was 32%–40% versus 12%. In the more treatment-refractory MOTIVATE population, remission was 40%–42% versus 20%, and endoscopic response was 29%–34% versus 11%. ([PubMed][12])

Among induction responders in FORTIFY, week-52 CDAI remission was approximately 52%–55% with risankizumab versus 40%–41% after withdrawal; endoscopic response was approximately 47% versus 22%. ([PubMed][13])

SEQUENCE provides particularly useful head-to-head evidence after anti-TNF failure. Risankizumab was noninferior to ustekinumab for week-24 clinical remission, 58.6% versus 39.5%, and superior for week-48 endoscopic remission, 31.8% versus 16.2%. ([PubMed][14])

**Guselkumab** produced substantial induction effects in GALAXI-1, with week-12 CDAI remission ranging from approximately 46% to 57% across doses versus 16% with placebo. ([PubMed][15])

In the phase 3 GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3 treat-through trials, approximately 47%–55% of guselkumab-treated participants achieved the composite of week-12 clinical response and week-48 clinical remission, compared with 12%–13% receiving placebo. Composite endoscopic outcomes also favored guselkumab. ([PubMed][16])

The all-subcutaneous GRAVITI study found week-12 remission in 56.1% versus 21.4% with placebo and endoscopic response in 41.3% versus 21.4%. At week 48, clinical remission was approximately 60%–66% with continued guselkumab versus 17% in the placebo-withdrawal group. ([ScienceDirect][17])

**Mirikizumab** was evaluated in the treat-through VIVID-1 trial. The composite of week-12 symptom response and week-52 CDAI remission occurred in 45.4% with mirikizumab versus 19.6% with placebo; the corresponding composite ending in week-52 endoscopic response occurred in 38.0% versus 9.0%. ([PubMed][18])

### JAK inhibition

**Upadacitinib** produced clinically and endoscopically significant induction effects in U-EXCEL and U-EXCEED:

* U-EXCEL week-12 remission: 49.5% versus 29.1%; endoscopic response: 45.5% versus 13.1%.
* U-EXCEED week-12 remission: 38.9% versus 21.1%; endoscopic response: 34.6% versus 3.5%.

In the original U-ENDURE maintenance analysis, week-52 remission was 37.3% with 15 mg and 47.6% with 30 mg versus 15.1% with placebo withdrawal. ([PubMed][19])

The final U-ENDURE analysis of all 673 randomized participants was similar: remission was 36.2% with 15 mg and 51.5% with 30 mg versus 15.2% with placebo; endoscopic response was 27.3%, 40.7%, and 7.2%, respectively. ([PubMed][20])

### Mixed or unsuccessful advanced-treatment programs

These are relevant to a complete harms-and-benefits assessment:

* **Filgotinib:** FITZROY showed a positive phase 2 induction signal, but the phase 3 DIVERSITY induction program did not meet all coprimary endpoints. The 200-mg dose did meet maintenance remission and endoscopic endpoints. ([PubMed][21])
* **Tofacitinib:** phase 2b induction and maintenance remission differences were not statistically significant. ([PubMed][22])
* **Etrolizumab:** BERGAMOT did not meet the induction remission or endoscopic endpoints, although some week-66 maintenance outcomes favored etrolizumab. ([PubMed][23])
* **Brodalumab:** the anti-IL-17 receptor trial was stopped early because of an imbalance in worsening Crohn’s disease and showed no meaningful efficacy. ([PubMed][24])

## Harms

The randomized trials are useful for common and intermediate-frequency adverse events but are generally underpowered for rare outcomes such as malignancy, opportunistic infection, tuberculosis, venous thrombosis, or major cardiovascular events.

**Anti-TNF agents:** Serious infection is the principal recurring concern. In PRECISE 1, serious infection occurred in 2% receiving certolizumab and less than 1% receiving placebo; anti-certolizumab antibodies developed in 8%. PRECISE 2 reported one tuberculosis case. In SONIC, serious infection rates were 3.9% with combination therapy, 4.9% with infliximab, and 5.6% with azathioprine, although follow-up was not sufficient to exclude uncommon long-term harms. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

**Natalizumab:** One participant developed fatal progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy during an open-label extension associated with the ENACT program. This rare but serious central nervous system infection materially changes its benefit–harm profile. ([New England Journal of Medicine][9])

**Vedolizumab:** Infections and serious infections were numerically somewhat higher with vedolizumab in GEMINI 2, but overall trial findings did not reveal a specific opportunistic-infection or neurologic signal comparable to natalizumab. Interpretation is complicated because serious adverse-event counts include Crohn’s disease complications and because exposure differed between maintenance groups. ([PubMed][7])

**Ustekinumab and selective IL-23 inhibitors:** Overall and serious adverse-event rates were generally similar to placebo or active comparators in UNITI, SEAVUE, ADVANCE/MOTIVATE, FORTIFY, SEQUENCE, GALAXI, and VIVID-1. These trials nevertheless have limited power and follow-up for very rare infections or malignancies. In VIVID-1, serious adverse events occurred in 10.3% with mirikizumab, 10.7% with ustekinumab, and 17.1% with placebo; none of the three deaths was considered treatment-related. ([PubMed][10])

**Upadacitinib:** Herpes zoster occurred more frequently with upadacitinib, particularly the 45-mg induction and 30-mg maintenance regimens. Hepatic laboratory disorders and neutropenia were more frequent with 30 mg. Gastrointestinal perforations occurred in four induction participants receiving 45 mg and one participant in each active maintenance-dose group in the original analysis. The complete U-ENDURE population reported no deaths, active tuberculosis, lymphoma, nonmelanoma skin cancer, adjudicated major cardiovascular events, or venous thromboembolism during the controlled maintenance period, but zoster remained more frequent than with placebo. ([PubMed][19])

**Anti-IL-17 therapy:** Brodalumab’s worsening-disease imbalance is an important mechanism-specific harm signal rather than simply a failure to demonstrate efficacy. ([PubMed][24])

## Overall interpretation

The most defensible conclusions from the individual trials are:

1. **Infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, risankizumab, guselkumab, mirikizumab, and upadacitinib** each have randomized evidence of benefit for at least one induction and maintenance remission or endoscopic endpoint.
2. **Certolizumab** has convincing responder-enriched maintenance evidence, but its unselected PRECISE-1 trial showed response rather than remission benefit.
3. Direct comparative evidence is limited. SONIC supports infliximab–azathioprine combination therapy over either monotherapy in selected naive patients; SEAVUE found no superiority of ustekinumab over adalimumab; SEQUENCE favored risankizumab over ustekinumab, especially for endoscopic remission after anti-TNF failure. ([PubMed][2])
4. Clinical remission, endoscopic response, endoscopic remission, and corticosteroid-free remission should be synthesized separately. Maintenance trials should also be stratified into **re-randomized responders**, **treat-through trials**, and **withdrawal designs**.
