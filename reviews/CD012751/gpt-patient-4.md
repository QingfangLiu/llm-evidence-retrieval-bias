## Scope

This is a **primary-study-only evidence map** focused on adults with moderate-to-severe, predominantly luminal Crohn’s disease. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, consensus statements, reviews, and editorials.

Trial percentages should **not be compared directly across drugs**. Populations, prior biologic exposure, endpoint definitions, corticosteroid tapering, and assessment times differed. Many maintenance studies enrolled only people who had already responded during induction—an enriched “randomized-withdrawal” design that produces higher remission rates than would be expected among everyone starting treatment.

## Overall findings

Advanced therapies consistently improved the chance of symptomatic remission compared with placebo. More recent trials also required objective endoscopic improvement, which is a more persuasive indicator of inflammation control than symptom-based CDAI remission alone.

The most clinically useful comparative findings were:

* In biologic-naive patients, **ustekinumab and adalimumab produced similar one-year clinical remission** in SEAVUE: 65% versus 61%. ([PubMed][1])
* After anti-TNF failure, **risankizumab was superior to ustekinumab for endoscopic remission** at week 48 in SEQUENCE: 31.8% versus 16.2%, with similar reported adverse-event incidence. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])
* **Infliximab plus azathioprine** was more effective than either alone in immunomodulator- and biologic-naive patients in SONIC, although the added long-term risks of combination immunosuppression cannot be reliably determined from this relatively short trial. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])
* **Upadacitinib showed large clinical and endoscopic effects**, including in a heavily biologic-experienced population, but had distinctive trial safety findings including herpes zoster, liver abnormalities, neutropenia and uncommon gastrointestinal perforations. ([New England Journal of Medicine][4])
* Selective **IL-23 inhibitors**—risankizumab, mirikizumab and guselkumab—produced substantial clinical and endoscopic benefits with generally similar or lower short-term serious-adverse-event rates than placebo in their pivotal trials. ([ScienceDirect][5])

## Benefits and harms by treatment

### Infliximab — anti-TNF infusion

**Induction:** In the original randomized trial, one infliximab infusion produced clinical remission at week 4 in 33% versus 4% with placebo. Clinical response occurred in 65% across infliximab doses versus 17% with placebo. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

**Maintenance:** Among patients who responded to the initial infusion in ACCENT I, week-30 remission occurred in 39% with continued 5-mg/kg infliximab and 45% with the dose-escalation regimen, versus 21% after withdrawal to placebo. Median time to loss of response was also substantially longer with scheduled treatment. ([PubMed][7])

**Harms:** Overall adverse effects and serious infections were similar between groups in these trials, although infusion reactions occurred. These studies were not large enough to quantify rare serious infections, malignancies or neurologic events. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

**Combination therapy:** In SONIC, corticosteroid-free remission at week 26 was 56.8% with infliximab plus azathioprine, 44.4% with infliximab alone and 30.0% with azathioprine alone. Serious infections occurred in 3.9%, 4.9% and 5.6%, respectively, during the trial. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])

### Adalimumab — anti-TNF injection

**Induction:** In CLASSIC-I, the currently recognizable 160-mg/80-mg induction regimen produced week-4 remission in 36% versus 12% with placebo. Injection-site reactions were more frequent with adalimumab. ([PubMed][8])

**Maintenance:** Among induction responders in CHARM, remission at week 56 occurred in 36% with every-other-week treatment and 41% with weekly treatment, versus 12% with placebo. No significant efficacy difference was demonstrated between the two active schedules. ([PubMed][9])

**Head-to-head:** In biologic-naive SEAVUE participants, week-52 remission did not differ significantly between adalimumab and ustekinumab—61% and 65%, respectively. Serious infections occurred in 3% and 2%. Dose optimization was not permitted, which differs from routine practice. ([PubMed][1])

### Certolizumab pegol — anti-TNF injection

**Induction:** PRECiSE 1 found only a modest improvement in clinical response—35% versus 27% at week 6—and no statistically significant improvement in clinical remission. ([PubMed][10])

**Maintenance:** Among induction responders in PRECiSE 2, week-26 remission was 48% with certolizumab versus 29% after withdrawal to placebo. ([New England Journal of Medicine][11])

**Harms:** Serious infectious adverse events occurred in 3% versus under 1% in PRECiSE 2, including one case of pulmonary tuberculosis. Anti-drug antibodies developed in approximately 9% of participants entering induction. ([PubMed][12])

### Vedolizumab — gut-selective anti-integrin

**Induction:** GEMINI 2 found week-6 remission in 14.5% versus 6.8% with placebo. The trial did not meet its other primary induction endpoint of CDAI-100 response, suggesting a relatively modest or slower early effect. ([New England Journal of Medicine][13])

**Maintenance:** Among vedolizumab responders, week-52 remission was 39.0% with dosing every 8 weeks and 36.4% every 4 weeks, versus 21.6% after withdrawal to placebo. ([New England Journal of Medicine][13])

**Harms:** In the integrated trial, serious adverse events occurred in 24.4% versus 15.3%, and serious infections in 5.5% versus 3.0%. Some adverse events reflected worsening Crohn’s disease rather than direct drug toxicity, but the trial did report more overall serious events with vedolizumab. ([New England Journal of Medicine][13])

### Ustekinumab — IL-12/23 p40 inhibitor

**Induction:** In UNITI-1, involving prior anti-TNF failures, clinical response at week 6 was approximately 34% with ustekinumab versus 21.5% with placebo. In UNITI-2, which included conventional-treatment failures, response was 55.5% versus 28.7%. The approximately 6-mg/kg regimen also significantly increased week-8 clinical remission in both populations. ([New England Journal of Medicine][14])

**Maintenance:** Among ustekinumab induction responders, remission at week 44 was 53.1% with every-8-week treatment and 48.8% with every-12-week treatment, versus 35.9% after withdrawal to placebo. Adverse-event rates were similar between groups. ([New England Journal of Medicine][14])

**Comparison with adalimumab:** No significant remission advantage was demonstrated for either drug in biologic-naive SEAVUE participants. ([PubMed][1])

### Risankizumab — selective IL-23 p19 inhibitor

**Induction:** With the 600-mg regimen in ADVANCE, week-12 CDAI remission was 45% versus 25% and endoscopic response was 40% versus 12%. In the biologic-failure MOTIVATE population, remission was 42% versus 20% and endoscopic response 29% versus 11%. Overall adverse-event incidence was similar between groups. ([ScienceDirect][5])

**Maintenance:** Among induction responders in FORTIFY, the 360-mg maintenance regimen produced week-52 clinical remission in 52% versus 41% after withdrawal and endoscopic response in 47% versus 22%. Adverse-event rates were similar, with worsening Crohn’s disease, arthralgia and headache among the most frequently reported events. ([ScienceDirect][5])

**After anti-TNF failure:** In SEQUENCE, risankizumab was noninferior to ustekinumab for week-24 clinical remission—58.6% versus 39.5%—and superior for week-48 endoscopic remission—31.8% versus 16.2%. Adverse-event incidence appeared similar. ([New England Journal of Medicine][2])

### Guselkumab — selective IL-23 p19 inhibitor

**Subcutaneous induction:** In GRAVITI, week-12 clinical remission was 56.1% with guselkumab versus 21.4% with placebo; endoscopic response was 41.3% versus 21.4%. ([ScienceDirect][15])

**Treat-through maintenance:** At week 48, clinical remission was 60.0% or 66.1% with the two maintenance regimens versus 17.1% in the placebo-assigned group; endoscopic response was 44.3% or 51.3% versus 6.8%. Adverse-event rates were not higher with guselkumab. ([ScienceDirect][15])

**Intravenous induction:** The separate GALAXI-2 and GALAXI-3 trials also demonstrated superiority over placebo for composite endpoints requiring both week-12 clinical response and week-48 clinical remission or endoscopic response. Serious adverse events occurred in 7%–11% of guselkumab groups, 12% with ustekinumab and 15% with placebo; no deaths were reported. ([PubMed][16])

### Mirikizumab — selective IL-23 p19 inhibitor

VIVID-1 used a treat-through design rather than re-randomizing responders. Clinical response at week 12 followed by endoscopic response at week 52 occurred in 38.0% with mirikizumab versus 9.0% with placebo. Clinical response followed by week-52 CDAI remission occurred in 45.4% versus 19.6%. Serious adverse events occurred in 10.3% with mirikizumab, 10.7% with ustekinumab and 17.1% with placebo. No death was considered treatment-related. ([PubMed][17])

Because these are composite endpoints, the percentages are not directly equivalent to a conventional induction-remission rate or a responder-enriched maintenance-remission rate.

### Upadacitinib — oral selective JAK inhibitor

**Induction:** At week 12, remission was 49.5% versus 29.1% in U-EXCEL and 38.9% versus 21.1% in U-EXCEED. Endoscopic response was 45.5% versus 13.1% and 34.6% versus 3.5%, respectively. ([New England Journal of Medicine][4])

**Maintenance:** Among induction responders, week-52 remission was 37.3% with 15 mg and 47.6% with 30 mg, versus 15.1% after withdrawal to placebo. Endoscopic response was 27.6%, 40.1% and 7.3%, respectively. ([New England Journal of Medicine][4])

**Harms:** Herpes zoster was more frequent with induction-dose and 30-mg therapy. Hepatic disorders and neutropenia were more frequent with 30 mg. Gastrointestinal perforations occurred in four induction patients receiving 45 mg and in one patient in each active maintenance-dose group. The individual trials were not sufficiently large to settle questions about rare cardiovascular, thrombotic or malignancy outcomes. ([New England Journal of Medicine][4])

## How to interpret the harm evidence

The pivotal trials are strongest for **common short-term adverse events**, infections and laboratory abnormalities. They are substantially weaker for events occurring once per several thousand patient-years, risks that emerge after several years, pregnancy outcomes or risks concentrated in particular comorbidity groups.

Consequently, findings such as “similar adverse-event rates to placebo” should be interpreted as **no clear difference detected during that trial**, not proof that the therapy has no rare or long-term risks. Most trials also excluded active serious infection, uncontrolled abscesses and a number of major comorbidities, limiting applicability to higher-risk patients. ([New England Journal of Medicine][3])
