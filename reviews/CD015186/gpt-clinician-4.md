## Scope

I use **SC-MIGS** narrowly here: procedures that incise, excise, ablate, or dilate the trabecular meshwork/Schlemm’s canal—such as GATT, ab interno canaloplasty, suture or microhook trabeculotomy, Kahook Dual Blade goniotomy, Trabectome, and excimer-laser trabeculotomy. I have not treated iStent or Hydrus implantation as SC-MIGS interventions, although one trial used iStent as a comparator.

The evidence summary below cites **individual primary studies only**, prioritizing randomized comparative trials and adding comparative cohorts where they provide useful information about durability or uncommon harms.

## Clinical bottom line

SC-MIGS generally produces a **modest-to-moderate reduction in IOP and medication burden**, while avoiding a conjunctival bleb. The incremental benefit over cataract surgery alone is inconsistent and appears strongly influenced by baseline IOP:

* When preoperative IOP was approximately **25–27 mmHg**, adding microhook trabeculotomy to phacoemulsification produced substantially greater pressure reduction than phaco alone.
* When preoperative IOP was already approximately **17 mmHg**, adding KDB produced little demonstrable incremental benefit.
* In eyes with relatively low treated pressures, the principal benefit may be **reducing medications rather than lowering IOP much further**. ([PubMed][1])

Among standalone procedures, circumferential trabeculotomy—particularly GATT—may lower IOP more effectively than canaloplasty alone or limited needle goniotomy, but causes more postoperative blood reflux and hyphema. ([PubMed][2])

SC-MIGS should not be assumed to reproduce the very low pressures achievable with trabeculectomy. A very small randomized trial was inconclusive, while a larger retrospective comparison found substantially lower IOP and greater two-year success after trabeculectomy, at the cost of more postoperative complications. ([PubMed][3])

## Benefits found in comparative studies

### Added to cataract surgery

**Microhook trabeculotomy**

In a 114-eye randomized trial, phaco-microhook trabeculotomy reduced mean IOP from 26.5 to 12.5 mmHg at one year, compared with 25.3 to 20.0 mmHg after phaco alone. At two years, mean IOP was 15.2 versus 18.2 mmHg, respectively. Medication use decreased in the combined group but not in the phaco-alone group. Interpretation is complicated by a baseline imbalance in medication burden between groups. ([PubMed][1])

**Circumferential suture trabeculotomy**

A pilot RCT of only 18 eyes found mean IOPs of 11.8 mmHg after combined surgery and 14.6 mmHg after phaco alone at 24 months. Medication-free composite success was more frequent with trabeculotomy, although direct mean-IOP comparisons at individual visits were not statistically significant and only 14 eyes attended the 24-month visit. ([PubMed][4])

**Kahook Dual Blade**

Results depend on the study population:

* In a 42-eye RCT with baseline IOP around 17 mmHg, adding KDB did not improve 12-month IOP or medication use compared with phaco alone. ([PubMed][5])
* In the 120-eye Swedish randomized trial, IOP reduction was similar after phaco-KDB, phaco-iStent, and phaco alone—approximately 3 mmHg—but KDB and iStent reduced medications more than phaco alone. ([ScienceDirect][6])

This pattern suggests that when treated IOP is already in the mid-teens, expectations should focus more on **drop reduction** than on a large additional pressure decrease.

### Standalone SC-MIGS comparisons

**GATT versus ab interno canaloplasty**

In 77 randomized eyes, 12-month mean IOP was 16.0 mmHg after GATT and 19.0 mmHg after ABiC. Medication-free rates were 77.8% and 57.2%, respectively, but that difference did not reach statistical significance. Additional glaucoma surgery was required in one GATT and three ABiC eyes. ([PubMed][2])

**GATT versus bent-needle goniotomy**

In a randomized trial of 45 operated pseudophakic POAG eyes, complete success was 60.1% with GATT versus 4.5% with bent ab interno needle goniotomy; qualified success was 64.7% versus 22.7%. After medication washout at 12 months, however, the between-group IOP difference was imprecise and not statistically significant. ([PubMed][7])

**Extent of trabeculotomy**

A 99-eye RCT found no meaningful difference in IOP, medications, or success between 360°, upper-180°, and lower-180° suture trabeculotomy. Across all groups, mean IOP decreased from 18.6 to 13.7 mmHg and medications from 3.1 to 1.4 at 12 months. Thus, a larger incision did not produce greater pressure lowering. ([BMJ Best Practice][8])

### Versus laser treatment

A 30-eye RCT compared excimer-laser trabeculotomy with 180° SLT in medically refractory POAG. At two years, IOP decreased from 25.0 to 17.6 mmHg with excimer trabeculotomy and from 23.9 to 19.1 mmHg with SLT. Complete success was 53.3% versus 40%, but the small trial did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in success. ([Nature][9])

### Versus trabeculectomy

A prematurely terminated 19-patient RCT found similar mean IOP at one year after phaco-Trabectome and phaco-trabeculectomy—16.8 and 17.1 mmHg—but was far too small to establish equivalence or comparative safety. Severe complications occurred only after phaco-trabeculectomy, and one Trabectome eye required further glaucoma surgery. ([PubMed][3])

A larger nonrandomized cohort gives a different—and probably more clinically realistic—picture for uncontrolled glaucoma:

* Trabectome: IOP 28.1 to 15.9 mmHg; two-year success 22.4%.
* Trabeculectomy: IOP 26.3 to 10.2 mmHg; two-year success 76.1%.
* Further glaucoma procedures: 43.5% versus 10.8%.
* Complications other than hyphema were more frequent after trabeculectomy. ([PubMed][10])

Because this comparison was observational, selection bias is possible. Nevertheless, it supports counseling that SC-MIGS may offer a gentler safety profile but is generally **less likely to reach a low-teens or single-digit target**.

## Harms

### Hyphema and blood reflux

These are the signature adverse effects of trabecular/Schlemm’s-canal procedures:

* In the 18-eye suture-trabeculotomy pilot, intraoperative reflux bleeding occurred in every treated eye; 22% developed postoperative hyphema, which resolved within one week. ([PMC][11])
* With 360° versus lower-180° suture trabeculotomy, layered hyphema occurred in 26% versus 6%; one 360° eye required anterior-chamber washout. ([BMJ Best Practice][8])
* In the GATT-versus-ABiC trial, hyphema was reported in 87% versus 47%. ([PubMed][2])
* In the GATT-versus-BANG trial, transient hyphema occurred in 56.5% versus 40.9%. ([PubMed][7])
* In the microhook RCT, four eyes developed hyphema and one required washout. ([PubMed][1])

Most hyphemas resolved spontaneously, but they can temporarily reduce vision, delay recovery, precipitate an IOP rise, or occasionally require washout.

### Early IOP elevations

Transient IOP spikes occur after SC-MIGS. In a 197-eye KDB cohort, 10.2% had an IOP rise exceeding 10 mmHg at one week. In the small suture-trabeculotomy RCT, approximately one-third of eyes in both surgical groups had a postoperative spike requiring temporary systemic carbonic-anhydrase inhibitor treatment. ([PubMed][12])

### Supraciliary effusion

The GATT-versus-ABiC RCT reported supraciliary effusion in 92% of GATT and 71% of ABiC eyes. The report nevertheless described overall safety as favorable, but the high imaging-detected frequency deserves attention when interpreting early postoperative findings. ([PubMed][2])

### Need for subsequent treatment

Failure does not preclude later filtering or tube surgery because the conjunctiva is generally preserved. However, patients should understand that additional drops, SLT, or incisional surgery may still be necessary. The need is likely higher when the target pressure is very low or disease is advanced; the Trabectome-versus-trabeculectomy cohort found substantially more subsequent surgery after Trabectome. ([PubMed][10])

### Rare but serious events

Endophthalmitis, sustained hypotony, suprachoroidal hemorrhage, and permanent severe visual loss were uncommon or absent in these studies. That should **not** be interpreted as proof that they never occur: most trials included only tens of participants and were inadequately powered to characterize rare harms.

## What remains uncertain

These studies principally measured IOP, medication use, and heterogeneous composite “success” definitions. They do **not establish that SC-MIGS slows visual-field progression, preserves quality of life, or prevents blindness better than alternative treatments**. Follow-up was generally one to two years, with little adequately controlled evidence beyond that period. ([PubMed][5])

Clinically, the evidence best supports discussing SC-MIGS as:

* A conjunctiva-sparing option for mild-to-moderate OAG.
* Particularly reasonable when cataract surgery is already planned.
* More predictable for reducing drops or achieving pressures in the mid-teens than for achieving very low target pressures.
* Associated with frequent but usually transient hyphema and possible early IOP spikes.
* Less well supported for advanced glaucoma where a low-teens or single-digit target is necessary.
