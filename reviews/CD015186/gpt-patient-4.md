## Scope

I treated **Schlemm’s canal–based MIGS (SC-MIGS)** as ab-interno procedures that improve aqueous outflow through the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal:

* Trabecular bypass or intracanal scaffolds: **iStent/iStent inject, Hydrus**
* Trabecular excision or trabeculotomy: **Kahook Dual Blade, microhook trabeculotomy, GATT**
* Canal dilation with or without trabeculotomy: **ABiC/iTrack, OMNI**

I prioritized randomized and comparative human studies, then added prospective and larger observational cohorts where randomized data—especially safety data—were limited. This is a focused review of the most informative primary studies rather than every published case series.

## Main findings

### 1. Hydrus plus cataract surgery has the strongest long-term comparative evidence

In the HORIZON randomized trial, adding Hydrus to cataract surgery produced an additional mean unmedicated IOP reduction of about **2.3 mmHg** at two years compared with cataract surgery alone. Approximately **77% versus 58%** achieved at least a 20% unmedicated IOP reduction. Patients with Hydrus also used fewer medications. ([PubMed][1])

At five years:

* **66% versus 46%** were medication-free.
* **49.5% versus 33.8%** had IOP ≤18 mmHg without medication.
* Subsequent incisional glaucoma surgery occurred in **2.4% versus 6.2%**.
* Endothelial-cell loss was not significantly different between groups. ([PubMed][2])

A post hoc analysis found slower visual-field mean-deviation deterioration with Hydrus: approximately **−0.26 versus −0.49 dB/year**. This is encouraging, but it was not the trial’s prespecified primary outcome and therefore is less definitive than the IOP and medication results. ([PubMed][3])

An earlier randomized trial likewise found greater medication-free control after Hydrus plus cataract surgery than cataract surgery alone, although focal peripheral anterior synechiae around the implant were observed. ([PubMed][4])

### 2. iStent inject provides a modest additional benefit over cataract surgery alone

In the pivotal randomized iStent inject trial, at two years:

* **75.8% versus 61.9%** achieved at least a 20% unmedicated IOP reduction.
* Mean unmedicated IOP decreased by **7.0 versus 5.4 mmHg**—an incremental treatment effect of approximately **1.6 mmHg**.
* **63.2% versus 50.0%** had medication-free IOP ≤18 mmHg.
* Overall safety was similar to cataract surgery alone. ([PubMed][5])

First-generation iStent studies also showed reduced medication dependence, but some differences diminished by two years. ([PubMed][6])

For standalone treatment, two first-generation iStents maintained IOP control similar to travoprost over five years, while fewer stent-treated patients needed additional medication. This study involved treatment-naïve patients and does not directly answer whether standalone stents are preferable to other surgical choices in previously treated glaucoma. ([ScienceDirect][7])

In the randomized COMPARE study, standalone Hydrus produced greater medication reduction than two first-generation iStents at one year, while safety was broadly comparable. ([PubMed][8])

### 3. Trabecular cutting procedures may lower IOP substantially, but randomized evidence is inconsistent

Results depend heavily on baseline IOP and the particular procedure.

A randomized trial of **Kahook Dual Blade plus cataract surgery** found no statistically significant additional IOP or medication benefit over cataract surgery alone at 12 months. Baseline IOP was already relatively well controlled, leaving less room for improvement. ([PubMed][9])

By contrast, a randomized study of **Tanito microhook trabeculotomy plus cataract surgery** in eyes with substantially higher baseline IOP reported mean IOP of approximately **12.5 versus 20.0 mmHg** at one year. Four patients developed hyphema and one required anterior-chamber washout. ([PubMed][10]) A two-year report continued to favor microhook surgery and reported no new complications during the second year, but this remains a single-setting trial with baseline imbalances between groups. ([ScienceDirect][11])

A very small randomized trial—only 18 eyes—also favored 360-degree suture trabeculotomy plus cataract surgery over cataract surgery alone at two years. Its size makes the effect estimate uncertain. ([PubMed][12])

### 4. GATT can produce substantial reductions, particularly at higher starting pressures, but bleeding is common

In the original GATT cohort, primary open-angle glaucoma eyes experienced IOP reductions of approximately **8–11 mmHg**, with about one fewer medication. Approximately **30%** had transient hyphema at one week, and **9%** ultimately required additional glaucoma surgery. ([PubMed][13])

A randomized comparison of **GATT versus ab-interno canaloplasty** found lower IOP after GATT at one year—approximately **16 versus 19 mmHg**—but more detected hyphema: **87% versus 47%**. Supraciliary effusions were also frequently observed in both groups. Most bleeding after trabeculotomy is transient, but it can cause temporary blurred vision, delayed recovery and occasionally require intervention. ([PubMed][14])

### 5. OMNI and ab-interno canaloplasty results are promising, but most evidence lacks a cataract-only control group

The prospective, single-arm GEMINI study evaluated OMNI canaloplasty and trabeculotomy combined with cataract surgery. At 12 months:

* Unmedicated IOP decreased from approximately **23.8 to 15.6 mmHg**.
* Medications decreased from **1.8 to 0.4**.
* Approximately **80%** were medication-free.
* Transient hyphema occurred in **6%** and transient IOP elevation in **2%**. ([PMC][15])

Among 66 patients followed in an extension study, mean unmedicated IOP remained approximately **16.3 mmHg** at three years and 74% were medication-free. However, because GEMINI had no cataract-surgery-only group and only a subset entered longer follow-up, the amount attributable specifically to OMNI is uncertain. ([PubMed][16])

The retrospective ROMEO studies also found sustained IOP and medication reductions. Reported events included mild inflammation, transient pressure spikes, hyphema and later need for additional laser or surgery. Approximately **8%** underwent an additional glaucoma intervention after the first postoperative year in the two-year analysis. ([PubMed][17])

## Likely benefits

For a patient resembling those enrolled in the major trials—generally **mild-to-moderate open-angle glaucoma, visually significant cataract and a target pressure in the mid-teens**—SC-MIGS may:

1. Reduce dependence on topical glaucoma medication.
2. Produce a modest additional IOP reduction beyond cataract surgery alone.
3. Delay or reduce the likelihood of later incisional glaucoma surgery.
4. Avoid a permanent subconjunctival bleb and generally have a faster recovery than trabeculectomy or tube-shunt surgery.

The most persuasive evidence is for **medication reduction**. The incremental average IOP benefit over cataract surgery alone is real but usually modest for trabecular stents—approximately **1.5–2.5 mmHg** in the large two-year randomized trials. ([PubMed][1])

## Important harms and limitations

### Common or usually transient harms

* **Hyphema or blood reflux:** particularly common after trabeculotomy or GATT; incidence varies greatly according to procedure and definition.
* **Early IOP elevation:** from retained viscoelastic, inflammation, blood or steroid response.
* **Inflammation, blurred vision and delayed visual recovery.**
* **Peripheral anterior synechiae** around an intracanal implant.
* Continued need for medication despite technically successful surgery. ([PubMed][14])

### Less common but more consequential outcomes

* Persistent hyphema requiring anterior-chamber washout.
* Failure to reach target pressure and need for laser, repeat MIGS, trabeculectomy or tube-shunt surgery.
* Loss of vision from an operative complication is uncommon in the available trials, but most trials are too small to reliably quantify very rare serious events. ([PubMed][2])

### Evidence gaps

Most studies enrolled patients with mild or moderate disease. Evidence is substantially weaker for:

* Advanced glaucoma with fixation-threatening visual-field loss.
* Eyes needing a consistently very low IOP, such as 10–12 mmHg.
* Normal-tension glaucoma or already-low baseline IOP.
* Eyes with extensive angle damage or scarring.
* Long-term prevention of severe visual disability.

Because these procedures remain dependent on the eye’s conventional drainage system and episcleral venous pressure, their pressure-lowering “floor” is generally less predictable than that of filtering surgery. A person with advanced glaucoma or a very low target pressure may therefore still need trabeculectomy or a drainage implant.

## Practical interpretation

For someone undergoing cataract surgery who mainly wants fewer drops and has mild-to-moderate glaucoma, **Hydrus or iStent inject has the clearest randomized comparative evidence**.

For a patient needing a larger reduction from a high starting pressure, **GATT or a broader trabeculotomy** may offer more IOP lowering, but at the cost of more postoperative bleeding and less robust comparative evidence.

For **OMNI or canaloplasty**, the observed results are favorable, but the absence of a cataract-only randomized comparator makes it harder to separate the procedure’s effect from that of cataract extraction itself.

The most useful questions for a glaucoma surgeon are your individualized target IOP, whether medication reduction or maximum pressure reduction is the main goal, whether cataract surgery is already indicated, and what the backup plan would be if SC-MIGS does not reach the target.
