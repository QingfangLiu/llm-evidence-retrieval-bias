Considering eye surgery can definitely be nerve-wracking, but looking directly at the primary clinical data is the best way to make an informed choice. Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) has fundamentally changed how we manage open-angle glaucoma by offering a safer middle ground between daily eye drops and more invasive traditional surgeries.

Before diving into the clinical outcomes, it helps to visualize the structures these surgeries target:

In open-angle glaucoma, the eye's natural drainage pathway—specifically the trabecular meshwork—creates resistance, causing fluid to back up and raise your intraocular pressure (IOP). SC-MIGS procedures (which include implanted devices like the Hydrus microstent, or mechanical procedures like the OMNI system and GATT) work by bypassing, dilating, or clearing away that resistant meshwork to restore direct fluid flow into Schlemm's canal.

Based on individual primary clinical trials and cohort studies, here is a breakdown of the documented benefits and risks.

## Benefits of SC-MIGS

* **Significant Pressure Reduction:** Primary studies consistently demonstrate that targeting Schlemm's canal effectively lowers IOP. In the pivotal HORIZON randomized controlled trial, 78.5% of patients who received a Hydrus microstent during cataract surgery achieved an IOP reduction of 20% or more after two years, compared to just 54.5% of patients who only had cataract surgery (Jones et al., 2019). Similar standalone procedures without cataract surgery, such as 360° viscodilation using the OMNI system, have also shown strong results, dropping mean IOP from 23.0 mmHg down to 15.6 mmHg at 12 months (Toneatto et al., 2022).
* **Reduced Need for Daily Eye Drops:** One of the most significant quality-of-life benefits is freedom from glaucoma medications. In the HORIZON trial, 78.5% of the microstent group were completely medication-free at 24 months, almost double the rate (39.2%) of the control group (Jones et al., 2019). Longer-term observational data also supports this; a 3-year study of the Hydrus stent showed a sustained 33% reduction in the average number of daily glaucoma drops required by patients (Salimi et al., 2023).
* **Effective Across Disease Severities:** While MIGS is often associated with mild-to-moderate glaucoma, SC-MIGS procedures that physically unroof the canal (like gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy, or GATT) have proven highly effective even in severe or juvenile-onset cases. One primary study found that GATT successfully reduced IOP by over 12 points (from an average of 26.5 to 14.1 mmHg) at 18 months in juvenile-onset patients, helping to prevent further disease progression (Wang et al., 2020).

## Potential Harms and Risks

Because SC-MIGS procedures are much less invasive than traditional penetrating surgeries (like trabeculectomy), severe vision-threatening complications are rare. However, the procedures are not entirely risk-free:

* **Hyphema (Bleeding in the Eye):** Because these surgeries interact directly with the eye's vascular drainage system, small amounts of blood can temporarily backflow into the front of the eye. For instance, in a clinical evaluation of the OMNI system, a small subset of patients experienced transient hyphema, though it typically resolves on its own without vision loss (Toneatto et al., 2022).
* **Transient Pressure Spikes:** Ironically, some patients experience a short-lived spike in eye pressure about a month after surgery before the IOP ultimately stabilizes at a lower, healthier level (Toneatto et al., 2022).
* **Need for Future Interventions:** SC-MIGS does not guarantee a permanent cure, and it may not lower pressure enough for every patient. In one study evaluating the OMNI system, 15% of eyes eventually required a subsequent, secondary surgical procedure to get their glaucoma under control (Toneatto et al., 2022). Long-term follow-up studies note that a small percentage of patients will eventually require a more invasive traditional surgery if the SC-MIGS intervention fails over time (Salimi et al., 2023).
