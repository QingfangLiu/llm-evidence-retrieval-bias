## Evidence summary

I focused on **direct comparative primary studies in women undergoing IVF/ICSI ovarian stimulation**, prioritizing randomized trials. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, opinions, and studies limited to ovulation induction or IUI.

### Overall interpretation

**Recombinant FSH versus urinary FSH:** Several older trials found that rFSH produced slightly more oocytes, sometimes with a shorter stimulation period or lower total dose. These differences generally did **not translate into a clearly higher clinical pregnancy or live-birth rate**. ([PubMed][1])

**Recombinant FSH versus hMG/HP-hMG:** rFSH generally produces more oocytes, while hMG produces a different steroidogenic response because it supplies LH/hCG activity as well as FSH activity. Across large trials, HP-hMG has generally been **noninferior to rFSH for ongoing pregnancy or cumulative live birth**. ([PubMed][2])

**High responders:** In the MEGASET-HR trial, HP-hMG and rFSH produced nearly identical cumulative live-birth rates, but HP-hMG was associated with substantially less OHSS. That finding is especially relevant to women with very high AMH or a predicted excessive response. ([PubMed][3])

**Adding recombinant LH to rFSH:** Routine rLH supplementation has not consistently improved oocyte yield or live birth. One relatively small trial in women aged 35–39 suggested improved implantation/live birth, but larger trials in women aged ≥35 or women with falling LH concentrations did not confirm a benefit. ([PubMed][4])

### 3. rFSH alone versus rFSH plus recombinant LH

| Primary study             | Population and design                                                                                           | Main findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Marrs et al., 2004**    | Randomized multicenter trial; 431 IVF patients                                                                  | Adding rLH did not increase metaphase-II oocytes: 10.3 versus 10.4. No meaningful overall improvement in ovarian response was demonstrated. ([PubMed][15])                                                                       |
| **Matorras et al., 2009** | Randomized trial; 131 women aged 35–39 undergoing ICSI                                                          | Oocyte and embryo numbers and quality were similar. The rLH group had higher implantation and live-birth outcomes, but the study was relatively small and the result was not subsequently reproduced consistently. ([PubMed][4]) |
| **Vuong et al., 2015**    | Randomized trial; 240 women aged ≥35; GnRH-antagonist protocol                                                  | Live birth was 16.7% with rFSH plus rLH and 17.5% with rFSH alone. Oocyte yield, implantation, miscarriage and clinical pregnancy were also similar. ([PubMed][16])                                                              |
| **Lahoud et al., 2017**   | Prospective randomized trial; 100 women randomized after experiencing a ≥50% reduction in LH during stimulation | rLH supplementation did not significantly improve live birth per transfer—27.8% versus 37.0%—or other major outcomes. ([PubMed][17])                                                                                             |

**Interpretation:** These studies do not support routine rLH addition for all IVF patients. The evidence for selected subgroups—older patients, profound suppression, hypo-response or very low endogenous LH—remains inconsistent because subgroup definitions and stimulation protocols vary.

## Practical implications

For a typical expected responder, the primary trials suggest that selecting between rFSH and HP-hMG should not be based on an expectation of a large live-birth difference. More relevant distinctions may be:

* desired versus excessive oocyte response;
* AMH/AFC and previous stimulation response;
* OHSS risk;
* whether LH activity is clinically desired;
* protocol, trigger and fresh-versus-freeze-all strategy;
* medication availability, administration burden and cost.

A major limitation is that many urinary-FSH and early hMG studies used long agonist suppression and cleavage-stage or multiple-embryo transfer. The later MEGASET trials, using antagonist stimulation and single-blastocyst transfer, are more similar to contemporary practice. ([PubMed][14])
