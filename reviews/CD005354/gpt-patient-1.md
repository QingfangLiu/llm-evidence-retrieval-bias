## Scope

I limited this evidence map to **individual primary clinical studies in women undergoing IVF/ICSI ovarian stimulation**. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, committee opinions, editorials, and comments.

The main comparisons are:

* **rFSH**: recombinant follitropin alfa or beta.
* **Urinary FSH/HP-uFSH**: FSH purified from human urine, with little LH activity.
* **HP-hMG/menotropins**: urinary-derived FSH plus LH biological activity.
* **rLH supplementation**: recombinant LH added to an rFSH regimen.

## Overall interpretation

**No gonadotropin preparation has shown a consistent, large live-birth advantage for the average IVF patient across the individual trials.**

Several older trials found that rFSH produced **slightly more follicles or oocytes, with a lower total dose or shorter stimulation**, compared with older urinary FSH formulations. However, these differences generally did **not consistently translate into significantly higher clinical-pregnancy or live-birth rates**. ([PubMed][1])

Compared with HP-hMG, rFSH usually produced **more oocytes**, whereas HP-hMG sometimes produced a higher proportion of embryos classified as high quality. The major HP-hMG trials found **similar ongoing-pregnancy or cumulative live-birth rates**, including in antagonist protocols using single-blastocyst transfer. ([OUP Academic][2])

### rFSH versus urinary FSH

In the large 1995 Out trial, rFSH produced more oocytes than urinary FSH—10.84 versus 8.95—and required fewer total IU and slightly fewer stimulation days. Ongoing pregnancy per fresh attempt was 22.2% versus 18.2%, a nonsignificant difference; when frozen transfers were included, ongoing pregnancy was 25.7% versus 20.4%, with a borderline *P*=0.05. ([PubMed][1])

The 496-patient Schats trial similarly found more oocytes with rFSH—13.1 versus 11.4—and a shorter course with fewer ampules. Pregnancy was 25.1% versus 20.1%, but the difference was not statistically significant. ([PubMed][3])

Other randomized studies were less suggestive of an ovarian-response difference. Lenton and colleagues found similar oocyte numbers, with nonsignificant differences in pregnancy and live birth. Dickey and colleagues found no significant differences in oocytes, continuing pregnancy, or live birth. ([PubMed][4])

A later US trial by Baker and colleagues provides one of the clearest live-birth comparisons: mean oocyte counts were 17.1 with rFSH and 16.3 with HP-uFSH, and the **live-birth rate was identical at 38.2% in both groups**. ([ScienceDirect][5])

A smaller PCOS-specific randomized trial also found similar oocyte numbers, embryo measures, OHSS rates, and clinical-pregnancy rates—41.2% with rFSH versus 45.0% with HP-uFSH. ([PubMed][6])

**Interpretation:** rFSH may be somewhat more potent than some older urinary FSH preparations, but the more clinically important outcomes—especially live birth—were generally similar.

### rFSH versus HP-hMG/menotropins

A 727-treated-patient multinational trial reported ongoing pregnancy rates of 25% with HP-hMG and 22% with rFSH. OHSS, miscarriage, adverse events, and injection-site reactions were similar. ([PubMed][7])

In the 731-patient MERIT trial, rFSH produced more oocytes—11.8 versus 10.0—but HP-hMG produced a higher proportion of locally classified top-quality embryos. Ongoing pregnancy was 27% with HP-hMG and 22% with rFSH. HP-hMG met the trial’s noninferiority standard, but superiority was not established because the confidence interval included no difference. ([OUP Academic][2])

The 749-patient MEGASET trial is more applicable to modern practice because it used a GnRH-antagonist protocol and compulsory single-blastocyst transfer. Fresh-cycle ongoing pregnancy was 29% with HP-hMG and 27% with rFSH in the intention-to-treat population. Including frozen transfers within one year, cumulative live birth was 40% versus 38%, respectively. ([PubMed][8])

The 620-patient MEGASET-HR trial specifically studied predicted high responders with AMH ≥5 ng/mL. Cumulative live birth was essentially identical—50.6% with HP-hMG and 51.5% with rFSH—but OHSS occurred less frequently with HP-hMG, 9.7% versus 21.4%. Early pregnancy loss was also lower with HP-hMG in this trial. ([PubMed][9])

**Interpretation:** HP-hMG generally retrieves fewer oocytes than rFSH but can achieve similar cumulative live-birth rates. The high-responder trial suggests HP-hMG may reduce OHSS in women with very high predicted ovarian response, although that finding should not automatically be applied to all patients.

### Does adding recombinant LH help?

The findings are mixed, but the larger and more recent randomized trials generally do not show a clear benefit from routinely adding rLH.

In 431 women undergoing ICSI, Marrs and colleagues found nearly identical numbers of mature oocytes with rFSH plus rLH and rFSH alone—10.3 versus 10.4. ([PubMed][10])

An 84-patient poor-responder trial found no improvement in ongoing pregnancy, implantation, ovarian response, or other outcomes from adding rLH. ([PubMed][11])

A small 131-patient trial in women aged 35–39 found no improvement in oocyte or embryo quantity or quality, although implantation and live birth were higher in the rLH group. Because this was a relatively small, single-center study, the finding requires caution. ([PubMed][12])

In contrast, a 2013 multicenter study of women aged 35 or older found no effect on pregnancy, implantation, or ovarian response, and a subsequent 240-patient trial found live-birth rates of 16.7% with rFSH/rLH versus 17.5% with rFSH alone. ([OUP Academic][13])

**Interpretation:** Routine LH supplementation is not consistently beneficial solely because a woman is 35 or older or has previously responded poorly. It may still be considered in selected situations, such as profound endogenous LH deficiency, but that is a different population from typical IVF patients.

## Important limitations

Many rFSH-versus-urinary-FSH trials were conducted in the 1990s or early 2000s. They commonly used long GnRH-agonist suppression, fresh cleavage-stage transfer, and transfer of up to three embryos. These protocols differ from contemporary antagonist cycles, blastocyst culture, single-embryo transfer, preimplantation genetic testing, GnRH-agonist triggers, and freeze-all strategies. ([PubMed][1])

Many studies were designed around oocyte yield or noninferiority rather than being adequately powered to detect a modest live-birth difference. Several pivotal trials also had manufacturer sponsorship or manufacturer-affiliated investigators, so objective endpoints such as cumulative live birth and OHSS deserve more weight than surrogate outcomes such as follicles or hormone concentrations. ([OUP Academic][2])

For an individual IVF cycle, age, AMH/antral follicle count, PCOS or high-response risk, prior stimulation response, planned trigger and transfer strategy, medication availability, and insurance coverage may matter more than a small average difference between products. Medication or dose changes should be made only with the reproductive endocrinology team.
