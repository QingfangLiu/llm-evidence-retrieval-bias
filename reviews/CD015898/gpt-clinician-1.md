## Scope

I interpreted **transfusion dose** as the amount of red cells administered during one transfusion episode after the decision to transfuse has already been made—for example, **1 versus 2 RBC units in adults** or **10–20 mL/kg in infants**. I excluded studies comparing hemoglobin thresholds, transfusion timing, RBC storage duration, product processing, or massive-hemorrhage protocols.

## Adults

### Randomized evidence

**Postpartum anemia:** In 66 hemodynamically stable postpartum patients, an initial one-unit strategy used fewer units than an initial two-unit strategy—mean 1.2 versus 2.1 units. Six of 33 patients initially assigned one unit required another unit. Post-transfusion hemoglobin was lower after one unit, 7.8 versus 8.7 g/dL, but there were no detected differences in symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, breastfeeding, fatigue, depression, or maternal attachment. ([PubMed][1])

**Acute leukemia or hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation:** A multicenter RCT randomized 245 patients to one or two units per episode at the same hemoglobin trigger. The composite of grade ≥3 nonhematologic complications, ICU admission, or death occurred in 22.4% versus 23.3%, meeting the prespecified noninferiority criterion. Median total exposure was seven versus eight units, so the one-unit policy did **not** clearly reduce total RBC use during the admission. Discharge hemoglobin and quality of life were comparable. ([ScienceDirect][2])

A smaller 2×2 factorial AML trial randomized 91 patients to hemoglobin triggers of 7 or 8 g/dL and to one or two units per episode. The number of units per episode did not significantly change total RBC use, bleeding events, or AML treatment outcomes. The trial was a feasibility study and was not powered to establish small mortality or toxicity differences. ([PubMed][3])

### Comparative cohort and implementation evidence

Before-after studies in hematology and transplantation generally found reductions in RBC utilization after changing from two units to one:

* **Berger et al.:** 25% fewer RBC units per treatment cycle and 24% fewer per aplasia-day, with no detected increase in severe bleeding, platelet use, outpatient transfusions, or mortality. ([PMC][4])
* **Bowman et al.:** fewer units per admission, 4.5 versus 5.1, but more transfusion episodes, 4.1 versus 2.7; no detected difference in length of stay, 30-day mortality, readmission, or postdischarge transfusion. ([PMC][5])
* **Marco-Ayala et al.:** 32% lower utilization after autologous transplantation, with similar numbers of transfusion episodes, discharge hemoglobin, length of stay, and 30-day mortality. ([ScienceDirect][6])
* **Chantepie et al.:** no statistically significant overall reduction, although the allogeneic-HSCT subgroup received fewer units; survival was similar and no anemia-related adverse effects were reported. ([PubMed][7])
* **Avdić et al.:** one-unit dosing was noninferior for the transfusion-episode rate in autologous HSCT; the allogeneic result narrowly missed the chosen noninferiority margin. Blood volume per hospital day was lower. ([PubMed][8])
* **Heyes et al.:** an education and one-unit-policy intervention on eight general medical wards was associated with a 50% reduction in RBC use. However, the study did not report patient-centered clinical outcomes, so it mainly establishes feasibility and utilization effects. ([PubMed][9])

### Adult benefit–harm balance

The clearest benefit of the smaller dose is **reduced immediate RBC exposure**, often accompanied by lower total utilization. Potential disadvantages are a lower post-transfusion hemoglobin, additional reassessments, and sometimes more separate transfusion episodes. Across the randomized trials, there was no detected clinical advantage to routinely administering two units, but important populations—active bleeding, hemodynamic instability, acute myocardial ischemia, major surgery, and most general medical diagnoses—were not adequately studied. ([PubMed][1])

## Children with severe anemia

A Ugandan phase II RCT compared **30 versus 20 mL/kg whole blood** in 160 children with severe anemia. Severe anemia was corrected within 24 hours in 90% versus 74%. There was one death and one nonfatal allergic reaction in the larger-volume arm versus six deaths in the standard-volume arm, but the study was too small to establish mortality effects. No death was attributed to volume overload. ([PubMed][10])

The subsequent multicenter TRACT trial included approximately 3,200 children in Uganda and Malawi. Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% with 30 mL/kg and 4.5% with 20 mL/kg; the difference was not statistically significant. Additional transfusion was less frequent with the larger dose, 12.3% versus 18.8%. However, there was a prespecified interaction with fever:

* Among children without fever, the larger volume was associated with lower mortality.
* Among febrile children, the larger volume was associated with higher mortality.

The interaction is clinically important but should not be interpreted as two separate definitive trials. The findings arose in African hospitals where malaria, hemolysis, sepsis, and severe nutritional or infectious anemia are common, and transfusions included whole blood and red-cell concentrates; generalizability to other pediatric inpatient settings is therefore uncertain. ([New England Journal of Medicine][11])

## Preterm and very-low-birth-weight infants

Small randomized studies consistently show that **20 mL/kg raises hemoglobin and hematocrit more than 10–15 mL/kg**:

* In Paul et al., 20 versus 10 mL/kg produced a higher post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit without a detected pulmonary-function difference. ([PubMed][12])
* Wong et al. randomized 20 infants to 20 versus 15 mL/kg. Mean subsequent transfusions were 2.8 versus 3.4, a nonsignificant difference; no adverse event was attributed to the larger volume. ([PubMed][13])
* In a randomized crossover study of 22 very-low-birth-weight infants, 20 mL/kg produced higher post-transfusion hemoglobin, 13.2 versus 11.8 g/dL, without detected differences in hypoxia, tachypnea, edema, hepatomegaly, NEC, or vital-sign instability. ([PubMed][14])
* In 39 stable preterm infants, cardiac output decreased and BNP transiently increased after both 15- and 20-mL/kg transfusions, but the changes did not differ significantly by dose. ([PubMed][15])
* A larger 2023 randomized study of 151 preterm infants found fewer total transfusions, 1.9 versus 2.6, and a modestly shorter hospitalization with 20 versus 15 mL/kg. Cerebral and intestinal oxygenation changes and reported complications did not differ. Because the study used multiple secondary outcomes and was described as “prospective, randomized, partially blinded observational,” independent confirmation would be valuable. ([PubMed][16])

Comparative Dutch neonatal cohorts found similar numbers of transfusions with 15- and 20-mL/kg practices but greater cumulative transfused volume with 20 mL/kg. Follow-up did not demonstrate a clear association between dose policy and death, neuromotor delay, blindness, or deafness, although the observational design leaves substantial residual confounding. ([PubMed][17])

Thus, the neonatal evidence establishes a larger hematologic increment and reasonable short-term tolerance in **stable** preterm infants. It does not reliably establish whether larger doses prevent major morbidity, reduce donor exposure, improve neurodevelopment, or increase uncommon volume-related complications.

## Overall interpretation

For **stable, nonbleeding adults**, smaller one-unit transfusions followed by clinical and laboratory reassessment are consistently blood-sparing, without a demonstrated clinical penalty in the populations studied. The evidence does not prove equivalence for uncommon serious harms or for unstable and actively bleeding patients.

For **children with profound severe anemia**, larger initial volumes reduce persistent anemia and repeat transfusion, but overall survival benefit is unproven and fever may identify a subgroup in whom rapid larger-volume correction is harmful.

For **stable preterm infants**, 20 mL/kg produces a larger hemoglobin increment and may reduce subsequent transfusions, but the patient-important benefit–harm balance remains uncertain because most studies are small and dominated by surrogate or short-term outcomes.
