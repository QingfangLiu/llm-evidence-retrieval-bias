## Scope used

I treated “amount” as the **RBC volume administered during a single transfusion event after a decision to transfuse had already been made**:

* Adults: usually **one versus two RBC units**
* Children/neonates: different **mL/kg doses**
* Pediatric whole-blood studies were included when they compared different **whole-blood-equivalent red-cell doses**

I excluded studies concerned only with transfusion thresholds, transfusion timing, infusion rate, storage age, massive-transfusion ratios, autologous blood, or different blood-product types. The search was updated through **July 26, 2026**.

## Overall findings

### Adults

The most reliable adult evidence consists of two randomized trials.

In 245 adults receiving intensive chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, one versus two units were given at the same hemoglobin trigger. Serious complications occurred in 22.4% versus 23.3%, satisfying the prespecified noninferiority criterion. Median total RBC use during admission was 7 versus 8 units, so the one-unit strategy did **not** significantly reduce cumulative use in this repeatedly transfused population. Discharge hemoglobin was similar. ([ScienceDirect][1])

In 66 hemodynamically stable women with postpartum anemia, an initial one-unit strategy reduced mean use from 2.1 to 1.2 units; only 18.2% subsequently required another unit. Post-transfusion hemoglobin was lower after one unit, 7.8 versus 8.7 g/dL, but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, breastfeeding, fatigue, depression and maternal-attachment measures did not differ. ([PubMed][2])

Comparative hematology and oncology cohort studies generally found that switching to single-unit transfusions:

* Reduced cumulative RBC utilization by approximately 25%–32% in several settings
* Produced lower post-transfusion or discharge hemoglobin
* Sometimes increased the number of separate transfusion episodes
* Did not demonstrate clear differences in mortality, bleeding, length of stay or early readmission

These studies are less reliable for causal inference because most used historical controls, and some simultaneously changed transfusion thresholds, ordering systems or other blood-management practices. ([PMC][3])

**Adult interpretation:** In stable, nonbleeding adults, starting with one unit reduces immediate exposure and frequently reduces cumulative RBC use. Available trials have not identified worse short-term clinical outcomes, but confidence intervals remain compatible with clinically important differences in uncommon adverse events.

### Children beyond the neonatal period

Two randomized studies evaluated 30 versus 20 mL/kg of whole blood—approximately equivalent to 15 versus 10 mL/kg packed RBCs—in African children hospitalized with severe anemia.

The phase II trial included 160 Ugandan children. Anemia correction to hemoglobin above 6 g/dL by 24 hours occurred in 90% with 30 mL/kg and 74% with 20 mL/kg. There were six deaths in the lower-volume group and one in the higher-volume group, but the trial was not adequately powered for mortality. ([PubMed][4])

The subsequent TRACT trial randomized 3,196 children. Higher volume reduced additional transfusions, 12.3% versus 18.8%. Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% versus 4.5%, hazard ratio 0.76 (95% CI 0.54–1.08), and therefore was not significantly different. A prespecified interaction with fever was notable:

* **No fever:** mortality favored 30 mL/kg, HR 0.43 (95% CI 0.27–0.69)
* **Fever present:** mortality favored 20 mL/kg, HR 1.91 (95% CI 1.04–3.49)

Readmissions, serious adverse events and hemoglobin recovery at 180 days did not differ overall. The divergent fever subgroups should not be collapsed into a single claim that higher volume is uniformly beneficial or harmful. ([PubMed][5])

**Pediatric interpretation:** A larger red-cell dose corrects severe anemia faster and decreases repeat transfusion, but the TRACT findings suggest that clinical context—particularly fever—may materially alter mortality effects.

### Preterm neonates

Small randomized studies consistently found a greater hemoglobin or hematocrit increment with 20 mL/kg than with 10 or 15 mL/kg.

* In 13 very-low-birth-weight infants, 20 versus 10 mL/kg produced higher post-transfusion hemoglobin, 14.2 versus 12.0 g/dL, and hematocrit, 41.2% versus 32.3%, without a detected difference in pulmonary function. ([PubMed][6])
* In 20 infants randomized to 20 versus 15 mL/kg, mean transfusion numbers were 2.8 versus 3.4; the difference was not significant, and no adverse event was attributed to the larger dose. ([PubMed][7])
* A randomized crossover trial of 22 infants found higher hemoglobin and hematocrit after 20 mL/kg, without significant differences in tachypnea, hypoxia, edema, hepatomegaly, necrotizing enterocolitis or vital-sign instability. ([PubMed][8])
* In 39 stable preterm infants, both 15 and 20 mL/kg were followed by reduced cardiac output and a transient BNP rise, but the changes did not differ significantly between doses. ([PubMed][9])
* In 151 infants, 20 versus 15 mL/kg did not produce different cerebral or intestinal oxygen-saturation responses, but was associated with fewer transfusions, 1.9 versus 2.6, a larger hemoglobin increment and a roughly three-day shorter hospitalization, without more recorded neonatal complications. ([PubMed][10])

A two-center cohort comparing 15 with 20 mL/kg found that 20 mL/kg prolonged the interval to the next transfusion and reduced transfusion episodes among infants born at 28–31 weeks, while composite bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy, intraventricular hemorrhage and mortality outcomes were similar. Interpretation is limited because the centers appeared to use somewhat different clinical triggers. ([PubMed][11]) Long-term follow-up of these cohorts found no independent association between total transfused volume and death or neurosensory/developmental impairment at approximately two years. ([Springer][12])

Formula-calculated dosing can deliver substantially more blood than fixed weight-based dosing and produces a larger hematocrit increment, but has not consistently reduced repeat transfusion. In a 68-infant trial, neonatal morbidity and death were similar between formula-based and fixed 15 mL/kg dosing. ([PMC][13])

**Neonatal interpretation:** Larger doses produce a more durable hematologic response and may reduce repeat transfusion. No clear short-term toxicity signal has emerged, but most trials were far too small to evaluate mortality, necrotizing enterocolitis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, severe intraventricular hemorrhage, retinopathy, transfusion-associated circulatory overload or long-term neurodevelopment reliably.

## Benefits and harms by strategy

| Strategy         | Potential benefits demonstrated                                                                                                     | Potential disadvantages or unresolved harms                                                                                                                                      |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Smaller dose** | Lower immediate blood exposure; often fewer cumulative units in adults; avoids an unnecessary second adult unit; lower resource use | Lower post-transfusion hemoglobin; more reassessment or separate transfusion episodes; possible need for earlier repeat transfusion                                              |
| **Larger dose**  | Greater hemoglobin/hematocrit increment; faster anemia correction; fewer repeat transfusions in several pediatric/neonatal studies  | More blood exposure at each event; rare volume-related and transfusion reactions inadequately studied; possible increased mortality among febrile children in the TRACT subgroup |
| **Either dose**  | Most studies found no obvious difference in measured short-term complications                                                       | Absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as equivalence because most studies were underpowered for patient-important harms                                  |

## Important evidence gaps

The evidence applies mainly to selected populations: hematology/HSCT patients, stable postpartum patients, African children with severe anemia and stable preterm neonates. There is little direct randomized evidence in general medical wards, adult ICUs, perioperative patients, patients with cardiovascular disease, actively bleeding patients, older frail adults or term infants.

Most studies measured blood utilization and laboratory responses rather than functional recovery, organ dysfunction, donor exposure, quality of life or long-term outcomes. Rare transfusion complications cannot be estimated reliably from the available sample sizes.
