Historically, the standard dose for a red blood cell (RBC) transfusion in hospitalized, non-bleeding adults was two units. However, modern patient blood management has largely shifted toward a single-unit strategy (giving a smaller amount of blood per episode, followed by clinical reassessment).

Based on primary observational and cohort studies in heavily transfused populations (such as those with hematologic malignancies or undergoing stem cell transplants), the clinical trade-offs between larger (double-unit) and smaller (single-unit) transfusion doses are clear:

## Benefits of Smaller (Single-Unit) Transfusions

* **Reduced Total Blood Consumption:** Patients managed with a single-unit policy receive significantly fewer total RBC units over their hospital stay compared to those receiving two units at a time. Transitioning to a single-unit policy has been shown to reduce total RBC utilization by 25% to 29% (Berger et al., 2011; Bowman et al., 2019).
* **Decreased Allogeneic Exposure:** By reducing the total volume of blood transfused, a single-unit strategy limits the patient's overall exposure to allogeneic blood products, which inherently lowers the risk of transfusion-related adverse events and alloimmunization (Berger et al., 2011).
* **Equivalent Safety and Survival:** Administering a smaller dose per transfusion episode does not compromise patient safety. Studies indicate no significant differences in 30-day mortality, length of hospital stay, severe bleeding events, or the need for subsequent platelet transfusions when comparing single-unit to double-unit dosing (Avdić et al., 2016; Berger et al., 2011; Bowman et al., 2019).

## Harms and Drawbacks of Smaller Transfusions

* **Increased Transfusion Episodes:** Because patients receive less blood per dose, they may require more individual transfusion episodes to maintain target hemoglobin levels throughout their admission. This can result in increased clinical labor (more frequent lab draws for reassessment and more individual setups by nursing staff) and repeated interruptions for the patient (Bowman et al., 2019).
* **Lower Hemoglobin Levels:** Patients managed with single-unit strategies tend to have slightly lower mean pre-transfusion and discharge hemoglobin levels compared to those receiving two units. However, this lower baseline has not been linked to any adverse clinical consequences or worsened symptom burdens (Berger et al., 2011; Bowman et al., 2019).
