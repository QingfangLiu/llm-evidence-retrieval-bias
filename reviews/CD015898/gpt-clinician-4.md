## Scope

I interpreted **“larger or smaller amount” as the red-cell dose given during a single transfusion event after the decision to transfuse has already been made**. I therefore excluded studies that compared only hemoglobin transfusion thresholds, as well as reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, opinions and editorials.

## Clinical summary

### Adults: usually 1 unit versus 2 units

The direct evidence principally concerns **hemodynamically stable, nonbleeding adults** with hematologic malignancies, stem-cell transplantation or postpartum anemia.

**Potential benefits of starting with 1 unit**

* Less red-cell exposure when the second unit proves unnecessary. In the postpartum RCT, mean use was 1.2 versus 2.1 units, and only 18.2% of patients initially given 1 unit subsequently required another unit.
* Several before-and-after hematology studies reported approximately 25%–32% reductions in institutional or per-patient RBC use.
* No convincing deterioration in symptoms, length of stay, mortality, bleeding or other measured clinical outcomes was demonstrated in the randomized studies. ([PubMed][1])

**Potential disadvantages of starting with 1 unit**

* Lower immediate or discharge hemoglobin. In the postpartum trial, post-transfusion hemoglobin was 7.8 versus 8.7 g/dL; in one hematologic-malignancy cohort, discharge hemoglobin was 8.9 versus 9.5 g/dL.
* More separate transfusion episodes and reassessments may be required. In the Bowman cohort, episodes increased from 2.7 to 4.1 per admission despite fewer total units.
* One large hematology RCT found that 1 unit was noninferior to 2 units for serious complications, but it **did not materially reduce the total number of units per admission**: median 7 versus 8 units. The AML factorial pilot likewise found no statistically clear reduction in total use from changing the dose alone. ([PubMed][1])

**Potential benefits of routinely giving 2 units**

* Greater immediate hemoglobin increment.
* Fewer separate administration and reassessment cycles in some populations.

However, the adult studies did not demonstrate an important patient-centered benefit from routinely giving the second unit. Their ability to exclude uncommon harms—TACO, TRALI, myocardial ischemia, thrombosis or serious transfusion reactions—is limited because most were small, and six of the nine principal adult studies used historical rather than concurrent controls. ([PubMed][2])

**Adult interpretation:** In the populations actually studied, giving **1 unit and then reassessing** generally avoids unnecessary second units without a demonstrated clinical penalty. This conclusion cannot be confidently extended to active hemorrhage, acute coronary ischemia, unstable shock, major surgery or massive-transfusion situations.

### Children

Two related Ugandan/Malawian randomized trials compared:

* **Higher volume:** 30 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent, corresponding to approximately 15 mL/kg packed or settled RBCs.
* **Lower volume:** 20 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent, corresponding to approximately 10 mL/kg packed or settled RBCs.

The definitive TRACT trial enrolled 3,196 children aged 2 months to 12 years with severe anemia:

* Additional transfusion: **12.3% with higher volume versus 18.8% with lower volume**.
* Twenty-eight-day mortality: **3.4% versus 4.5%**, respectively; hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.08—no statistically clear overall difference.
* No clear overall differences in serious adverse events, readmission or hemoglobin recovery at 180 days.
* A notable interaction occurred with presenting fever: higher volume was associated with lower mortality among children without fever but higher mortality among those with fever. This subgroup result is clinically important but should not automatically be generalized beyond this setting. ([PubMed][3])

The preceding 160-child phase II trial also found faster and more frequent correction of severe anemia with 30 mL/kg: 90% versus 74% had corrected severe anemia by 24 hours. It was too small to determine mortality or uncommon adverse effects reliably. ([PMC][4])

**Pediatric interpretation:** A larger dose can improve hematologic correction and reduce repeat transfusions in children with severe anemia in sub-Saharan Africa. The fever interaction prevents treating the higher dose as universally preferable, and there are essentially no comparable outcome trials in general pediatric wards or PICUs in high-resource settings.

### Preterm neonates

The neonatal evidence consists mostly of small trials in **stable preterm or very-low-birth-weight infants**.

Across comparisons of 20 versus 10 or 15 mL/kg, a larger dose consistently produced a larger hemoglobin or hematocrit increment. The early 10-versus-20 mL/kg trial found no difference in measured pulmonary function; trials of 15 versus 20 mL/kg found no clear increase in short-term clinical signs of volume overload or selected neonatal complications. ([PubMed][5])

Physiologic studies found reductions in cardiac output and transient BNP elevations following transfusion, but no statistically clear difference between 15 and 20 mL/kg. A later 151-infant trial reported fewer transfusions with 20 mL/kg—1.9 versus 2.6 per infant—and approximately three fewer hospital days, without a detected increase in complications. ([PubMed][6])

A formula-based trial delivered a larger individualized volume than a fixed 15 mL/kg strategy and achieved a greater hematocrit rise; retinopathy, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, intraventricular hemorrhage, NEC and death were similar, but the sample of 68 infants was inadequate for reliable safety conclusions. ([PubMed][7])

**Neonatal interpretation:** Larger aliquots reliably improve post-transfusion hemoglobin and may reduce repeat transfusions, but evidence for mortality, neurodevelopment, NEC, BPD, ROP, IVH or TACO remains inadequate. The findings primarily apply to stable preterm infants, not unstable neonates.

## Overall conclusions

* **Adults:** Smaller initial dosing—usually 1 unit—is blood-conserving and has not shown worse clinical outcomes in stable, nonbleeding patients. More reassessment and separate transfusion episodes may result.
* **Children:** Higher weight-based dosing improves anemia correction and decreases repeat transfusion, but the overall mortality benefit is uncertain and presenting fever may modify the effect.
* **Preterm neonates:** Larger doses improve the hemoglobin/hematocrit response and may reduce repeat transfusions, but clinically important safety and long-term outcome evidence is insufficient.
* There is little or no robust randomized dose evidence for adult general medicine, general ICU, perioperative patients, acute coronary syndromes, active bleeding, trauma or massive transfusion.
