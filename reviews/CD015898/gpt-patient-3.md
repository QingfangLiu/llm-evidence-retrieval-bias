## Scope

I interpreted “larger or smaller amount” as the **dose given during one transfusion event after the decision to transfuse has already been made**:

* Adults: usually **two red-cell units versus one unit**
* Children and newborns: usually a weight-based comparison, such as **15 versus 10 mL/kg** or **20 versus 15 mL/kg**

I excluded studies that only compared different hemoglobin thresholds, storage duration, transfusion timing, or blood-product ratios. One study that changed both the threshold and dose is identified separately because it cannot isolate the effect of dose.

## Bottom line

### Adults

For **stable adults who are not actively bleeding**, starting with one unit rather than routinely giving two units:

* Usually reduces the amount of blood administered.
* Leaves a somewhat lower post-transfusion hemoglobin.
* May result in more separate transfusion episodes or reassessments.
* Has not been shown to increase death, length of stay, serious complications, bleeding, thrombosis, fatigue or other measured patient outcomes.

However, most participants had leukemia, lymphoma or stem-cell transplantation. One small trial involved stable postpartum patients. The studies were not large enough to reliably determine whether one strategy changes uncommon harms such as circulatory overload, severe allergic reactions or transfusion-related lung injury.

In a 245-person hematology RCT, serious complications occurred in 22.4% with one-unit transfusions and 23.3% with two-unit transfusions. The one-unit strategy met the study’s noninferiority criterion, but it did not significantly reduce the total number of units per patient: median seven versus eight. It required substantially more separate transfusion events. ([ScienceDirect][1])

In a 66-person postpartum RCT, patients assigned one initial unit received an average of 1.2 units, compared with 2.1 units in the two-unit group. Only 18.2% subsequently needed more blood. The one-unit group had a lower post-transfusion hemoglobin, 7.8 versus 8.7 g/dL, but similar symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, fatigue, breastfeeding, depression and maternal attachment. ([PubMed][2])

A 91-person AML pilot trial found that assigning one versus two units per episode did not significantly change total red-cell use, treatment outcomes or bleeding. Because it was a small factorial trial, it could not rule out clinically important differences in uncommon complications. ([PubMed][3])

Several adult policy-change cohorts found reductions of approximately 25% to 32% in red-cell utilization after changing from predominantly two-unit to one-unit transfusions. They generally found no corresponding increase in mortality, length of stay, severe bleeding or readmission, but these were nonrandomized comparisons and may have been affected by changes in care over time. ([PubMed][4])

### Children

The best child evidence comes from African hospitals treating severe anemia, commonly associated with malaria or infection. It therefore may not apply to children with routine postoperative anemia, cancer treatment, trauma or chronic transfusion needs.

In the large TRACT trial, 3,196 children with severe anemia were assigned 30 versus 20 mL/kg of whole-blood equivalent—approximately 15 versus 10 mL/kg of packed cells:

* Additional transfusions: 12.3% with the larger dose versus 18.8% with the smaller dose.
* Twenty-eight-day mortality: 3.4% versus 4.5%; the overall difference was not statistically significant.
* Serious adverse events: 27.0% versus 26.0%.
* Allergic reactions: 1.6% versus 1.3%.
* Suspected pulmonary or cardiovascular serious events were uncommon and similar.

A prespecified finding requires particular caution: among children without fever, the larger dose was associated with lower mortality, while among children with fever above 37.5°C, it was associated with higher mortality. This was a subgroup result from a distinctive severe-anemia population and should not be applied automatically to other children. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

An earlier 160-child Ugandan trial found that 30 mL/kg corrected severe anemia within 24 hours more often than 20 mL/kg—90% versus 74%. There was one death in the larger-volume group and six in the smaller-volume group, but the study was too small for a reliable mortality comparison. No deaths were attributed to volume overload. ([PubMed][6])

### Premature newborns

Larger doses consistently produce a greater immediate rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit. Some studies suggest fewer subsequent transfusions, but the studies are generally too small or too confounded to establish effects on death, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, necrotizing enterocolitis, brain injury, retinopathy or long-term development.

A randomized trial of only 13 very-low-birth-weight infants found that 20 mL/kg produced higher post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit than 10 mL/kg, without a measured difference in pulmonary function. The sample was far too small to assess uncommon or long-term harm. ([PubMed][7])

Other small trials comparing 20 with 15 mL/kg found higher hemoglobin after the larger dose and no clear increase in short-term respiratory, cardiovascular or gastrointestinal complications. One 151-infant trial reported fewer transfusions and a slightly shorter hospitalization with 20 mL/kg, but these findings need replication. ([PubMed][8])

A two-center cohort comparing 15 with 20 mL/kg found similar overall neonatal complications. Among infants born at 28 to under 32 weeks, 20 mL/kg was associated with approximately one fewer transfusion, although differences between the hospitals—including possible differences in when clinicians transfused—limit causal interpretation. ([PubMed][9])

## Practical interpretation for a patient

For a **stable, non-bleeding adult**, the direct evidence suggests that giving one unit and then reassessing symptoms and hemoglobin often avoids an unnecessary second unit without worsening measured clinical outcomes. The trade-off is a lower immediate hemoglobin and, in some populations, more separate transfusion visits or episodes.

This evidence should not be extrapolated to active hemorrhage, shock, massive transfusion, exchange transfusion or other rapidly changing situations. The appropriate amount may also be influenced by heart or kidney disease, previous circulatory overload, symptoms, starting hemoglobin, body size and how quickly the blood can be administered.
