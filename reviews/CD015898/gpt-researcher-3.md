## Scope used

I treated **transfusion dose** as the quantity of red cells administered during one transfusion episode—such as 1 versus 2 units in adults or 10 versus 20 mL/kg in infants—after the decision to transfuse had already been made.

I excluded studies that compared only:

* Higher versus lower hemoglobin **transfusion thresholds**
* Liberal versus restrictive strategies without a separable dose difference
* Massive transfusion or active hemorrhage protocols
* Autologous transfusion, cord-blood graft dose, or mixed blood components without a definable RBC-equivalent dose
* Reviews, guidelines, opinions, editorials, and protocols without outcome data

I found both **direct comparative studies** and less-direct before/after implementation studies. The direct randomized evidence is limited.

## What the primary studies show

### Adults

The two most informative randomized trials compared an initial dose of **1 versus 2 RBC units**.

In 66 hemodynamically stable postpartum patients, a one-unit initial strategy reduced mean exposure from 2.1 to 1.2 units. Only 18.2% of those initially receiving one unit required another unit. Post-transfusion hemoglobin was lower after one unit, but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, fatigue, breastfeeding, depression, and maternal-attachment outcomes did not differ detectably. ([PubMed][1])

In 245 adults receiving intensive chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, one-unit transfusion was noninferior to two-unit transfusion for a composite of severe complications, ICU admission, or death: 22.4% versus 23.3%. However, it did **not clearly reduce total RBC use**—median 7 versus 8 units per hospitalization—and generated substantially more transfusion episodes. ([ScienceDirect][2])

Most adult observational studies involved hematologic malignancy or transplantation. They generally found that a single-unit policy:

* Reduced total RBC use by roughly 20%–32%
* Produced a lower post-transfusion or discharge hemoglobin
* Sometimes increased the number of separate transfusion episodes
* Did not reveal a detectable increase in mortality, bleeding, transfusion reactions, readmission, or length of stay

Examples include a 25% reduction in RBC use in Berger et al.; a 29% reduction in Bowman et al.; and a 32% reduction after autologous transplantation in Marco-Ayala et al. ([PMC][3])

Not every study demonstrated reduced use. Chantepie et al.’s prospective single-unit cohort had a nonsignificant overall difference of −0.79 units per patient, although the allogeneic-transplant subgroup received fewer units. ([PubMed][4]) Avdić et al. found that a single-unit policy was noninferior for transfusion-episode rate among autologous transplant patients but narrowly missed its prespecified noninferiority criterion in allogeneic transplant patients. ([PubMed][5])

### Children

The major pediatric study was the 3,196-participant TRACT randomized trial in Uganda and Malawi. It compared **30 versus 20 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent**, corresponding approximately to 15 versus 10 mL/kg when packed RBCs were used.

Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% with the larger dose and 4.5% with the smaller dose, HR 0.76 (95% CI 0.54–1.08); this was not statistically significant. Additional transfusion was less frequent with the larger dose: 12.3% versus 18.8%. There were no overall differences in serious adverse events, readmission, or hemoglobin recovery at 180 days. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

A prespecified fever interaction is clinically important:

* Without fever, mortality favored 30 mL/kg: HR 0.43
* With fever, mortality favored 20 mL/kg: HR 1.91

This interaction survived the study’s multiplicity adjustment, but it remains a subgroup result and should not be generalized automatically to other pediatric populations. ([New England Journal of Medicine][7])

### Preterm neonates

Small neonatal randomized trials consistently found that **20 mL/kg produces a larger rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit than 10 or 15 mL/kg**.

Paul et al. found post-transfusion hemoglobin of 14.2 versus 12.0 g/dL after 20 versus 10 mL/kg, without a detectable difference in pulmonary-function measurements. ([PubMed][8]) Wong et al. and Mallett et al. found no attributed increase in acute adverse events with 20 versus 15 mL/kg, but both trials were very small. ([PubMed][9])

Choi et al. found transient changes in cardiac output and B-type natriuretic peptide after transfusion, but the changes did not differ significantly between 20 and 15 mL/kg groups. ([PubMed][10]) Chen et al.’s larger randomized study found no meaningful between-group difference in cerebral or intestinal oxygen saturation; the 20-mL/kg group had fewer subsequent transfusions and a slightly shorter hospitalization, without an observed increase in neonatal complications. ([ScienceDirect][11])

A formula-based trial administered substantially more blood than a fixed 15-mL/kg strategy and achieved a greater hematocrit increase, but did not reduce repeat transfusions or donor exposure; neonatal morbidities were similar. ([PubMed][12])

The two-center Dutch cohort is difficult to interpret causally because clinical transfusion triggers differed despite nominally common guidelines. Among infants born at 28–31 weeks, 20 mL/kg was associated with approximately one fewer transfusion episode, but a larger proportion of infants at that center received any transfusion. The composite of bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy, intraventricular hemorrhage, or death was similar. ([PubMed][13]) Its approximately two-year follow-up did not find transfusion volume to be an independent predictor of death or major neurodevelopmental, visual, or hearing impairment. ([PMC][14])

## Overall interpretation

The clearest established benefit of a **smaller adult dose** is reduced RBC exposure. The trade-offs are a lower post-transfusion hemoglobin and, in some populations, more separate transfusion episodes. Existing adult studies have not shown that routinely giving two units improves mortality, symptoms, length of stay, bleeding, or other major clinical outcomes, but most estimates are imprecise.

In children and neonates, a **larger weight-based dose** reliably produces a larger hemoglobin increment and may postpone or prevent another transfusion. Evidence regarding mortality, organ injury, neurodevelopment, TACO, TRALI, and other uncommon harms is inadequate. The pediatric fever interaction in TRACT is the principal signal suggesting that the effect of volume may depend strongly on the clinical context.

Important limitations are:

* Small neonatal trials and few events
* Historical controls and secular changes in most adult studies
* Changes in thresholds, order sets, or other blood-management practices occurring alongside the dose change
* Inadequate power for rare transfusion-associated harms
* Duplicate or overlapping cohorts and preliminary conference reports
