## Scope and search approach

I treated **transfusion dose** as the amount of RBCs administered after the clinical decision to transfuse had already been made—for example, one versus two units in adults or 10 versus 15–20 mL/kg in children and neonates.

I excluded studies that only compared:

* Higher versus lower hemoglobin **transfusion thresholds**
* Transfusion versus no transfusion
* RBC storage duration or processing
* Massive transfusion, whole-blood resuscitation, or mixed-component protocols
* Cumulative perioperative transfusion without a defined per-event dose comparison

I conducted a targeted search of PubMed-indexed reports and journal full texts through **July 26, 2026**. This is an evidence map rather than a dual-screened systematic review.

## Overall findings

### Adults

The adult evidence almost exclusively concerns **hemodynamically stable, nonactively bleeding patients**, predominantly people with hematologic malignancies or undergoing hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation. One trial enrolled patients with postpartum anemia.

Three randomized trials directly compared an initial **one-unit strategy with a two-unit strategy**:

* In stable postpartum anemia, one-unit transfusion substantially reduced RBC use; approximately 18% of the one-unit group required another unit. There were no detected differences in symptoms, vital signs, hospital stay, 30-day complications, or later quality of life, although the trial was too small to establish safety for rare outcomes. ([PubMed][1])
* In hospitalized patients with hematologic disease, including acute leukemia, one-unit transfusion was noninferior to two units for a composite of serious complications. Quality of life was similar, but the one-unit strategy did not clearly decrease cumulative RBC use or hospital stay. ([ScienceDirect][2])
* A small factorial AML trial found no clear effect of one versus two units on total RBC use, bleeding, treatment outcomes, or other clinical endpoints. ([PubMed][3])

Controlled before–after and cohort studies generally found that implementing a one-unit policy:

* Reduced RBC units administered per transfusion episode
* Frequently reduced cumulative RBC utilization
* Increased the number of separate transfusion episodes in some settings
* Did not identify higher mortality, major bleeding, length of stay, or transfusion-reaction rates

However, these studies relied heavily on historical controls and were not sufficiently powered or controlled to exclude clinically meaningful differences in uncommon harms. ([PubMed][4])

**Adult interpretation:** For the populations studied, routinely giving two units provides no demonstrated clinical advantage over giving one unit followed by reassessment. A one-unit strategy reliably reduces the amount administered at the initial event and often reduces total utilization, but evidence concerning rare adverse outcomes remains imprecise.

### Children

The major pediatric study was the multicenter TRACT randomized trial involving 3,196 children aged 2 months to 12 years with severe anemia in Uganda and Malawi. It compared:

* **Higher volume:** 30 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent, corresponding to approximately 15 mL/kg packed or settled RBCs
* **Lower volume:** 20 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent, corresponding to approximately 10 mL/kg packed or settled RBCs

The higher dose produced a greater early hemoglobin increase and reduced the need for additional transfusions—12.4% versus 18.8%. Overall 28-day mortality was not significantly different: 3.4% versus 4.5%, hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.08. Serious adverse events and longer-term hemoglobin recovery were also similar. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

A notable interaction occurred according to fever at presentation:

* Without fever, mortality was lower with the higher volume: HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.27–0.69.
* With fever, mortality was higher with the higher volume: HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.04–3.49.

Although the interaction remained significant after multiplicity adjustment, it is a subgroup result from a particular severe-anemia population in malaria-endemic settings and requires replication before being used as a general dosing rule. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

**Pediatric interpretation:** A larger dose improves early anemia correction and reduces repeat transfusions, but does not improve overall mortality. The fever interaction raises the possibility of context-dependent harm from the higher volume.

### Preterm neonates

Neonatal studies compared 20 mL/kg with either 10 or 15 mL/kg, or compared a fixed 15 mL/kg dose with an individualized formula-calculated volume.

Consistent physiological findings were:

* Larger volumes produced greater post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit increases.
* Most studies found no clear between-group differences in short-term pulmonary function, cardiac output, cerebral or intestinal oxygenation, or immediate clinical instability.
* Some studies suggested fewer subsequent transfusions or shorter hospitalization with 20 mL/kg.
* None of the small trials was adequately powered to rule out differences in mortality, necrotizing enterocolitis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity, intraventricular hemorrhage, or circulatory overload. ([PubMed][6])

A randomized comparison of fixed 15 mL/kg with formula-calculated dosing found that the calculated-dose group received a substantially larger amount and had a greater hematocrit rise, while neonatal morbidities and death were similar; again, the study was small. ([PMC][7])

A two-center Dutch cohort comparing routine 15 versus 20 mL/kg dosing found no clear short-term clinical advantage from 20 mL/kg. Its linked follow-up study found no association between cumulative transfused volume and death after discharge, developmental delay, blindness, or deafness at approximately 24 months, but center-level confounding and limited sample size weaken causal interpretation. ([PubMed][8])

**Neonatal interpretation:** Larger volumes improve laboratory correction of anemia and might reduce repeat transfusion, but evidence for patient-important benefits or harms is insufficient. “No detected difference” in these small studies should not be interpreted as evidence that uncommon serious harms are equivalent.

## Evidence gaps

The available evidence is concentrated in adult hematology/HSCT populations, African children with severe anemia, and preterm neonates. There is little direct comparative evidence for general medical wards, adult intensive care, perioperative patients, cardiac disease, renal disease, trauma, or active bleeding.

Across age groups, the studies inconsistently measured:

* Transfusion-associated circulatory overload and acute lung injury
* Allogeneic donor exposure
* Organ dysfunction
* Patient-reported recovery
* Long-term neurodevelopment
* Rare serious adverse reactions
