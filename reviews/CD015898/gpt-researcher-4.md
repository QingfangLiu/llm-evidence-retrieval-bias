## Scope

I searched for primary comparative studies in hospitalized patients in which the **amount of red cells given during an individual transfusion event** differed between groups:

* Adults: usually **one unit followed by reassessment versus two units given routinely**.
* Children and neonates: different weight-based doses, such as **10 versus 20 mL/kg**.

I excluded transfusion-threshold trials, transfusion-rate studies, storage-age studies, massive transfusion, autologous transfusion, reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, protocols, editorials, and comments. The search was updated through **July 26, 2026**. This is a targeted evidence map rather than a registered systematic review.

## Main findings

### Adults

The adult evidence is dominated by hemodynamically stable postpartum patients and adults receiving intensive chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation.

**Single-unit transfusion usually reduced RBC use**, although the size of the reduction varied:

* In 66 women with stable postpartum anemia, one unit followed by reassessment reduced mean use from 2.1 to 1.2 units; only 18.2% required another unit. Post-transfusion hemoglobin was lower, but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, breastfeeding, fatigue, depression, and maternal attachment did not differ. ([PubMed][1])
* In hematology before-and-after studies, changing from two units to one unit was associated with reductions in RBC use of approximately 25% to 32%, although patients generally underwent more separate transfusion episodes and sometimes left hospital with slightly lower hemoglobin levels. ([Haematologica][2])
* A 245-patient multicenter randomized hematology trial found one-unit transfusion noninferior to two-unit transfusion for severe complications, ICU admission, or death: 22.4% versus 23.3%. However, total RBC use was not significantly reduced—median seven versus eight units per admission. ([ScienceDirect][3])
* A 91-patient AML factorial trial likewise found that one versus two units per event did not materially change total RBC use, AML outcomes, or bleeding. ([PubMed][4])

Across these adult studies, there was **no convincing signal that routinely giving two units improved mortality, length of stay, bleeding, serious complications, quality of life, or symptoms**. However, most trials were too small to exclude clinically important differences in uncommon events such as transfusion-associated circulatory overload, thrombosis, myocardial injury, or death.

### Children

The principal study is the large TRACT factorial randomized trial involving 3,196 hospitalized Ugandan and Malawian children with severe anemia and severity features.

Children received 30 versus 20 mL/kg of whole-blood equivalent—approximately **15 versus 10 mL/kg of packed red cells**:

* Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% with the larger volume and 4.5% with the smaller volume: HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.08.
* Additional transfusion was required in 12.3% versus 18.8%.
* There was important effect modification by fever at presentation. Among children without fever, the larger volume reduced mortality, HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.27–0.69. Among febrile children, the larger volume increased mortality, HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.04–3.49.
* Readmissions, serious adverse events, and hemoglobin recovery at 180 days did not otherwise differ. ([PubMed][5])

Thus, the pediatric findings do **not** support a simple conclusion that a larger dose is always better or worse. The TRACT fever interaction is clinically important but has not yet been independently replicated in another large dose trial.

### Preterm neonates

The neonatal studies are much smaller and primarily measure post-transfusion hematology, cardiopulmonary physiology, tissue oxygenation, and subsequent transfusion requirements.

Consistent benefits of a larger dose included:

* A larger rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit with 20 versus 10 or 15 mL/kg. ([PubMed][6])
* Possible reductions in subsequent transfusion episodes. One 151-infant trial reported 1.9 versus 2.6 transfusions and a modestly shorter hospitalization with 20 versus 15 mL/kg. ([PubMed][7])
* No material difference in cerebral or intestinal oxygenation between 20 and 15 mL/kg. ([PubMed][7])

Detected harms were uncommon:

* Small randomized studies found no significant between-group differences in pulmonary function, edema, hypoxia, necrotizing enterocolitis, vital-sign instability, cardiac output changes, or BNP response. ([PubMed][6])
* A two-center cohort comparing 15 with 20 mL/kg found similar composite rates of bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity, intraventricular hemorrhage, and mortality. ([PubMed][8])
* Follow-up of the extremely preterm subgroup found no association between total transfusion volume and death or neurodevelopmental, visual, or hearing impairment at approximately 24 months. ([Springer][9])

These studies are substantially underpowered for rare or delayed harms, and the observational comparisons are vulnerable to center effects and different clinical transfusion decisions.

## Overall interpretation

For **stable, nonbleeding adults**, one unit followed by clinical and hemoglobin reassessment generally avoids unnecessary RBC exposure without demonstrated loss of clinical benefit. The evidence for uncommon harms remains imprecise.

For **children with severe anemia**, the optimal dose may depend on the clinical phenotype. TRACT suggests that fever status could reverse the mortality effect of a larger volume.

For **preterm neonates**, 20 mL/kg produces a larger hematologic response and may lengthen the interval to the next transfusion, but there is insufficient evidence to determine whether it improves or worsens major neonatal or long-term outcomes.
