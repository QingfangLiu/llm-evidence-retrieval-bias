Here are individual primary studies (RCTs and prospective cohort studies) specifically addressing whether iron supplementation improves iron stores and/or reduces deferral risk in blood donors. I've excluded reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines as requested.

**Kiss JE, Brambilla D, Glynn SA, et al. "Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA. 2015;313(6):575-583.**
This is one of the pivotal RCTs in the field (also called the HEIRS trial), randomizing 215 donors to study the effect of blood donation and iron supplementation on iron balance. It showed that iron supplementation reduces post-donation hemoglobin and ferritin recovery time.

**Cable RG, Brambilla D, Glynn SA, et al. "Effect of iron supplementation on iron stores and total body iron after whole blood donation." Transfusion. 2016;56(8):2005-2012.**
A secondary/companion analysis of the same 215-donor RCT cohort as Kiss et al., examining iron stores and total body iron more specifically.

**Karregat JHM, Meulenbeld A, Quee FA, et al. "Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals (FORTE): a double-blind, randomised, controlled trial." Lancet Haematology. 2025;12:e694-e704.**
A large double-blind RCT conducted at seven Sanquin donation centers in the Netherlands. Donors with ferritin ≤30 μg/L were randomized to six groups (placebo, 30 mg, or 60 mg elemental iron, given daily or on alternate days) for 56 days. The trial found that daily supplementation with 60 mg of elemental iron was the most effective strategy for mitigating iron deficiency, low ferritin, and low hemoglobin, without causing gastrointestinal discomfort or affecting donors' willingness to return.

**Waldvogel-Abramowski S, et al. "A randomized trial of blood donor iron repletion on red cell quality for transfusion and donor cognition and well-being." Blood. 2022;140(25):2730.**
An RCT testing intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (800 mg) for iron repletion in donors. Iron repletion did not induce a clinically relevant decrease in self-described fatigue and general well-being, though it addressed red cell quality outcomes as well.

**Radtke H, Tegtmeier J, Röcker L, et al. "Daily doses of 20 mg of elemental iron compensate for iron loss in regular blood donors: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study." Transfusion. 2004;44:1427-1432.**
An earlier randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial testing whether a modest daily iron dose (20 mg) could offset the iron loss from repeated donation.

**Marks DC, Speedy J, Robinson KL, et al. "An 8-week course of 45 mg of carbonyl iron daily reduces iron deficiency in female whole blood donors aged 18 to 45 years: results of a prospective randomized controlled trial." Transfusion. 2014;54(3pt2):780-788.**
A prospective randomized controlled trial specifically in female donors testing carbonyl iron supplementation.

**Gordeuk V, Brittenham G, Bravo J, Hughes M, Keating L. "Prevention of iron deficiency with carbonyl iron in female blood donors." Transfusion. 1990;30(3):239-245.**
An earlier RCT testing carbonyl iron for prevention of iron deficiency in female donors.

**Baart AM, et al. (IronWoMan trial) "The Effect of Parenteral or Oral Iron Supplementation on Fatigue, Sleep, Quality of Life and Restless Legs Syndrome in Iron-Deficient Blood Donors."**
This RCT (NCT01787526) randomized 176 iron-deficient whole-blood and apheresis donors (ferritin ≤30 ng/mL) to intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose) or oral iron (iron fumarate) for 10 weeks, comparing effects on iron-related symptoms.

**FIND'EM Trial — van Dongen et al. "Effectiveness of ferritin-guided donation intervals in whole-blood donors in the Netherlands (FIND'EM): a stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial." The Lancet. 2024.**
Note: this trial tests *donation-interval extension* guided by ferritin rather than supplementation directly, but it's a relevant primary RCT on preventing deferral due to low iron. Ferritin-guided donation intervals significantly improved hemoglobin and ferritin concentrations and significantly decreased iron deficiency over the study period, and hemoglobin-based deferrals decreased significantly for male donors.

A few notes on relevance and design differences:
- The **FORTE and Kiss/Cable/HEIRS trials** most directly answer your question — they test oral supplementation's effect on ferritin/hemoglobin recovery and iron deficiency in donors already flagged as low-iron.
- The **Waldvogel-Abramowski and IronWoMan trials** test intravenous rather than oral iron, and focus more on symptoms (fatigue, well-being) than on deferral rates per se.
- The **FIND'EM trial** is a primary study but tests an alternative strategy (interval extension) rather than supplementation — worth knowing about as a comparator even though it doesn't answer your exact question.
