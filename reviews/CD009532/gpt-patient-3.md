## Bottom line

**Yes.** Individual primary studies—especially randomized trials—consistently show that oral iron after blood donation can replenish ferritin and total-body iron, accelerate hemoglobin recovery, and reduce the likelihood of having a low hemoglobin value that could prevent donation. Actual donor deferral was measured in some older trials; many newer trials measured low hemoglobin or donation eligibility as the closest proxy.

In these studies, **ferritin** was generally used to measure iron stores, while the immediate accept/defer decision was generally based on **hemoglobin**, often from a fingerstick.

## Primary prospective implementation studies

These were not placebo-controlled randomized trials but directly examined donors with low hemoglobin or ferritin in real blood-center practice.

| Study                            | Design and findings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Bryant et al., 2012**          | Among 1,236 low-hemoglobin-deferred donors and 400 controls, iron-depleted or iron-deficient donors received 325 mg ferrous sulfate tablets daily. Iron-related laboratory abnormalities generally normalized even with continued donation. Adherence was 68%, and 21% reported gastrointestinal effects. Importantly, evaluation identified 13 donors with serious medical conditions, including eight with gastrointestinal bleeding—evidence that low hemoglobin should not automatically be assumed to result solely from donation. ([PubMed][13]) |
| **Magnussen et al., 2008**       | Prospective ferritin-guided supplementation in 879 donors whose hemoglobin was at or below the donation threshold. Hemoglobin and ferritin increased among returning donors, and after two years **79% of women and 85% of men remained active donors**. Because there was no untreated comparison group, causality is less certain than in the randomized trials. ([Wiley Online Library][14])                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Alvarez-Ossorio et al., 2000** | Ferritin was monitored in long-term donors, and donors with ferritin below 15 µg/L received iron. Hemoglobin rose rapidly in those beginning with low hemoglobin; the investigators reported that deferral was not subsequently required in the supplemented high-frequency donors. This was a retrospective/interventional program rather than a randomized trial. ([PubMed][15])                                                                                                                                                                     |

## Overall interpretation

The primary studies support several conclusions:

1. **Iron supplementation reliably improves iron stores.** The clearest effects are on ferritin and estimated total-body iron.

2. **It speeds hemoglobin recovery.** The HEIRS trial suggests that donors with low ferritin may otherwise require several months to recover from one donation.

3. **It can reduce the chance of donation failure.** Gordeuk directly demonstrated fewer repeat-donation deferrals, while Marks and FORTE found fewer donors with low hemoglobin or ineligible screening values.

4. **Duration matters.** Benefits were often substantial within four to eight weeks, but a 21-day course did not preserve stores through three months in one trial.

5. **Low hemoglobin is not always iron deficiency.** Persistent or recurrent low hemoglobin warrants a CBC, ferritin, and evaluation for other causes rather than indefinite self-treatment. This is particularly important for men, postmenopausal women, or anyone with gastrointestinal symptoms or unexplained blood loss. ([PubMed][13])
