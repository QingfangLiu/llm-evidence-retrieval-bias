## Bottom line

Yes. **Individual randomized trials in blood donors consistently show that oral iron improves ferritin/iron stores and accelerates hemoglobin recovery.** Several studies also found fewer low-hemoglobin deferrals or better eligibility for the next donation. The effect is clearest in donors with low ferritin, frequent donors, and menstruating women.

Donation centers generally determine eligibility from **hemoglobin**, whereas **ferritin** more directly reflects stored iron. Thus, improving ferritin may reduce later low-hemoglobin deferral, but the outcomes are related rather than identical.

## Interpretation

The strongest direct evidence is:

* **Iron-store recovery:** FORTE, HEIRS, STRIDE, Marks and Radtke all show substantial improvement in ferritin or related iron indices.
* **Hemoglobin recovery:** HEIRS shows that recovery can be shortened from several months to roughly one month with supplementation.
* **Reduced inability to donate:** Gordeuk found repeat-donation deferral of 8% versus 36%; Marks found significantly better Hb-based eligibility; FORTE reduced the prevalence of low Hb; older repeated-donation trials also maintained Hb and iron status.

The doses studied vary widely—from about **19–20 mg/day** to 100 mg/day or more. The trials do not establish one universally appropriate regimen. Lower-dose regimens often worked, particularly when given for approximately 56–60 days after donation, whereas higher doses may produce more gastrointestinal symptoms. ([PubMed][2])

A low donor-screening Hb should not automatically be assumed to result solely from donation-related iron deficiency. The Bryant study found important underlying conditions among some deferred donors. Confirming a CBC and ferritin—and seeking medical evaluation for persistent or unexplained low Hb, particularly in men or nonmenstruating women—is prudent before repeatedly self-treating with iron.
