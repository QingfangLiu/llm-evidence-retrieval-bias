Yes, primary research robustly supports that iron supplementation significantly improves iron stores and reduces deferral rates for blood donors. The standard interval between donations (often 56 days) is generally insufficient for many donors—especially women and frequent donors—to replenish the 200–250 mg of iron lost during a single whole-blood donation purely through diet.

Per your criteria, I have excluded reviews and guidelines, focusing entirely on individual primary studies (randomized controlled trials) that explicitly test the efficacy of iron supplementation on donor iron stores and hemoglobin deferral.

## Key Primary Studies

### 1. The Impact of Oral Iron on Hemoglobin Recovery Time

**Kiss et al. (2015)** conducted a landmark randomized clinical trial investigating the kinetics of hemoglobin and iron recovery in blood donors taking daily low-dose oral iron (37.5 mg elemental iron) compared to a control group receiving no iron.

* **Iron Stores:** Participants who took the iron supplement recovered their iron stores in a median of 76 days. In stark contrast, those not taking supplements required a median of more than 168 days, with 67% of the non-supplemented group failing to recover their iron stores entirely by the 168-day mark.
* **Hemoglobin/Deferral:** Iron supplementation dramatically reduced the time required for hemoglobin to recover to 80% of pre-donation levels, successfully mitigating the depletion that leads to deferral, regardless of whether the donor started with low or normal ferritin levels.

### 2. Intravenous Iron Repletion and Deferral Rates

**Hod et al. (2022)** conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effects of replacing the exact amount of iron lost during donation via an intravenous infusion (800 mg ferric carboxymaltose) versus a placebo.

* **Replenishment:** Subjects randomized to intravenous iron saw a full recovery of hemoglobin concentrations 40 to 42 days following their donation, whereas those receiving the placebo remained largely iron deficient and anemic.
* **Deferral:** At the time of the next donation visit, 58% of the women and 23% of the men in the placebo group would have been deferred from allogeneic donation. In the group that received iron repletion, deferral rates plummeted to just 16% of women and 0% of men.

### 3. Evaluating Dose Efficacy for Tolerability

**Bialkowski et al. (2017)** investigated whether lower doses of oral iron were just as effective as higher doses for mitigating iron deficiency. This is a vital clinical question, as GI side effects from high-dose iron often cause donors to abandon the supplements, leading to future deferrals.

* **Findings:** The trial demonstrated that daily oral doses of 19 mg and 38 mg of elemental iron were equivalent in their ability to mitigate iron deficiency and improve total body iron estimates in donors experiencing repeated phlebotomy. Recommending a lower, better-tolerated dose can successfully maintain donor eligibility while keeping side-effect attrition low.
