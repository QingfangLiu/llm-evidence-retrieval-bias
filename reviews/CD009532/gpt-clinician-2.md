## Bottom line

**Oral iron consistently improves ferritin, total-body iron, and hemoglobin recovery after blood donation.** Several randomized trials also found fewer low-Hb/low-ferritin outcomes or better donation eligibility. However, the operational outcome—actually being able to donate at the next visit—is less consistently improved than laboratory measures, particularly among donors already deferred for low screening hemoglobin. ([PubMed][1])

## Clinical interpretation

For donors with **documented low ferritin**, the evidence most strongly supports the following conclusions:

* Oral iron can produce clinically meaningful ferritin recovery within roughly **4–12 weeks**, whereas recovery without supplementation may take more than six months after a single donation. ([PubMed][1])
* Low daily doses—approximately **19–40 mg elemental iron** in several studies—can maintain iron balance in frequent donors, although donors beginning with substantial depletion may require longer treatment or a different regimen. ([PubMed][9])
* The likelihood of low-Hb or low-ferritin ineligibility appears reduced, especially with ferritin-targeted supplementation, but actual donation success also depends on capillary-Hb measurement variability, menstrual or gastrointestinal blood loss, adherence, donation timing and other causes of anemia. ([PubMed][1])
* A low screening Hb should not automatically be attributed to donation-related iron deficiency. In the Bryant study, evaluation of deferred donors identified serious medical conditions, including gastrointestinal bleeding, and many donors with low fingerstick Hb had acceptable venous Hb or were iron replete. ([PMC][5])

The most useful starting papers for this specific clinical question are **FORTE**, **HEIRS**, **STRIDE**, **Marks et al.**, and **Gordeuk et al.**
