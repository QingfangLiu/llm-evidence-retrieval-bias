Now I have enough well-sourced primary studies. Here's a curated list of individual primary studies (RCTs and controlled trials) — not reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines — that test whether iron supplementation improves iron stores/hemoglobin and reduces deferral risk in blood donors.

## Primary studies on iron supplementation in blood donors

**1. Kiss JE, Brambilla D, Glynn SA, et al. "Oral Iron Supplementation After Blood Donation: A Randomized Clinical Trial" (HEIRS study).** *JAMA*. 2015;313(6):575–583.
215 donors at 4 US blood centers, randomized to daily oral ferrous gluconate (37.5 mg) vs. no treatment for 24 weeks post-donation. Participants were stratified by ferritin level and randomized to receive daily oral iron supplements or no treatment for 24 weeks, with primary outcomes of time to recovery of 80% of pre-donation hemoglobin and ferritin recovery to baseline. This is the foundational RCT showing supplementation speeds hemoglobin/ferritin recovery.

**2. Cable RG, Glynn SA, Kiss JE, et al. "Effect of iron supplementation on iron stores and total body iron after whole blood donation."** *Transfusion*. 2016;56(8). Companion analysis of the same 215-donor HEIRS cohort, focused on total body iron/store recovery rather than just hemoglobin.

**3. Bryant BJ, Mast AE, Bialkowski W, et al. "A randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled trial of education and iron supplementation for mitigation of iron deficiency in regular blood donors."** *Transfusion*. 2016. Tests whether dietary education vs. iron pills (vs. placebo) prevents iron deficiency in frequent donors.

**4. Bialkowski W, Kiss JE, Wright DJ, et al. "Estimates of total body iron indicate 19 mg and 38 mg oral iron are equivalent for the mitigation of iron deficiency in individuals experiencing repeated phlebotomy."** *American Journal of Hematology*. 2017;92:851–857. RCT comparing two oral iron doses in donors undergoing repeated phlebotomy.

**5. Hod EA, Brittenham GM, Bitan ZC, et al. "A randomized trial of blood donor iron repletion on red cell quality for transfusion and donor cognition and well-being."** *Blood*. 2022;140(25):2730–2739. Double-blind trial of 79 iron-deficient donors randomized to intravenous iron repletion (1 g low-molecular-weight iron dextran) or placebo; iron repletion did not induce a clinically relevant decrease in self-described fatigue and general well-being, though it did significantly raise hemoglobin and ferritin (as noted in span 1 above from a citing source).

**6. Keller P, von Känel R, Hincapié CA, et al. "The effects of intravenous iron supplementation on fatigue and general health in non-anemic blood donors with iron deficiency: a randomized placebo-controlled superiority trial."** *Scientific Reports*. 2020;10:14219. In terms of secondary outcomes, mean differences were 114.2 µg/L for ferritin and 5.7 g/L for hemoglobin, significantly higher in the iron supplementation group; no group differences were observed for general well-being.

**7. Waldvogel-Abramowski S, Pedrazzini B, Vaucher P, et al. "Clinical evaluation of iron treatment efficiency among non-anemic but iron-deficient female blood donors: a randomized controlled trial."** *BMC Medicine*. 2012;10:8. 154 iron-deficient female donors randomized to 4-week oral ferrous sulfate vs. placebo; treatment increased hemoglobin by 5.2 g/L and ferritin by 14.8 ng/mL, though no significant effect on fatigue was seen.

**8. Schotten N, van Kraaij MGJ, et al. "IronWoMan" RCT** (Sanquin/Graz), with related secondary analysis: 176 iron-deficient whole-blood/apheresis donors randomized to IV ferric carboxymaltose vs. oral ferrous fumarate, comparing recovery of iron stores and symptom outcomes between delivery routes (no placebo arm, since deferred donors already needed treatment).

**9. Karregat JHM, Meulenbeld A, Quee FA, et al. "Ferritin-guided iron supplementation as an alternative or complement to prolonged blood donation intervals (FORTE): a double-blind, randomised, controlled trial."** *Lancet Haematology*. 2025;12:e694–e704. Donors with ferritin ≤30 μg/L randomized to six groups (placebo, 30 mg or 60 mg iron, daily or alternate-day) for 56 days; daily 60 mg elemental iron was most effective for mitigating iron deficiency, low ferritin, and low hemoglobin without GI side effects or reduced donor return.

**10. "Impact of Iron Supplementation in Anemic Voluntary First-Time Blood Donors – Results of a Pilot Trial in Ghana."** *Blood* (ASH annual meeting abstract). 2023;142(Suppl 1):1290. Tested whether low-dose iron supplementation (65 mg elemental iron, three times weekly for four months) could raise hemoglobin enough to make anemic/iron-deficient prospective donors eligible to donate, compared with a non-iron-deficient control group receiving standard nutritional counseling — the study most directly aimed at reversing donor *ineligibility* due to low iron/anemia.

### A note on interpreting these
These trials consistently show iron supplementation raises ferritin and hemoglobin and speeds recovery after donation, and some (e.g., #5, #9) show it can reduce hemoglobin-based deferral risk at a subsequent donation. However, effects on subjective symptoms (fatigue, well-being) have been inconsistent — several trials found lab improvement without matching symptom improvement. I excluded the Cochrane systematic review, the FIND'EM/INTERVAL trials (which test donation-interval policy rather than supplementation itself, though I included FIND'EM context only where cited by others), and the Lancet Haematology comment letter, per your criteria.
