Here are individual primary studies (RCTs and prospective comparative trials) comparing blue-light-assisted TURBT (blue-light cystoscopy, BLC, using hexaminolevulinate/HAL or 5-ALA) with standard white-light TURBT. I've excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guideline documents as requested — though you'll see some of these primary trials get cited repeatedly within the Cochrane review that circulates in this space.

**Stenzl et al. 2010, *Journal of Urology*** — A prospective, randomized, 28-center trial (US, Canada, Europe) enrolling 814 patients suspected of having bladder cancer at increased recurrence risk. Patients underwent white-light cystoscopy, then were randomized to also receive HAL fluorescence cystoscopy or not, prior to resection. Hexaminolevulinate fluorescence cystoscopy significantly improved detection of Ta and T1 lesions and significantly reduced the rate of tumor recurrence at 9 months.

**Grossman, Stenzl et al. 2012, *Journal of Urology*** — Long-term follow-up (extension) of the Stenzl 2010 cohort, following 280 patients in the white-light group and 271 in the fluorescence group for extended time. Median time to recurrence was 9.4 months in the white-light group versus 16.4 months in the HAL group (p = 0.04). The paper concluded that HAL cystoscopy improved long-term time to recurrence with a trend toward better bladder preservation.

**O'Brien et al. 2013, *BJU International*** (Guy's Hospital, UK) — Prospective RCT of 249 newly presenting NMIBC-suspected patients, comparing HAL-guided TURBT plus single-shot mitomycin C against white-light TURBT plus mitomycin C. Despite HAL-PDD offering more accurate diagnostic assessment, the trial did not show lower recurrence rates for newly presenting NMIBC compared with the best current standard of care.

**Prospective RCT reported in *International Urology and Nephrology*, 2013** — 130 patients randomized to HAL-guided blue-light vs. white-light-only cystoscopy/resection, followed up to 40 months. Recurrence-free survival was not significantly different between groups (log-rank p = 0.202), although HAL helped detect additional flat and papillary lesions that would have been missed under white light alone in 16 of 54 patients.

**HELENA study (2021, multicenter European phase III non-inferiority RCT)** — 129 patients with EORTC intermediate-risk NMIBC were randomized to white-light TURB with immediate plus maintenance intravesical chemotherapy, or HAL (Hexvix)-guided TURB with immediate chemotherapy only, testing whether blue-light-guided resection could substitute for prolonged chemotherapy maintenance while maintaining recurrence-free survival.

**China phase III bridging study (2022–2023, NCT05600322)** — A within-patient controlled multicenter trial at seven tertiary hospitals, enrolling 158 screened patients, with 120 undergoing white-light cystoscopy before randomization to high-definition HAL blue-light cystoscopy versus continued white light. This focused on detection sensitivity/specificity and safety rather than long-term recurrence, but is a genuine head-to-head primary trial.

**UK single-center study (Histopathological characteristics and recurrence rates, *PMC*)** — A comparative cohort study of HAL blue-light TURBT/biopsy, examining both primary and secondary (re-resection) arms. In the secondary arm, blue-light re-resection detected significantly more carcinoma in situ than white light (20.7% vs 51.7%, p = 0.0277), with recurrence rates of 33.3% (primary arm) and 37.5% (secondary arm) at 24 months.

On complications specifically: individual trials in this space (including Stenzl 2010 and O'Brien 2013) generally report perioperative adverse events (bleeding, perforation, irritative symptoms) as secondary safety endpoints, and across these trials serious surgical complication rates have not differed meaingfully between blue-light and white-light arms — but I'd recommend pulling the safety tables directly from each paper (via PubMed/full text) if complication rates are your main decision point, since none of these trials were specifically powered to detect complication differences.

A few notes for your own reading:
- Several of the largest, most rigorous trials (Stenzl 2010/Grossman 2012 with 814/551 patients) show a recurrence benefit; other well-conducted trials (O'Brien 2013, the 2013 IUN trial) did not, particularly when patients also received standard adjuvant intravesical chemotherapy — suggesting blue light's benefit may be attenuated once other recurrence-reduction measures are already in place.
- Trial populations differ (newly diagnosed vs. recurrent, low- vs. high-risk NMIBC), which matters a lot for interpreting whether results apply to your specific situation.
- I'm not a doctor — this summary is meant to help you have an informed conversation with your urologist about which trials are most comparable to your case, not to replace their guidance.
