As a clinician managing non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), balancing optimal initial resection with the risks of recurrence and progression is a primary concern. When looking exclusively at individual, primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—and excluding meta-analyses or reviews—the evidence comparing blue-light-assisted TURBT (BL-TURBT) to standard white-light TURBT (WL-TURBT) presents a nuanced picture regarding recurrence, with more consistent findings on progression and safety.

Here is what the primary literature demonstrates across your three clinical outcomes of interest.

## Impact on Recurrence

The primary literature is somewhat mixed on whether BL-TURBT significantly prevents recurrence, largely split between older registrational trials and modern pragmatic trials.

* **Older Phase III Trials:** Early, large-scale RCTs demonstrated a clear advantage for BL-TURBT. A pivotal international phase III trial found that the addition of hexaminolevulinate-guided fluorescence cystoscopy to standard white-light cystoscopy significantly improved tumor detection and reduced recurrence rates at the 9-month follow-up (Stenzl et al., 2010). Long-term follow-up of this same cohort confirmed that the decrease in bladder cancer recurrence remained significant over time (Grossman et al., 2012).
* **Contemporary Pragmatic Trials:** However, a more recent, large-scale pragmatic trial challenges those initial findings. The PHOTO trial—a multicenter RCT in the UK randomizing 538 patients with suspected high-risk NMIBC—found no statistically significant difference in 3-year recurrence-free survival rates between the photodynamic BL-TURBT and WL-TURBT groups (Heer et al., 2022). In this cohort, approximately 42% of patients in both arms experienced a recurrence by year three.

## Impact on Progression

The evidence from primary trials does not strongly support BL-TURBT as a tool for reducing disease progression (advancing to muscle-invasive disease).

* The early foundational RCTs were primarily powered to detect differences in diagnostic yield and early recurrence, so they did not definitively prove a long-term progression benefit (Grossman et al., 2012; Stenzl et al., 2010).
* More recently, the PHOTO trial explicitly evaluated clinical effectiveness over 3 years and found no evidence that photodynamic diagnosis reduced the rate of progression compared to standard white light (Heer et al., 2022).

## Complications and Surgical Safety

Adding a photosensitizer (like hexaminolevulinate or 5-ALA) and using blue light does not appear to compromise surgical safety or increase complications.

* The PHOTO trial prospectively tracked adverse events and found the frequency to be very low and almost identical between the two modalities—5.7% in the photodynamic diagnosis group versus 5.5% in the white-light group (Heer et al., 2022). This indicates that integrating blue light does not introduce significant perioperative morbidity.
