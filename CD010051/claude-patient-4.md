Here's what the primary literature (individual RCTs, not reviews) shows about cyclosporine A (CsA) eye drops for dry eye disease:

## Benefits reported across trials

**Signs and symptoms improve modestly, with effects taking weeks to appear.** In the original phase 3 program, two identical 6-month, multicenter, double-masked, vehicle-controlled trials in 877 patients with moderate to severe dry eye disease compared cyclosporine 0.05% and 0.1% ophthalmic emulsions to vehicle, establishing the drug's basic efficacy signal that led to FDA approval. A companion dose-ranging phase 2 trial found that the therapeutic effect generally emerges after several weeks, and 0.05% and 0.1% concentrations achieve similar efficacy, with higher concentrations offering no additional benefit.

**Newer formulations show similar patterns.** The SANSIKA trial (0.1% cationic emulsion, Ikervis) in 246 patients with severe dry eye found that corneal fluorescein staining improved significantly more with cyclosporine than vehicle, though the composite sign-and-symptom primary endpoint didn't reach significance. Its companion trial, SICCANOVE, found a significantly greater improvement in corneal staining with cyclosporine than vehicle over 6 months in moderate-to-severe dry eye. The newest water-free 0.1% formulation was tested in the ESSENCE-2 randomized trial published in JAMA Ophthalmology in 2023.

**A recurring theme: vehicle/placebo drops often perform surprisingly well**, narrowing the measured benefit of active drug — the vehicle used in these trials is an unpreserved oil-in-water nanoemulsion that itself lubricates and hydrates the eye, which may explain why some trials show smaller-than-expected differences between active drug and control.

## Harms/safety reported

Across the long-term extension studies, cyclosporine appears **locally tolerable but frequently causes stinging/burning at instillation**, and is **not associated with systemic toxicity**. A 3-year open-label extension of the original phase 3 trials in 412 patients found cyclosporine 0.1% was safe, well tolerated, and not associated with systemic side effects, with 95% of patients saying they would continue therapy. In the SANSIKA/SICCANOVE pooled dataset, real-world follow-up work noted treatment-related adverse events occurred in roughly a third of patients, with eye pain and irritation being the most common, consistent with earlier randomized trials.

## A note on interpreting these

Effect sizes are generally modest, and several trials (SANSIKA in particular) failed to hit their primary composite endpoint even though individual sign measures (like corneal staining) improved — this is a known pattern in dry eye trials because symptoms and objective signs often don't move together. If you're weighing this against other options (autologous serum, punctal plugs, lifitegrast, newer agents like perfluorohexyloctane), it may be worth discussing your specific dry eye subtype (aqueous-deficient vs. evaporative, severity, whether Sjögren's is involved) with your ophthalmologist, since several of these trials showed CsA performing differently depending on those factors.
