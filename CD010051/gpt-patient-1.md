## Bottom line

Based only on individual primary studies, topical cyclosporine A appears to **improve objective dry-eye signs more consistently than it improves symptoms**. Across formulations, the most reproducible benefits were reduced corneal or conjunctival staining and, in some trials, increased Schirmer tear production. Large trials frequently found little or no advantage over vehicle for global dryness, discomfort, or OSDI scores. ([PubMed][1])

The principal harm is **temporary burning, stinging, or instillation-site pain**. Its frequency differs considerably by formulation: approximately 15%–24% with the studied 0.09% nanomicellar formulation, 29% with the 0.1% cationic emulsion in SANSIKA, but rates closer to vehicle with the water-free 0.1% solution. Serious treatment-related ocular or systemic events were uncommon in the randomized trials. When blood concentrations were measured, systemic cyclosporine exposure was absent or negligible. ([PMC][2])

## Benefits seen in the trials

### Ocular-surface staining

This is the outcome with the most consistent evidence. CsA improved corneal or conjunctival staining compared with vehicle in the pivotal 0.05% emulsion trials, both 0.09% nanomicellar trials, SICCANOVE and SANSIKA, and the water-free 0.1% trials. The average between-group differences were often statistically significant but numerically modest. For example, SICCANOVE found an approximately 0.23-grade additional improvement in corneal staining, and ESSENCE-2 found an additional 0.4-point improvement on a 15-point total corneal staining scale at 29 days. ([PubMed][1])

Responder analyses sometimes produced more interpretable absolute differences. In ESSENCE-2, 71.6% of CsA-treated participants versus 59.7% receiving vehicle improved by at least three corneal-staining grades. In SANSIKA, however, the prespecified combined sign-and-symptom responder outcome was not significantly different: 28.6% versus 23.1%. ([PMC][3])

### Tear production

In the 0.09% phase 3 trial, 16.6% of treated eyes versus 9.2% of vehicle-treated eyes had a ≥10-mm improvement in unanesthetized Schirmer testing at 12 weeks—an absolute difference of about seven percentage points. The original 0.05% phase 3 program also reported better categorized Schirmer results, although not every tear-production or tear-film-stability outcome was positive. ([AAO Journal][4])

### Symptoms and daily functioning

Symptom improvement is substantially less certain:

* The 877-participant 0.05% program found advantages for blurred vision, artificial-tear use and physician global assessment, but not a broad, consistent benefit across all symptom outcomes. ([PubMed][1])
* Both 0.09% nanomicellar trials found improvement from baseline in symptoms in all groups, with **no significant difference from vehicle in the global symptom score**. ([PMC][2])
* SICCANOVE found no significant difference in average ocular discomfort, although 50.2% versus 41.9% achieved a prespecified ≥25% improvement. ([PubMed][5])
* SANSIKA found nearly identical OSDI changes: −13.6 with CsA and −14.1 with vehicle. ([Sage Journals][6])
* ESSENCE-2 found no dryness advantage at day 29: −12.2 points with CsA versus −13.6 with vehicle. The Chinese water-free trial similarly found no significant difference in its primary dryness outcome. ([JAMA Network][7])
* A Chinese 0.05% emulsion trial did find significantly better OSDI scores at days 56 and 84, suggesting that some populations or formulations may experience a delayed symptom benefit. ([PubMed][8])

The large improvement frequently seen in vehicle groups is clinically important. The vehicle is not necessarily inert from the patient’s perspective: it can lubricate the eye and improve symptoms, making it difficult for CsA to demonstrate additional symptom relief.

## Harms

### Instillation pain, burning and irritation

Rates depend strongly on formulation:

* **CsA 0.09% nanomicellar solution:** instillation pain occurred in 15.1% with 0.09%, 13.2% with 0.05%, and 3.3% with vehicle in the phase II/III trial. In the larger phase 3 trial, it occurred in 24.2% versus 4.3%; 3.5% versus 0.5% stopped medication because of treatment-related adverse events. ([PMC][2])
* **CsA 0.1% cationic emulsion:** SANSIKA reported instillation pain in 29.2% versus 8.9%, predominantly mild. ([Sage Journals][6])
* **CsA 0.05% emulsion:** in the 240-participant Chinese trial, drug-related adverse events occurred in 5.0% versus 2.5%, most commonly mild-to-moderate ocular pain. ([PubMed][8])
* **Water-free CsA 0.1%:** ESSENCE reported instillation pain in 2.5% versus 1.2%. ESSENCE-2 found overall adverse-event rates of 16.8% versus 17.8%, with mostly mild instillation reactions in 10.2% versus 8.8%. ([PMC][9])

These rates should not be compared as though they came from a single head-to-head trial: populations, terminology, follow-up and vehicles differed.

### Serious and systemic harms

The controlled trials did not identify a consistent increase in serious ocular events, intraocular pressure changes, clinically important vision loss or treatment-related systemic events. The early dose-ranging study found a maximum blood CsA concentration of only 0.16 ng/mL. In the SANSIKA extension, concentrations were generally undetectable or negligible; the two patients with higher measurements had already been receiving systemic cyclosporine. ([PubMed][10])

Longer-term safety evidence is less rigorous because it generally comes from open-label extensions. In the 52-week ESSENCE-2 extension, 27.5% experienced an ocular treatment-emergent event; the most common was mild instillation pain, occurring in 6.5%. Without a control group, that study cannot determine how much was attributable to CsA. ([PubMed][11])

## How I would interpret this for a treatment decision

A realistic expectation is:

**More likely:** improvement in corneal staining, ocular-surface inflammation or tear production.

**Less predictable:** noticeable improvement in dryness, burning, fluctuating vision or overall OSDI score.

**Most likely adverse effect:** short-lived burning or pain after the drop, although formulation appears to matter substantially.

Most pivotal studies evaluated treatment over approximately 12 weeks to six months. Some newer formulations demonstrated staining differences within two to four weeks, whereas symptom separation—when present—often appeared later. An absence of dramatic symptom improvement in the first few weeks therefore does not necessarily mean that ocular-surface signs are not improving. ([PubMed][8])

Important limitations are the strong vehicle response, heterogeneous dry-eye phenotypes, numerous outcome measures, occasional reliance on post hoc responder analyses and limited direct head-to-head evidence. Many pivotal studies were manufacturer-sponsored, and several included sponsor employees as authors. ([AAO Journal][12])
