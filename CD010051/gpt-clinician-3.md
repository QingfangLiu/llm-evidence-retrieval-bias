## Evidence summary

I identified prospective primary human studies through **July 25, 2026**, prioritizing randomized vehicle-controlled trials and using extension studies for longer-term safety and durability. I excluded reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, committee statements, and editorials. This is a focused evidence synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic review of every small or disease-subgroup study.

### Overall conclusion

Topical cyclosporine A produces a **reasonably consistent improvement in objective dry-eye signs**, particularly corneal staining and, in some trials, tear production. The incremental improvement over vehicle is often modest.

The effect on **patient-reported symptoms is substantially less consistent**. Several pivotal trials met a sign endpoint but failed their prespecified symptom endpoint. This signs–symptoms discordance is seen across conventional emulsion, nanomicellar, cationic-emulsion, and water-free formulations. ([PubMed][1])

Harms are predominantly **local and transient**—burning, stinging, instillation-site pain, irritation, and hyperemia. Serious ocular or systemic adverse effects were uncommon in the trials, and systemic cyclosporine exposure was generally undetectable or negligible. Tolerability differs meaningfully between formulations. ([ScienceDirect][2])

## Benefits

### Corneal and conjunctival staining

This is the most reproducible benefit.

The two large phase 3 conventional-emulsion trials, totaling 877 participants, found significantly greater improvement in corneal staining with both 0.05% and 0.1% CsA than with vehicle. The 0.05% formulation also improved blurred vision and reduced artificial-tear use, although not every measured symptom or sign separated from vehicle. ([PubMed][1])

Similar findings occurred with newer formulations:

* **OTX-101 0.09% nanomicellar solution:** improved corneal staining at days 28, 56, and 84 and conjunctival staining at days 56 and 84. ([PubMed][3])
* **CsA 0.1% cationic emulsion:** SICCANOVE showed a mean corneal-fluorescein-staining advantage of approximately 0.23 grades over vehicle at six months. SANSIKA also showed greater staining improvement, although its composite primary endpoint was negative. ([PMC][4])
* **Water-free CsA 0.1%:** ESSENCE-1, ESSENCE-2, and a Chinese phase 3 trial showed statistically greater total corneal staining improvement, generally apparent within two to four weeks. ([PubMed][5])
* **CsA gel 0.05%:** in COSMO, 73.7% versus 53.2% achieved at least a one-grade improvement in inferior corneal staining at day 84—an absolute difference of 20.5%, corresponding to approximately one additional responder for every five treated. ([PMC][6])

The clinical importance of small mean staining-grade differences is less certain than their statistical significance. Responder analyses are generally easier to interpret clinically, but responder definitions differed substantially across studies.

### Tear production

Some trials demonstrated increased Schirmer responses, but the finding was not universal.

In the OTX-101 phase 3 trial, **16.6% versus 9.2%** achieved an increase of at least 10 mm in Schirmer score—an absolute difference of 7.4%, or approximately one additional responder per 14 treated. ([PubMed][3])

The conventional-emulsion phase 3 studies also found more categorized Schirmer responders with CsA than vehicle. The Chinese CsA-emulsion trials reported Schirmer improvements at selected time points, although results across tear-film measures were mixed. ([PubMed][1])

Schirmer testing is variable, and these results should not be interpreted as showing that most treated patients develop large increases in tear production.

### Symptoms and visual function

Symptom benefits were inconsistent and usually smaller than sign benefits.

Examples of negative or equivocal symptom findings include:

* OTX-101 improved staining but did **not** significantly improve the prespecified symptom endpoint versus vehicle. Both groups had substantial symptom improvement. ([PubMed][7])
* In SANSIKA, OSDI improved similarly with CsA and vehicle; the composite staining-plus-OSDI primary endpoint was not significant. ([Sage Journals][8])
* In ESSENCE-1, OSDI did not differ significantly, although an eye-dryness visual-analog score favored CsA. ([PubMed][5])
* In ESSENCE-2, dryness improvement did not differ significantly despite better corneal staining. ([PMC][9])
* In the Chinese water-free trial, staining improved substantially, but the dryness-score difference was not significant. ([PubMed][10])

Positive findings included improvements in selected symptoms—such as dryness, foreign-body sensation, blurred vision, artificial-tear use, or responder-defined ocular discomfort—in some conventional-emulsion and cationic-emulsion trials. In SICCANOVE, 50.2% versus 41.9% achieved at least a 25% ocular-discomfort reduction, an absolute difference of 8.3%, or approximately one additional responder per 13 treated. ([PubMed][1])

An uncontrolled six-month study of conventional CsA 0.05% reported improved staining, blurred vision, and functional visual measures, but without a control group it cannot distinguish treatment effects from regression to the mean, concomitant treatment, or natural variation. ([PMC][11])

### Time to improvement and durability

Objective signs can begin improving within **two to four weeks**, particularly with water-free and gel formulations. Symptom improvement may lag behind sign improvement and may require several months. A 36-month cationic-emulsion study reported that staining improvement preceded symptom improvement by roughly three to six months, although its initial treatment period was open-label. ([PubMed][5])

Long-term continuation data suggest that benefits can be maintained, but much of this evidence is open-label. Following discontinuation after SANSIKA, approximately 39% of selected prior responders relapsed during the two-year observation period; the sample was small and enriched for initial responders. ([PubMed][12])

## Harms

### Instillation discomfort

The clearest attributable harm is local instillation discomfort.

* **OTX-101 0.09%:** instillation-site pain occurred in **24.2% versus 4.3%** with vehicle—an absolute excess of 19.9%, or roughly one additional case for every five treated. About 2.4% discontinued because of instillation pain versus none receiving vehicle. ([AAO Journal][13])
* **CsA 0.1% cationic emulsion in SANSIKA:** instillation-site pain occurred in **29.2% versus 8.9%**, again approximately one additional case per five treated. Most episodes were mild. ([Sage Journals][8])
* **CsA gel 0.05% in COSMO:** eye pain occurred in 8.1% versus 2.2%, an absolute excess of 5.9%, or approximately one additional case per 17 treated. ([PMC][6])
* **Conventional emulsion during long-term treatment:** burning was reported in 10.9%, stinging in 3.9%, and conjunctival hyperemia in 3.4%. This extension had no vehicle comparator. ([ScienceDirect][2])
* **Water-free CsA 0.1%:** in ESSENCE-2, mild instillation reactions were similar—9.9% with CsA and 8.5% with vehicle. In its one-year extension, instillation pain was reported by 6.5%, all mild. ([JAMA Network][14])

Thus, irritation is not simply a class-wide fixed rate: both the CsA delivery system and its vehicle appear important.

### Discontinuation and serious adverse events

Treatment discontinuation because of adverse events was generally uncommon, although it was higher during the first year of the 36-month cationic-emulsion study, where approximately 7% discontinued during the open-label treatment phase. ([Springer][15])

Across the larger trials:

* No consistent treatment-related reduction in visual acuity or increase in intraocular pressure was found.
* Serious treatment-related ocular events were rare.
* No consistent signal for ocular infection or microbial overgrowth emerged.
* Measured blood cyclosporine concentrations were absent, negligible, or far below systemic therapeutic levels. ([PubMed][16])

However, trials were not large or long enough to exclude extremely rare harms, and many excluded patients with active ocular infection or substantial comorbidity.

## Clinical interpretation

For counseling, the evidence supports framing CsA as a treatment that is **more likely to improve ocular-surface staining than to produce a large, readily perceptible symptom improvement**. Some patients do obtain symptom or visual-function benefit, but average symptom separation from a lubricating vehicle is often small or absent.

A reasonable evidence-based expectation is:

* Improvement in staining may appear during the first month.
* Symptom response, when it occurs, may take several months.
* Burning or instillation pain is common enough to discuss in advance and is formulation-dependent.
* Serious systemic immunosuppressive toxicity has not been demonstrated with topical ocular dosing in these trial populations.
* Direct cross-trial comparisons should be avoided: formulations, vehicles, dosing frequency, baseline severity, outcome instruments, and responder definitions differed. Most formulations have not been compared head-to-head in adequately powered masked trials.

The evidence is also affected by large vehicle responses. Ophthalmic vehicles lubricate the surface and are not physiologically inert, making the incremental CsA effect smaller than the before–after improvement seen within active-treatment groups.
