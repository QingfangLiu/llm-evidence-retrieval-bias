## Scope

I searched for **human primary studies of topical cyclosporine A used to treat established dry eye disease**, emphasizing randomized controlled trials, active-comparator trials, prospective cohorts, and long-term extensions available through **July 25, 2026**. I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, guidelines, committee statements, editorials, animal studies, and studies primarily concerned with postoperative prophylaxis rather than treatment of dry eye.

## Overall interpretation

**Cyclosporine eye drops improve objective ocular-surface signs more consistently than they improve symptoms.** Across formulations, the most reproducible findings are reductions in corneal or conjunctival staining and, in some trials, increased Schirmer tear production. Differences in OSDI, global discomfort, or dryness scores are often small or statistically nonsignificant because both groups improve substantially. ([ScienceDirect][1])

Benefits appear to take different amounts of time depending on the formulation. Conventional emulsions were generally studied over three to six months. Nanomicellar and water-free preparations showed statistically detectable staining improvements within two to four weeks in some trials, although rapid improvement in staining did not consistently translate into rapid symptom relief. ([PubMed][2])

**The principal harm is transient local discomfort—burning, stinging, pain, or irritation after instillation.** Its incidence varies considerably by formulation. Serious ocular or systemic treatment-related events were uncommon in the controlled trials, and clinically important effects on intraocular pressure, visual acuity, or systemic cyclosporine exposure were generally not detected. ([Sage Journals][3])

### Conventional oil-in-water emulsion, usually 0.05% twice daily

The original dose-ranging trial found improvements in staining and several symptoms, but no clear dose-response relationship; the vehicle itself produced substantial improvement. ([PubMed][4])

The two pivotal phase 3 trials—published together, with 877 participants total—found better corneal staining and categorized Schirmer results with 0.05% and 0.1% cyclosporine than with vehicle. The 0.05% arm also showed advantages for blurred vision, artificial-tear use, and investigator global response, but many other symptom outcomes were not different. ([PubMed][5])

A later Chinese phase 3 trial of 0.05% emulsion reported a higher composite “overall effective rate” at 12 weeks, 70.6% versus 27.8%, and better OSDI at days 56 and 84. Drug-related adverse events occurred in 5.0% versus 2.5%, chiefly ocular pain. ([PMC][6])

### Cationic emulsion, 0.1% once daily

In **SICCANOVE**, corneal fluorescein staining improved modestly more with cyclosporine than vehicle, −1.05 versus −0.82 modified Oxford grades. Mean ocular-discomfort improvement was similar, although a responder analysis favored cyclosporine. ([PMC][7])

In the more severe **SANSIKA** population, the prespecified combined sign-and-symptom primary endpoint was not met: 28.6% versus 23.1%, (P=.326). Corneal staining and the HLA-DR inflammatory marker favored cyclosporine, but mean OSDI improvement was essentially identical. Instillation-site pain occurred in 29.2% versus 8.9%, mostly mild or moderate. ([Sage Journals][3])

Longer-term SANSIKA follow-up suggests that improvement can continue during 12 months of treatment and may persist after discontinuation in some responders, but these phases were open-label or selected only treatment responders, substantially weakening causal interpretation. ([PMC][8])

### Nanomicellar solution, OTX-101 0.09% twice daily

The phase 2/3 dose-ranging study met its conjunctival-staining endpoint for both 0.05% and 0.09%, but not its symptom endpoint. Instillation-site pain occurred in approximately 15.1% with 0.09%, 13.2% with 0.05%, and 3.3% with vehicle. ([PMC][9])

The larger phase 3 trial found a ≥10-mm Schirmer response in 16.6% versus 9.2% and better corneal and conjunctival staining, but no between-group separation in global symptoms. Instillation-site pain occurred in 24.2% versus 4.3%; 2.4% stopped treatment because of it. No serious ocular adverse events were reported. ([AAO Journal][10])

A 2026 single-arm switch study reported improvements after patients inadequately controlled with 0.05% emulsion changed to 0.09% solution. Because it lacked a concurrent comparator and included treatment preference as an outcome, it cannot establish that the new formulation caused the improvement or is superior. ([PubMed][11])

### Water-free cyclosporine 0.1% twice daily

A phase 2 study found earlier staining improvement with the water-free formulation than with vehicle and an open-label Restasis arm, but it was exploratory and the Restasis comparison was not masked. ([PubMed][2])

In **ESSENCE**, total corneal staining favored cyclosporine at four weeks by −0.8 points, but the hierarchically tested OSDI endpoint was not significant. Eye-dryness VAS favored cyclosporine as a secondary outcome. ([PMC][12])

In **ESSENCE-2**, the mean total staining difference at day 29 was −0.4 points. The dryness primary endpoint did not favor cyclosporine, while a ≥3-grade staining response occurred in 71.6% versus 59.7%. Overall treatment-emergent adverse events were similar—16.8% versus 17.8%—as were ocular events and instillation-site reactions. ([PubMed][13])

The corresponding Chinese phase 3 trial found a larger staining difference, −1.8 points, but again no statistically significant dryness-score difference. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 14.6% versus 10.7%. ([PMC][14])

The one-year ESSENCE-2 extension reported continued within-patient improvement and instillation-site pain in 6.5%, but all participants received cyclosporine and there was no long-term control group. ([PubMed][15])

### Ophthalmic gel

In the phase 3 **COSMO** trial, once-nightly 0.05% CyclAGel produced a ≥1-grade inferior corneal-staining improvement in 73.7% versus 53.2%. Schirmer and other staining outcomes also favored cyclosporine, but dryness and other symptom scores were not superior to vehicle. Eye pain occurred in 6.9% versus 1.6%; no serious treatment-related events occurred. ([PMC][16])

### Clinically important limitations

Most pivotal studies were manufacturer-sponsored. Vehicle formulations often contained emulsions, carbomers, semifluorinated alkanes, or other lubricating components that had therapeutic effects of their own. This helps explain the large vehicle responses and makes these trials comparisons of **cyclosporine plus formulation effects versus formulation alone**, rather than cyclosporine versus no treatment. ([Sage Journals][3])

Most controlled follow-up was 12 weeks to six months. The longer studies primarily used open-label extensions, selected responders, or before-and-after comparisons. They are useful for detecting common tolerability problems but provide weaker evidence about sustained efficacy and uncommon harms. ([ScienceDirect][17])

Dry-eye populations and outcome definitions differed considerably: aqueous-deficient versus evaporative disease, Sjögren versus non-Sjögren disease, mild versus severe keratitis, different staining scales, and numerous symptom instruments. Results should therefore not be assumed to be directly interchangeable across formulations.
