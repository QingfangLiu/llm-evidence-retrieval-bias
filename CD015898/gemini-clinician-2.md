As a clinician, you are likely navigating the ongoing cultural shift away from default double-unit red blood cell (RBC) orders toward smaller, single-unit strategies (often championed by "Why give two when one will do?" patient blood management programs).

For hemodynamically stable, non-actively bleeding patients, primary cohort studies and interventional trials demonstrate that smaller transfusion doses provide equal efficacy while limiting donor exposure.

Here is the breakdown of the clinical benefits and harms based on primary literature.

## Benefits of Smaller Doses (Single-Unit)

* **Reduced Total Donor Exposure:** Implementing a single-unit policy significantly lowers the total number of RBC units a patient receives during a hospital admission or treatment cycle (Berger et al., 2011; Bowman et al., 2019).
* **Preventing Hemoglobin Overshoot:** Double-unit transfusions frequently push a patient's post-transfusion hemoglobin higher than evidence-based targets. A single-unit approach correctly titrates the dose, preventing overtreatment while maintaining safe physiological parameters (Frank et al., 2017).
* **Preserved Clinical Outcomes:** Reducing the dose to a single unit per transfusion event does not negatively impact 30-day mortality, length of hospital stay, or overall survival rates (Berger et al., 2011; Bowman et al., 2019).
* **System-Wide Resource Conservation:** System-wide campaigns prioritizing single units lead to a notable decrease in the odds of unnecessary transfusions across surgical and medical wards, protecting the blood supply (Medvecz et al., 2020).

## Harms and Trade-offs

* **Increased Transfusion Episodes:** Even though total blood volume decreases, patients may undergo more distinct, separate transfusion events. One study found single-unit patients averaged 4.1 transfusion episodes per admission compared to 2.7 in double-unit patients (Bowman et al., 2019).
* **Lower Discharge Hemoglobin:** Patients receiving smaller doses may be discharged with a statistically (though typically not clinically) lower mean hemoglobin, such as 8.9 g/dL compared to 9.5 g/dL in double-unit cohorts (Bowman et al., 2019).
* **Clinical Workflow Burden:** Transfusing a single unit requires nursing and phlebotomy staff to clinically reassess the patient and draw a new hemoglobin level before a second unit can be ordered. This increases intermediate testing and workflow demands on the floor.

## Strategy Comparison

| Clinical Factor | Single-Unit Strategy (Smaller Dose) | Double-Unit Strategy (Larger Dose) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Total RBC Usage** | Lower total units per admission | Higher overall donor blood exposure |
| **Post-Transfusion Target** | Accurately reaches evidence-based goals | Often overshoots the hemoglobin target |
| **Transfusion Episodes** | More frequent individual events | Fewer separate IV procedures |
| **Overall Survival** | Equivalent | Equivalent |
| **Staff Workload** | Higher (requires reassessment between units) | Lower (less intermediate testing) |
