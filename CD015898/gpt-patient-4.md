## Scope

This answer concerns **how much red blood cell product is given after the decision to transfuse has already been made**:

* Adults: usually **one unit versus two units per transfusion episode**.
* Children and neonates: different weight-based volumes, such as **10, 15, 20, or 30 mL/kg**.

I excluded studies that only compared different hemoglobin thresholds for deciding *whether* to transfuse.

## Main findings

### Stable, nonbleeding adults

The strongest direct evidence consists of three randomized trials:

* In 66 hemodynamically stable postpartum patients, starting with one unit instead of two reduced mean use from 2.1 to 1.2 units. Only 18% needed a second unit. Post-transfusion hemoglobin was lower after one unit, but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, fatigue, breastfeeding, depression, and maternal attachment were similar. ([PubMed][1])
* In 245 adults undergoing intensive chemotherapy or stem-cell transplantation, one unit was noninferior to two units for a composite of serious complications, ICU admission, and death: 22.4% versus 23.3%. Total red-cell use was not significantly reduced—median seven versus eight units—and the one-unit group had more transfusion episodes. ([ScienceDirect][2])
* In a small factorial trial of patients with acute myeloid leukemia, 91 were randomized and 77 completed the protocol. One versus two units did not materially affect total red-cell use, bleeding, leukemia outcomes, or recorded transfusion reactions. The trial was too small to exclude uncommon harms. ([PubMed][3])

Several before-and-after and historical-control studies generally found that switching to one unit reduced total red-cell use by roughly 10%–32%, although some found more transfusion visits or episodes and a slightly lower hemoglobin at discharge. None demonstrated a clear increase in mortality, length of stay, severe bleeding, or transfusion complications, but these observational designs cannot reliably exclude moderate or rare harms. ([PubMed][4])

**Interpretation:** For a stable, nonbleeding adult, giving one unit and then reassessing often avoids an unnecessary second unit. The trade-off is a smaller immediate hemoglobin increase and, in some populations, more separate transfusion episodes. Evidence is concentrated in hematology, transplantation, and postpartum care—not general medical, surgical, cardiac, or actively bleeding patients.

### Children

The major study was the TRACT randomized trial involving 3,196 children aged 2 months to 12 years with severe anemia in Uganda and Malawi. Children received either 30 or 20 mL/kg of whole-blood equivalent—approximately 15 versus 10 mL/kg of packed cells.

* Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% with the higher volume and 4.5% with the lower volume, a difference that was not statistically significant.
* Additional transfusion was needed less often with the higher volume: 12.3% versus 18.8%.
* A prespecified/subgroup analysis showed an important interaction with fever: among children without fever, mortality was lower with the higher volume; among children with fever, mortality was higher with the higher volume.
* Readmission, serious adverse events, and hemoglobin recovery at 180 days were similar. ([New England Journal of Medicine][5])

**Interpretation:** A larger dose reduced repeat transfusions and corrected anemia faster, but it did not improve overall survival. The fever interaction raises a genuine safety concern but comes from children with very severe anemia, frequent malaria, and care circumstances that may differ substantially from other hospitals. It should not be applied automatically to other children.

### Preterm neonates

The neonatal studies consistently show that a larger volume produces a greater immediate rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit:

* In a 20-infant randomized trial, 20 mL/kg produced substantially higher post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit than 10 mL/kg, without a measured difference in pulmonary function. ([PubMed][6])
* A second small randomized trial comparing 20 with 15 mL/kg found no attributed adverse events and no significant reduction in the number of transfusions. ([PubMed][7])
* A 22-infant crossover trial found higher hemoglobin and hematocrit after 20 versus 15 mL/kg, without detected differences in edema, tachypnea, hypoxia, necrotizing enterocolitis, or unstable vital signs. ([PMC][8])
* In 39 stable preterm infants, both 15 and 20 mL/kg produced transient hemodynamic changes, but there was no significant difference between doses in cardiac output or B-type natriuretic peptide response. ([PubMed][9])
* In a randomized study of 151 premature infants, 20 mL/kg produced a larger hemoglobin/hematocrit rise, fewer subsequent transfusions, and a shorter post-transfusion hospitalization than 15 mL/kg. Tissue oxygenation changes and neonatal complications did not differ significantly. ([ScienceDirect][10])
* An observational comparison of two neonatal centers found that 20 mL/kg lengthened the interval to the next transfusion and reduced episodes in some older-gestation preterm infants, but morbidity and mortality were similar. Different practical transfusion thresholds between centers make the dose effect difficult to isolate. ([PubMed][11])

These trials were generally too small to determine effects on uncommon but important outcomes such as circulatory overload, necrotizing enterocolitis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, death, or long-term neurodevelopment.

## Benefits and harms in practical terms

| Smaller amount                                                                                | Larger amount                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Less donor blood administered initially                                                       | Greater and faster hemoglobin/hematocrit increase                                              |
| Often reduces total adult blood use                                                           | May reduce repeat transfusions, especially in children or neonates                             |
| Allows reassessment before giving more                                                        | May be useful when a rapid, substantial correction is required                                 |
| Lower post-transfusion hemoglobin                                                             | More blood and fluid exposure at one time                                                      |
| May require another transfusion or more episodes                                              | Rare volume-related or other adverse effects remain insufficiently studied                     |
| In febrile children with severe anemia, the lower dose performed better in the TRACT subgroup | In the same trial, the higher dose was associated with higher mortality among febrile children |

For a **stable adult who is not actively bleeding**, the evidence supports asking: **“Could I receive one unit and then have my symptoms, vital signs, and hemoglobin reassessed before receiving another?”** This evidence does not directly apply to major bleeding, shock, urgent surgery, severe ongoing hemolysis, or situations in which rapid replacement is necessary.
