## Overview

Below are individual primary studies (randomized controlled trials) directly comparing restrictive vs. liberal transfusion thresholds/strategies in hospitalized patients. These are landmark, peer-reviewed RCTs — not systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or guidelines.

**General pattern across trials:** restrictive transfusion strategies (lower hemoglobin thresholds, typically 7–8 g/dL) generally produce outcomes similar to or better than liberal strategies (thresholds around 9–10 g/dL) in most populations, while reducing blood exposure. Exceptions and nuances exist in cardiac patients (acute MI, cardiac surgery), where some trials show signals favoring a liberal approach.

**Critically ill adults (general ICU):**
The TRICC trial enrolled 838 critically ill patients and randomly assigned 418 to a restrictive strategy (transfusion below 7.0 g/dL, maintained at 7.0–9.0 g/dL) and 420 to a liberal strategy (transfusion below 10.0 g/dL, maintained at 10.0–12.0 g/dL). Overall 30-day mortality was similar between groups (18.7% vs. 23.3%, P=0.11), and the authors concluded a restrictive strategy is at least as effective as, and possibly superior to, a liberal strategy, with the possible exception of patients with active coronary ischemic syndromes.

**Septic shock:**
The TRISS trial randomized 998 ICU patients with septic shock and hemoglobin below 9 g/dL to a restrictive (≤7 g/dL) or liberal (≤9 g/dL) transfusion strategy, finding similar 90-day mortality but 50% fewer blood units used in the restrictive group.

**Hip fracture surgery (high cardiovascular risk):**
The FOCUS trial enrolled 2016 patients aged 50+ with cardiovascular disease or risk factors and hemoglobin below 10 g/dL after hip-fracture surgery, randomizing them to a liberal strategy (threshold 10 g/dL) or restrictive strategy (symptoms of anemia or physician discretion for hemoglobin below 8 g/dL). At 60 days, the rate of death or inability to walk unassisted was similar between groups.

**Cardiac surgery:**
The TRICS III trial compared restrictive vs. liberal red-cell transfusion in patients undergoing cardiac surgery at moderate-to-high risk of death, finding restrictive strategy was noninferior to liberal strategy for the composite outcome of death, MI, stroke, or new renal failure requiring dialysis, with less blood transfused. A related earlier trial (TITRe2) was also noted to have found a restrictive transfusion strategy was associated with increased 90-day mortality compared to a liberal strategy in cardiac surgery patients, highlighting inconsistency in this population.

**Acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding:**
The Villanueva trial enrolled 921 patients with severe acute upper GI bleeding, randomly assigning 461 to a restrictive strategy (transfusion below 7 g/dL) and 460 to a liberal strategy (transfusion below 9 g/dL). The restrictive strategy significantly improved outcomes: reduced further bleeding, reduced need for rescue therapy, reduced complications, and increased survival.

**Critically ill children:**
The TRIPICU trial enrolled 637 stable, critically ill children with hemoglobin below 9.5 g/dL, comparing a restrictive strategy (7 g/dL) to a liberal strategy (9.5 g/dL) with the primary outcome of new or progressive multiple organ dysfunction; the restrictive approach was as safe as the liberal one while significantly reducing transfusion exposure.

**Acute myocardial infarction with anemia:**
The MINT trial enrolled 3506 patients with MI and anemia from April 2017 through April 2023. Death occurred in 9.9% of restrictive-strategy patients versus 8.3% of liberal-strategy patients (risk ratio 1.19, 95% CI 0.96–1.47); myocardial infarction occurred in 8.5% vs. 7.2% — a trend, not statistically significant, favoring the liberal strategy.
