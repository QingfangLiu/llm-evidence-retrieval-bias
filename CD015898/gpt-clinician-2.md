## Scope

I treated “larger versus smaller amount” as the **RBC dose per transfusion event after the decision to transfuse has already been made**:

* Adults: usually **1 versus 2 RBC units**
* Children/neonates: different weight-based volumes, such as **10, 15, or 20 mL/kg**

I excluded transfusion-threshold trials, transfusion-versus-no-transfusion studies, massive-transfusion protocols, reviews, guidelines, opinions, and editorials.

## Main findings

### Stable, nonbleeding adults

The randomized adult evidence I found is limited to postpartum anemia and hematology/hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation populations.

In 66 hemodynamically stable postpartum patients, beginning with one unit reduced mean utilization from 2.1 to 1.2 units. Only 18.2% of the one-unit group subsequently needed another unit. The two-unit group had a higher post-transfusion hemoglobin—8.7 versus 7.8 g/dL—but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, fatigue, breastfeeding, depression, and maternal attachment outcomes did not differ detectably. ([PubMed][1])

In a multicenter randomized noninferiority trial of 245 adults receiving intensive chemotherapy or stem-cell transplantation, serious grade ≥3 complications, ICU admission, or death occurred in 22.4% with one-unit dosing and 23.3% with two-unit dosing. Median total utilization was 7 versus 8 units per admission, which was not a significant or clinically large reduction; discharge hemoglobin was comparable. Thus, one-unit dosing was noninferior for the composite safety outcome but did not necessarily reduce total exposure substantially in this intensely transfusion-dependent population. ([ScienceDirect][2])

Comparative cohort studies in hematologic malignancy and transplantation generally found:

* Approximately **25% to 32% lower total RBC utilization** after changing to one-unit dosing
* A **lower immediate hemoglobin increment** and sometimes lower discharge hemoglobin
* The same or a greater number of separate transfusion episodes
* No detected increase in mortality, length of stay, major bleeding, readmission, or recorded transfusion reactions—but the observational designs cannot reliably exclude uncommon harms such as TACO, TRALI, thrombosis, or serious infection. ([PubMed][3])

For example, Bowman et al. found a 29% reduction in overall utilization and fewer units per admission, but more transfusion episodes—4.1 versus 2.7—and a lower discharge hemoglobin—8.9 versus 9.5 g/dL. Length of stay, 30-day mortality, readmission, outpatient transfusion, and recorded transfusion reactions were similar. ([PMC][4])

**Adult interpretation:** For a stable, nonbleeding adult, starting with one unit and reassessing reduces unnecessary second units and lowers immediate exposure. The principal tradeoff is a smaller hemoglobin rise and potentially more reassessments or separate transfusion episodes. The available studies have not shown a patient-important benefit from automatically administering two units, but they are not strong enough to rule out modest differences in rare adverse events.

This conclusion should not be extrapolated to active hemorrhage, hemodynamic instability, acute massive blood loss, exchange transfusion, or situations in which rapid achievement of a specific hematocrit is required.

### Children with severe anemia

The pediatric studies were conducted in sub-Saharan Africa among children with profound anemia, frequently related to malaria or other infections. They administered whole blood or the equivalent packed-RBC dose: approximately **20 versus 30 mL/kg whole blood**, corresponding to about **10 versus 15 mL/kg packed RBCs**.

A phase II randomized trial of 160 Ugandan children found that 30 mL/kg corrected severe anemia by 24 hours more frequently than 20 mL/kg—90% versus 74%. Fewer children appeared to meet criteria for repeat transfusion—5% versus 15%—and no increase in adverse or fatal events was detected, although the trial was too small for reliable mortality conclusions. ([PMC][5])

The subsequent TRACT randomized trial included 3,196 children. Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% with 30 mL/kg and 4.5% with 20 mL/kg, hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.08; therefore, the overall difference was not statistically conclusive. Additional transfusions were less common after the larger dose—12.3% versus 18.8%. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

TRACT found a notable treatment interaction with fever at presentation:

* Without fever, mortality was lower with 30 mL/kg: HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.27–0.69.
* With fever, mortality was higher with 30 mL/kg: HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.04–3.49.

Readmissions, serious adverse events, and hemoglobin recovery at 180 days did not differ overall. Because this fever interaction was a subgroup finding within a factorial trial, it warrants caution and external replication rather than automatic bedside application. ([New England Journal of Medicine][6])

**Pediatric interpretation:** A larger dose corrects profound anemia faster and reduces repeat transfusion, but does not have a uniform survival advantage. The TRACT fever interaction raises the possibility that the optimal volume depends on underlying infection, inflammatory state, or cardiovascular physiology. These results may not generalize to children hospitalized in high-resource settings or to less profound anemia.

### Preterm and very-low-birth-weight infants

Across neonatal randomized studies, 20 mL/kg consistently produced a larger hemoglobin and hematocrit increment than 10 or 15 mL/kg.

Paul et al. found post-transfusion hemoglobin of 14.2 versus 12.0 g/dL and hematocrit of 41.2% versus 32.3% after 20 versus 10 mL/kg, without detected differences in measured pulmonary function. ([PubMed][7])

In a 20-infant trial, Wong et al. found a nonsignificant reduction in the mean number of transfusions—2.8 with 20 mL/kg versus 3.4 with 15 mL/kg—and no adverse events attributed to the larger volume. ([PubMed][8])

In a randomized crossover study of 22 very-low-birth-weight infants, Mallett et al. found higher post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit after 20 versus 15 mL/kg, with no detectable difference in tachypnea, hepatomegaly, edema, hypoxia, vital-sign instability, or necrotizing enterocolitis. ([PubMed][9])

A 39-infant hemodynamic trial found reduced cardiac output and transient BNP elevations after transfusion in both the 15- and 20-mL/kg groups, but no significant between-group difference. This suggests that transfusion itself alters neonatal hemodynamics, while the additional 5 mL/kg did not clearly worsen those changes in stable infants. ([PubMed][10])

A larger randomized study of 151 preterm infants found that 20 versus 15 mL/kg produced a somewhat greater hemoglobin/hematocrit rise, fewer total transfusions—1.9 versus 2.6—and a shorter hospitalization—44.3 versus 47.6 days. Cerebral and intestinal oxygenation responses and neonatal complication rates did not differ detectably. ([PubMed][11])

A formula-based dosing trial in 68 preterm infants produced a larger administered volume and greater hematocrit increment than fixed 15-mL/kg dosing. ROP, BPD, IVH, NEC, and mortality were similar, but the trial was much too small to establish safety for these outcomes. ([PubMed][12])

Two-center observational data are less reassuring about the assumption that a larger dose necessarily means fewer transfusions. Among 459 premature infants, 20 mL/kg reduced transfusion episodes by approximately one per infant in a more mature gestational-age subgroup, but site-level differences in transfusion triggers substantially confounded the comparison; composite BPD, ROP, IVH, and mortality outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][13]) A follow-up of extremely preterm infants found no association between total neonatal transfusion volume and death or major neurodevelopmental impairment at approximately 24 months. ([PMC][14])

**Neonatal interpretation:** Larger volumes reliably increase hemoglobin more and may reduce repeat transfusions. Short-term physiologic studies have not demonstrated clear volume-overload harm in stable preterm infants, but most trials are far too small to evaluate mortality, NEC, BPD, ROP, IVH, TACO, or long-term neurodevelopment with confidence.

## Practical evidence summary

| Question                                                                                      | What the primary studies suggest                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Does a larger dose raise hemoglobin more?                                                     | Yes, consistently.                                                                                         |
| Does a smaller adult dose reduce RBC use?                                                     | Usually, especially when it prevents an automatic second unit.                                             |
| Does one-unit dosing always reduce total transfusions in highly transfusion-dependent adults? | No. It may increase the number of episodes, and one hematology RCT found little difference in total units. |
| Does a larger pediatric/neonatal dose reduce repeat transfusion?                              | Often, but not in every study.                                                                             |
| Is mortality lower with either strategy?                                                      | No consistent overall difference has been established.                                                     |
| Are TACO, TRALI, thrombosis, infection, or rare serious reactions adequately studied?         | No. Most studies were underpowered or did not systematically report these outcomes.                        |
| Most defensible adult application                                                             | One unit followed by clinical and hemoglobin reassessment in stable, nonbleeding patients.                 |
| Pediatric/neonatal application                                                                | Individualize; evidence is setting-specific and insufficient for a universally optimal mL/kg dose.         |
