I'll search for primary studies on this specific topic — the volume/amount of red blood cells given per transfusion episode (e.g., single-unit vs. multi-unit transfusion protocols), as distinct from the hemoglobin-threshold ("restrictive vs. liberal") literature.Based on your phrasing — "the amount of red blood cells given **during each transfusion**" — I focused on studies comparing **single-unit vs. multiple-unit (usually two-unit) transfusion episodes**. This is a distinct research question from the "restrictive vs. liberal" hemoglobin-*threshold* literature (which asks *when* to transfuse, not how much to give per episode), so I kept those out unless a study also manipulated per-episode dose. All studies below are primary research (RCTs or original cohort studies) — no reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, or commentaries.

**What the evidence shows so far:**
- Across hematology/oncology populations, single-unit transfusion protocols consistently cut total RBC units used (roughly 20–25% reduction) without clear evidence of harm (survival, bleeding, ICU transfer, infection were generally similar between strategies).
- In postpartum anemia, a single-unit-first protocol resulted in lower post-transfusion hemoglobin but no difference in symptoms, length of stay, or 30-day complications.
- The largest RCT designed specifically to test this (the French "1versus2" trial in leukemia/transplant patients) found single-unit transfusion **non-inferior** on its composite safety endpoint, though it did not reduce total units transfused as hoped.
- Most other supporting studies are retrospective/observational cohort comparisons of "before vs. after" policy changes, which is a weaker design than a randomized trial (susceptible to confounding by other practice changes over time, and to detection/reporting differences) — worth keeping in mind when weighing the strength of the "no harm" conclusion.
- Very little data exist outside hematology/oncology and obstetrics — I found essentially no dedicated single-vs-multi-unit dose trials in general medical/surgical inpatients, cardiac surgery, or pediatrics; most guidance for those settings is extrapolated from the threshold trials rather than studied directly.

Given how narrow this literature is, a broader systematic search (including trial registries, non-English sources, and citation-tracking) could surface additional studies, especially unpublished protocols or pediatric data. Let me know if you'd like me to go deeper.
