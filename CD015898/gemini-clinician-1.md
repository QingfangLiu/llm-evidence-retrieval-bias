Historically, ordering two units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) was the standard approach for treating anemia in hospitalized patients. However, patient blood management strategies have increasingly shifted toward a "transfuse one and reassess" protocol to avoid unnecessary exposure to allogeneic blood.

Based on primary clinical studies evaluating the dosage and volume of RBC transfusions in hemodynamically stable patients, here is a breakdown of the benefits and harms of smaller (single-unit) versus larger (multi-unit) transfusions.

### Benefits of Smaller Transfusion Amounts (Single-Unit Strategy)

* **Reduced Exposure to Allogeneic Blood:** The primary benefit of a smaller transfusion dose is minimizing the patient's exposure to donor blood, which inherently carries risks. In a pragmatic randomized controlled trial of hemodynamically stable women with postpartum anemia, utilizing a single-unit transfusion protocol successfully avoided the administration of a second RBC unit in over 80% of the patients (Hamm et al., 2021).
* **Conservation of Blood Resources:** Limiting transfusions to a single unit significantly reduces overall hospital blood consumption. In a retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing intensive chemotherapy or stem cell transplantation, shifting from a double-unit to a single-unit policy reduced the total number of RBC units used per therapy cycle by 25% (Berger et al., 2011).
* **Maintained Clinical Safety:** Avoiding the second unit does not appear to compromise patient outcomes. The single-unit approach in the postpartum cohort showed no significant negative impact on maternal morbidity (Hamm et al., 2021). Similarly, in the hematology-oncology cohort, the single-unit policy did not lead to an increase in severe bleeding events, a higher need for out-patient transfusions, or worse overall survival (Berger et al., 2011).

### Harms and Considerations of Smaller Amounts

* **Lower Maintained Hemoglobin Levels:** Because the volume of red cells is smaller, patients managed with a single-unit strategy may sustain slightly lower pre-transfusion hemoglobin levels throughout their hospitalization. For example, hematology patients managed under a single-unit policy experienced a lower median pre-transfusion hemoglobin (61 g/L) compared to those in the double-unit period (64 g/L) (Berger et al., 2011).
* **Increased Reassessment Burden:** A single-unit strategy relies on a workflow of transfusing one unit and then drawing new labs to reassess the patient's hemoglobin and clinical symptoms. This requires more frequent monitoring than a larger, corrective dose given all at once.

### Benefits and Harms of Larger Amounts (Multiple-Unit Strategy)

* **Benefits:** Transfusing two or more units provides a larger, more rapid increase in hemoglobin and hematocrit. This larger dose is typically reserved for actively bleeding patients or those with severe, highly symptomatic ischemia where a single unit would be physiologically insufficient.
* **Harms:** The primary harm of a multi-unit strategy in a stable, non-bleeding patient is over-transfusion. Delivering a larger volume than necessary exposes the patient to an increased risk of volume overload (Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload, or TACO) and alloimmunization without offering any additional survival or recovery benefit (Berger et al., 2011).
