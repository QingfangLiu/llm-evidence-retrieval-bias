## Scope

I treated “amount during each transfusion” as the **dose given after the decision to transfuse has already been made**:

* Adults: usually **one versus two red-cell units per transfusion event**
* Children and neonates: different weight-based doses, such as **10, 15, 20, or 30 mL/kg**

I excluded studies that only compared hemoglobin thresholds for deciding **when** to transfuse. All evidence cited below comes from individual primary studies, not reviews, guidelines, editorials, or committee statements.

## Main findings

### 1. Stable adults who are not actively bleeding

The most directly applicable adult evidence supports giving **one unit first and then reassessing**, rather than automatically giving two units.

In 66 hemodynamically stable postpartum patients, starting with one unit resulted in an average of 1.2 units compared with 2.1 units when two were initially ordered. Only 18% of the one-unit group required another unit. Their post-transfusion hemoglobin was lower—7.8 versus 8.7 g/dL—but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, fatigue, breastfeeding, depression, and maternal bonding did not differ. Thus, more than 80% avoided an unnecessary second unit. ([PubMed][1])

A multicenter randomized trial of 245 adults receiving intensive chemotherapy or stem-cell transplantation found that a one-unit policy was noninferior to a two-unit policy for a composite of severe complications, intensive-care admission, or death. However, it did **not clearly reduce the total number of units used during the admission**, probably because these patients needed repeated transfusions over prolonged periods. ([ScienceDirect][2])

A 91-patient randomized pilot trial in acute myeloid leukemia separately randomized patients to one versus two units per event and to different transfusion thresholds. The number of units given per event did not significantly change total red-cell use, bleeding, or leukemia-treatment outcomes. The trial was relatively small and designed mainly to establish feasibility. ([PubMed][3])

Several before-and-after hematology studies found that switching to one unit reduced total red-cell use by approximately 25%–32%, without detected differences in survival, serious bleeding, length of stay, readmission, or short-term mortality. Some also found **more separate transfusion episodes** and a slightly lower discharge hemoglobin under the one-unit policy. These nonrandomized findings are less certain because clinical practice may have changed in other ways over time. ([PubMed][4])

One hematology study changed both the dose and the hemoglobin threshold at the same time—two units at hemoglobin ≤8 g/dL became one unit at ≤7 g/dL. Red-cell use fell without detected increases in inpatient death, serious bleeding, ICU admission, falls, or length of stay, but the effects of the smaller dose cannot be separated from those of the lower threshold. ([PubMed][5])

**Adult interpretation:** For a selected, stable, nonbleeding adult, a one-unit-first approach commonly avoids the second unit and leaves the patient with a somewhat lower hemoglobin, without evidence so far of worse short-term clinical outcomes. It may require another assessment or transfusion later. The trials were not large enough to rule out small differences in uncommon adverse reactions.

### 2. Children

The strongest pediatric evidence is the TRACT randomized trial, which included 3,196 children aged 2 months to 12 years in Uganda and Malawi with severe anemia and clinical severity features. It compared **30 versus 20 mL/kg of whole-blood equivalent**.

Overall 28-day mortality was 3.4% with the larger dose and 4.5% with the smaller dose; this difference was not statistically conclusive. Additional transfusion was less common with the larger dose: 12.3% versus 18.8%. ([PubMed][6])

However, there was an important interaction with fever at enrollment:

* Among children **without fever**, mortality was lower with 30 mL/kg: hazard ratio 0.43.
* Among children **with fever**, mortality was higher with 30 mL/kg: hazard ratio 1.91.
* Overall serious adverse events, readmissions, and long-term hemoglobin recovery did not differ.

This result is highly context-specific: most children had malaria, all had severe anemia, and treatment occurred in sub-Saharan African hospitals. It should not be automatically generalized to children receiving transfusions for surgery, cancer, trauma, or chronic anemia elsewhere. ([PubMed][6])

### 3. Premature and very-low-birth-weight infants

Small randomized neonatal studies consistently show that a larger dose produces a **larger immediate rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit**.

A randomized trial comparing 20 with 10 mL/kg found post-transfusion hemoglobin levels of 14.2 versus 12.0 g/dL and no measured difference in pulmonary function. ([PubMed][7])

Trials comparing 20 with 15 mL/kg generally did not detect more short-term respiratory instability, edema, hypoxia, necrotizing enterocolitis, abnormal vital signs, or adverse cardiac effects with the larger dose. Nevertheless, most included only 20–39 infants and were far too small to establish the safety of uncommon complications. ([PubMed][8])

A newer randomized study of 151 premature infants found that 20 rather than 15 mL/kg produced a slightly greater hemoglobin/hematocrit rise, fewer total transfusions—1.9 versus 2.6—and a hospital stay approximately three days shorter, without detected increases in neonatal complications. These potentially important findings need independent replication. ([PubMed][9])

A randomized trial comparing a fixed 15 mL/kg dose with a formula-calculated dose found a substantially larger average volume in the formula group and a greater hematocrit rise. Major neonatal morbidities and death were similar, but the study of 68 infants was not powered to determine whether retransfusions or uncommon harms differed. ([PubMed][10])

Two-center observational studies comparing 15 with 20 mL/kg did not find better neonatal complications or two-year developmental outcomes with the larger volume. Interpretation is difficult because the centers differed in how often and at what hemoglobin levels they transfused. ([PubMed][11])

## Benefits and tradeoffs supported by the studies

**Potential advantages of a smaller amount**

* Avoids an automatically administered second adult unit that may not be needed.
* Usually lowers total red-cell use in adult before-and-after studies.
* Gives less transfused volume at that particular event.
* Permits reassessment of symptoms and hemoglobin response before giving more blood.

**Potential disadvantages of a smaller amount**

* Smaller rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit.
* Some patients need another unit after reassessment.
* May produce more separate transfusion episodes, particularly during prolonged cancer treatment.
* In severely anemic children, the smaller dose led to more additional transfusions, and among afebrile children in TRACT it was associated with higher mortality than the larger dose.

**Potential advantages of a larger amount**

* Greater and more sustained hemoglobin/hematocrit increase.
* Fewer repeat transfusions in the large pediatric trial and in some neonatal studies.
* Could reduce repeated procedures in selected infants or children.

**Potential disadvantages of a larger amount**

* More donor blood is administered before determining whether the entire amount is needed.
* In febrile children with severe anemia in TRACT, the higher dose was associated with increased mortality.
* Concerns such as circulatory or volume overload remain biologically plausible, particularly in small infants or people with heart or kidney disease, but the dose-comparison trials were too small to quantify these uncommon harms reliably.

## Practical conclusion

For **stable, nonbleeding adults**, the most consistent finding is that giving **one unit and reassessing** reduces unnecessary blood use and generally produces similar measured short-term outcomes to routinely giving two units. This conclusion should not be extended to major ongoing bleeding, hemodynamic instability, massive transfusion, or other emergencies, because those patients were not the population studied.

For **children**, the answer appears more dependent on the illness and clinical condition. TRACT demonstrated that a higher dose can have opposite effects depending on fever status.

For **premature infants**, larger doses reliably raise hemoglobin more and may reduce subsequent transfusions, but evidence on mortality, major complications, and long-term development remains limited.
