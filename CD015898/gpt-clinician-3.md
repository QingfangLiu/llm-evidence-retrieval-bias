## Scope

I treated “amount” as the **number of RBC units or weight-based volume given during one transfusion episode after the decision to transfuse had already been made**. I excluded studies that only compared hemoglobin thresholds, exchange transfusion, massive-hemorrhage protocols, and non-primary publications.

## Main findings

### Stable, nonbleeding adults

The direct randomized evidence generally supports **giving one unit and reassessing rather than routinely giving two units**:

* In hospitalized adults with hematologic malignancy or stem-cell transplantation, one-unit dosing was noninferior to two-unit dosing for a composite of severe adverse events, ICU admission, or death. It produced a similar discharge hemoglobin, but did **not clearly reduce total RBC use over the entire hospitalization**—median seven versus eight units per patient. ([ScienceDirect][1])
* A smaller factorial RCT in acute myeloid leukemia likewise found no material effect of one versus two units per episode on total RBC use, bleeding, or treatment outcomes. ([PubMed][2])
* In hemodynamically stable postpartum anemia, an initial one-unit strategy reduced mean exposure from 2.1 to 1.2 units. Only 18% required another unit. Post-transfusion hemoglobin was approximately 0.9 g/dL lower, but symptoms, vital signs, length of stay, 30-day complications, fatigue, breastfeeding, and postpartum patient-reported outcomes were similar. ([PubMed][3])

Multiple before-and-after and cohort studies found reductions in RBC utilization after adoption of single-unit policies. Reductions ranged from modest to approximately 25%–50%, depending on the population and study design. ([PMC][4])

However, single-unit policies can produce:

* A lower immediate or discharge hemoglobin.
* More separate transfusion episodes, laboratory checks, and reassessments.
* Little or no reduction in total units when patients remain transfusion-dependent for prolonged chemotherapy or transplantation.

For example, one hematologic-malignancy cohort found fewer units per admission but more transfusion episodes—4.1 versus 2.7—and a lower discharge hemoglobin, without detectable differences in mortality, length of stay, readmission, reactions, or outpatient transfusion. ([PMC][5])

**Adult interpretation:** For a stable, nonbleeding adult without an immediate need for a large hemoglobin increment, one unit followed by clinical and hemoglobin reassessment reduces unnecessary second units without evidence of worse common clinical outcomes. The studies were not large enough to exclude uncommon harms such as transfusion-associated circulatory overload, myocardial ischemia, thrombosis, or serious transfusion reactions.

### Children with severe anemia

The largest dose trial was the African TRACT trial, enrolling 3,196 children aged two months to 12 years with severe anemia. It compared:

* **Higher volume:** 30 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent, generally 15 mL/kg packed cells.
* **Lower volume:** 20 mL/kg whole-blood equivalent, generally 10 mL/kg packed cells.

Higher-volume transfusion:

* Produced an approximately 1 g/dL greater hemoglobin increase at 48 hours.
* Reduced additional transfusion from approximately 19% to 12%.
* Slightly accelerated hospital discharge.
* Did not significantly reduce overall 28-day mortality: 3.4% versus 4.5%, hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.08.
* Did not significantly change overall serious adverse events. ([PMC][6])

An important subgroup interaction was observed. Higher volume was associated with lower mortality among children without fever but higher mortality among children with fever at presentation. Because this was a subgroup finding, it should not automatically be applied to other pediatric populations, but it makes routine escalation to the larger volume concerning in febrile children in similar settings. ([PMC][6])

**Pediatric interpretation:** Larger weight-based volumes more reliably correct anemia and reduce repeat transfusion, but the trial did not establish an overall survival benefit and raised a possible fever-dependent safety concern. Applicability to well-resourced hospitals, chronic anemia, cardiac disease, neonates, or causes of anemia other than those common in sub-Saharan Africa is uncertain.

### Preterm and very-low-birth-weight infants

Neonatal trials predominantly compared **20 mL/kg with 10 or 15 mL/kg**.

Across the studies, 20 mL/kg usually produced a larger hemoglobin or hematocrit increment. The effect on later transfusion requirements has been inconsistent:

* A 20-infant trial of 20 versus 10 mL/kg found a higher post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit with 20 mL/kg, but no difference in measured pulmonary function. ([PubMed][7])
* A 20-infant trial of 20 versus 15 mL/kg found no significant reduction in the number of transfusions and no attributable adverse events. ([PubMed][8])
* A randomized crossover study of 22 very-low-birth-weight infants found higher post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit with 20 mL/kg but no difference in short-term respiratory, hemodynamic, edema, hypoxia, or necrotizing-enterocolitis outcomes. ([PMC][9])
* A 39-infant hemodynamic trial found transient cardiac-output and BNP changes after transfusion in both dose groups, without significant differences between 15 and 20 mL/kg. ([PubMed][10])
* The largest neonatal dose study randomized 151 preterm infants to 15 or 20 mL/kg. Twenty mL/kg produced slightly higher hemoglobin and hematocrit, fewer total transfusions—1.9 versus 2.6—and a roughly three-day shorter hospitalization, without a reported increase in complications. Cerebral and intestinal oxygenation responses were similar between groups. ([PubMed][11])
* A trial comparing fixed 15 mL/kg dosing with a larger formula-calculated dose found a greater hematocrit increment with formula dosing, but no clear reduction in retransfusion or donor exposure. ([PMC][12])

**Neonatal interpretation:** A larger dose produces a larger hematologic increment and may reduce subsequent transfusions, but the evidence remains insufficient to determine effects on mortality, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, severe intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, retinopathy, or long-term neurodevelopment. Most studies were single-center and too small to evaluate uncommon volume-related harms.

## Practical evidence summary

| Situation                                | Smaller amount                                                                                                             | Larger amount                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stable nonbleeding adult                 | Less RBC exposure per episode; similar measured clinical outcomes; requires reassessment and sometimes another transfusion | Higher immediate hemoglobin; fewer separate episodes, but routinely exposes some patients to an unnecessary second unit    |
| Transfusion-dependent hematology patient | Safe in available trials; total hospitalization-level savings may be small                                                 | May reduce episode frequency but has not improved major outcomes                                                           |
| Child with severe anemia                 | More repeat transfusions and smaller hemoglobin increase                                                                   | Better anemia correction and fewer repeat transfusions; no overall mortality benefit and possible harm in febrile subgroup |
| Stable preterm infant                    | Smaller hemoglobin/hematocrit increment; possibly more later transfusions                                                  | Larger increment and possibly fewer transfusions; major clinical benefits and rare harms remain uncertain                  |

These conclusions should **not** be extrapolated to active hemorrhage, rapidly evolving hemodynamic instability, massive transfusion, exchange transfusion, or situations where a predetermined large hemoglobin increment is urgently required.
