Dealing with hip osteoarthritis can be challenging, but looking into exercise is a fantastic step. Strengthening the muscles around the joint is one of the most effective ways to manage symptoms and protect your mobility.

To answer your question directly using only primary studies (specifically randomized controlled trials, or RCTs) and excluding reviews: yes, clinical evidence shows that land-based therapeutic exercise generally reduces pain and improves physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis.

Here is what the individual clinical trials reveal about how it helps.

## How Exercise Impacts Pain

Structured exercise programs consistently lead to meaningful reductions in self-reported pain. By strengthening the deep stabilizing muscles of the pelvis and buttocks, the joint is better supported, which absorbs shock and reduces painful friction.

A 12-week supervised land-based exercise trial for older adults demonstrated a significant pain decline of over 30% from the participants' baseline (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017).

## Improvements in Physical Function

Exercise directly translates to better joint mechanics and everyday mobility, though the exact gains depend on the specific program.

* **Strength and Range of Motion:** Objective measurements following 12 weeks of targeted exercise have shown significant improvements in maximal leg extensor strength (by 20%) and hip extension range of motion (by 30%) (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017).
* **Walking and Everyday Tasks:** In another pilot trial, patients engaging in a structured hip-specific resistance and aerobic program doubled their median walking distance on a 6-minute walk test. They also showed greater improvements in self-reported physical function compared to a control group, although the study's small sample size limited statistical significance (Thompson et al., 2019).

## Does the Type of Exercise Matter?

The most important factor seems to be consistency and progressing the intensity over time, rather than the exact type of land-based routine.

A recent large-scale cluster RCT compared high-intensity progressive resistance training (PRT) against neuromuscular exercise (NEMEX), which focuses on sensorimotor control and stability. After 12 weeks, both groups saw similar, meaningful improvements in functional performance (measured by the 30-second chair stand test), pain relief, and hip-related quality of life, with neither being clearly superior to the other (Kjeldsen et al., 2024).
