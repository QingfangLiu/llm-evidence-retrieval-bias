## Land-based exercise for hip osteoarthritis: primary study findings

Based on my search, here's what the primary randomized trials on this question show. Most compare a land-based (non-aquatic) exercise program — usually strength training, sometimes combined with education or manual therapy — against a no-exercise or usual-care control, in adults with radiographically or clinically confirmed hip OA.

**General pattern across trials:**
- Several trials show modest but real reductions in pain and improvements in physical function with land-based exercise, particularly strength/resistance-based programs (e.g., a 12-week exercise therapy program significantly reduced pain compared to control, with a mean difference of 5.7 points, and also improved physical function, though it didn't significantly change general health-related quality of life).
- A trial testing manual therapy plus home exercise against sham treatment found benefits on pain and function outcomes over 12 weeks with continued gains through 24-week follow-up in a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 102 community volunteers with hip pain and radiographically confirmed osteoarthritis.
- An early trial of an 8-week strength-training and lifestyle-advice program for older adults with hip OA evaluated outcomes including pain, hip function, disability, quality of life, and BMI, contributing to the evidence base that structured exercise programs can modify multiple OA-related outcomes, not just pain.
- One trial comparing Nordic walking, strength training, and home-based exercise found that Nordic Walking was superior to strength training and home-based exercise for improving function in people with hip osteoarthritis, with a companion paper from the same trial examining exercise-induced effects on muscle function and range of motion in hip OA patients.
- A pragmatic, two-year trial of supervised exercise plus GP care versus GP care alone in 120 adults aged 55–80 with hip OA evaluated the short- and long-term effectiveness of exercise training on pain, function, and healthcare costs.
- Longer-term follow-up work on one hip OA exercise cohort found that small-to-medium beneficial long-term effects persisted across outcome groups, though short-term between-group differences were only statistically significant for WOMAC function in one comparison, with adherence rates helping explain some of the differences.
- A trial testing whether exercise could delay the need for surgery found that exercise therapy may postpone total hip replacement surgery in patients with hip osteoarthritis, based on long-term follow-up of a randomized trial — relevant to "joint function" in the broadest structural/disease-progression sense, though most trials focus on pain/WOMAC-function scores rather than surgical outcomes.
- Not all approaches show clear benefit: a trial testing internet-based pain coping skills training as an add-on before a standard home exercise program found that online pain coping skills training improved pain coping and function early on but did not add extra benefit once the subsequent exercise program began, despite sustained improvements in pain coping — suggesting the exercise component itself was the active ingredient, not the psychological adjunct.

Overall, individual trials generally support a real but modest benefit of land-based exercise for pain in hip OA, with more mixed and smaller effects on self-reported physical function — consistent with what you'd expect going into this (small-to-moderate effect sizes, heterogeneous programs, and hip OA being understudied relative to knee OA in exercise trials).

Note: I excluded systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guideline documents as instructed. Two protocol-only papers (describing planned-but-not-yet-reported trials) are noted separately since they aren't yet studies with results.
