Based on primary randomized controlled trials, land-based exercise—including progressive resistance training, neuromuscular exercise, and aerobic regimens—is generally safe and associated with short-to-medium-term improvements in both pain and physical function for adults with hip osteoarthritis (OA), though the magnitude and durability of these effects can vary.

## Effects on Pain

Trials frequently demonstrate clinically meaningful reductions in self-reported pain following structured land-based interventions (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017). When evaluating specific modalities, progressive resistance training and neuromuscular exercises (which focus on balance and postural control) both yield significant pain relief, with neither proving definitively superior to the other (Kjeldsen et al., 2024).

However, it is worth noting that some long-term data suggest the addition of exercise therapy might not provide substantial additional pain reduction over robust patient education alone at extended follow-ups (Svege et al., 2016).

## Effects on Physical Function

Functional capacity generally sees notable gains following land-based exercise. Objective physical performance measures—such as leg extension power, 30-second chair stand tests, and range of motion—consistently improve following 12-week exercise interventions (Kjeldsen et al., 2024; Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017). Even in smaller pilot trials where between-group differences for functional capacity did not reach strict statistical significance, observable positive trends favored the land-based exercise groups over waitlist controls (Thompson et al., 2019).

In summary, prescribing structured, land-based exercises is a defensible and broadly effective primary intervention for reducing pain and improving functional capacity in the short-to-medium term.
