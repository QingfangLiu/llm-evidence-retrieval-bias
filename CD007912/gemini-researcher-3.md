Yes, primary studies demonstrate that land-based exercise therapies can reduce pain and improve joint function in adults with hip osteoarthritis (OA). However, the magnitude of these effects depends heavily on adherence, exercise dosing, and long-term follow-up.

## Key Findings from Randomized Controlled Trials

Primary research on land-based exercise—specifically strength, aerobic, and functional training—highlights several consistent clinical outcomes for patients with hip OA:

* **Improved Physical Function:** Structured aerobic and resistance exercise programs have been shown to yield clinically meaningful improvements in physical function. Even in smaller trials, structured interventions consistently lead to functional gains, such as increased 6-minute walk distances and better scores on standardized physical function indices (Thompson et al., 2019).
* **Reduced Pain and Symptoms:** Both short-term and long-term trials indicate that land-based exercise reduces self-reported pain in the hip. However, researchers note that maintaining these pain-relief benefits usually requires ongoing adherence to the exercise regimen and behavioral modifications (Pisters et al., 2010).
* **Delayed Need for Surgery:** One of the most significant clinical benefits of supervised exercise therapy is its impact on disease progression. Long-term follow-up of randomized controlled trials shows that participating in a 12-week exercise therapy program, when combined with patient education, can significantly increase the survival time of the native hip and substantially postpone the need for total hip replacement surgery compared to patient education alone (Svege et al., 2015).

While exercise is broadly beneficial, individualized programs that are adequately dosed to meet standard cardiovascular and resistance training guidelines tend to produce the most reliable and sustained improvements in functional outcomes.
