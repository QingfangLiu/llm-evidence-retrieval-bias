## Bottom line

Individual randomized trials suggest that **land-based exercise can produce small improvements in pain and physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the results are inconsistent**. Function appears somewhat more likely to improve than pain. Several trials found benefits only immediately after treatment or at selected follow-up points, while others found no advantage over education, usual care, wait-list control, or a credible sham intervention. ([PubMed][1])

This is a targeted map of primary studies available through **July 26, 2026**, rather than a formal PRISMA-complete systematic search. I excluded reviews, guidelines, editorials, aquatic-only programs, and routine postoperative rehabilitation.

### Interpretation of the direct trials

The most consistent pattern is:

* **Pain:** benefits were demonstrated in some trials, particularly Tak and Krauß, but not in Fernandes, Juhakoski, Teirlinck over the full follow-up, Thompson, or the sham-controlled Bennell trial.
* **Function:** improvements occurred more often, but were generally small, transient, confined to selected outcome measures, or imprecisely estimated.
* **Comparator matters:** effects tended to look more favorable against wait-list or no-treatment controls than against education, active usual care, or a credible sham. ([PubMed][1])

## Severe or pre-arthroplasty hip OA

These studies apply primarily to people with advanced disease or already listed for hip replacement.

**Villadsen et al., 2014** found that eight weeks of neuromuscular exercise plus education improved activities of daily living, pain, and performance compared with education in people awaiting hip or knee replacement. The hip subgroup had a moderate standardized effect on HOOS ADL, but this was a secondary subgroup analysis. ([PubMed][9])

**Hermann et al., 2016** randomized 80 adults awaiting total hip arthroplasty to ten weeks of supervised explosive resistance training or usual care. HOOS function improved 10.0 points more with training, 95% CI 4.7–15.3, and the other HOOS subscales, including pain, also favored training. ([OARS-I Journal][10])

However, in people already considered surgical candidates, exercise was substantially less effective than total hip arthroplasty:

* In **PROHIP**, resistance training improved the Oxford Hip Score by 4.5 points at six months, compared with 15.9 points after hip replacement. Twenty-one percent of the exercise group underwent surgery within six months. ([New England Journal of Medicine][11])
* In **Talonpoika et al., 2026**, the self-directed exercise group improved by only 3.7 WOMAC points and 5.7 Harris Hip Score points at 12 months; hip replacement produced much larger improvements, and 38% of the exercise group underwent surgery during follow-up. ([PubMed][12])

These surgery-comparator trials do not test exercise against no exercise, but indicate that exercise alone may provide limited symptom relief for many people with moderate-to-severe or end-stage disease.

## Does one type of land-based exercise work better?

Head-to-head trials do not identify a consistently superior program:

* **Nordic walking:** Bieler et al. found better performance on several functional tests than strength training or unsupervised home exercise, although chair-stand improvements were similar between groups immediately after treatment. ([PubMed][13])
* **Progressive resistance vs neuromuscular exercise:** both produced similar chair-stand and HOOS pain improvements; progressive resistance was not superior. ([PubMed][14])
* **Adding aerobic activity to resistance exercise:** PHOENIX found improvements in both groups, but adding aerobic activity did not improve pain or function more than resistance exercise alone. ([Monash University][15])
* **Cycling plus education:** CLEAT found a 6.9-point greater improvement in HOOS activities of daily living than usual physiotherapy, although this narrowly missed the trial’s prespecified 7.4-point minimum clinically important difference. ([ScienceDirect][16])
* High- versus low-dose exercise and high- versus low-velocity resistance programs found no clear between-program difference. ([PubMed][17])

## Mixed hip-and-knee studies

Several trials enrolled both hip and knee OA participants. Their overall findings may be relevant, but they should not be treated as hip-specific estimates when results were not reported separately.

* **van Baar et al., 1998:** exercise plus usual GP care improved pain and observed disability over 12 weeks in the combined hip/knee population. ([PubMed][18])
* **Hopman-Rock and Westhoff, 2000:** education plus exercise vs wait-list in older adults with hip or knee OA. ([TNO Repository][19])
* **Foley et al., 2003:** gym-based strengthening, hydrotherapy, or control in adults with hip or knee OA; the gym arm is land-based, but joint-specific estimates were limited. ([PubMed][20])
* **Abbott et al., 2013:** exercise added to usual care improved physical performance, but the WOMAC estimate was imprecise and the study combined hip and knee OA. ([PubMed][21])
* **Saw et al., 2016:** exercise and education improved pain severity and pain interference among people awaiting hip or knee arthroplasty, but no hip-specific subgroup results were reported. ([PMC][22])
* **Chopp-Hurley et al., 2017:** a small workplace trial reported improvements in pain and function in 24 employees with hip and/or knee OA, without a sufficiently large separate hip analysis. ([PubMed][23])
