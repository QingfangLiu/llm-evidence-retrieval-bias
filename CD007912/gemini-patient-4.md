Based on primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs), land-based exercise is generally effective at improving physical function and joint mobility for adults with hip osteoarthritis, though its impact on pain reduction can be more variable depending on the specific program.

Here is a breakdown of how land-based exercise impacts these two areas, drawing exclusively from individual primary studies.

## Impact on Physical Function

Across various trial designs, land-based interventions—typically involving a mix of aerobic conditioning, muscle strengthening, and flexibility exercises—consistently demonstrate functional benefits:

* **Improved Mobility and Self-Reported Function:** An 8-week program of exercise therapy significantly improved self-reported function, patient-perceived improvement, and hip range of motion (French et al., 2013).
* **Long-Term Functional Gains:** A pragmatic trial examining a mostly home-based exercise program (supported by general practitioners) found statistically significant improvements in physical functioning scores at both 6 months and 18 months (Juhakoski et al., 2010).
* **Functional Capacity in Daily Tasks:** A pilot RCT focusing on a 3-month hip-specific aerobic and resistance program showed that participants who exercised doubled their median walking distance on a 6-minute walk test compared to waitlist controls, indicating strong functional improvements (Thompson et al., 2019).
* **Workplace Performance:** When an exercise program was specifically implemented in the workplace for older workers with osteoarthritis, participants experienced significant improvements in overall physical function and work ability compared to those who did not exercise (Chopp-Hurley et al., 2017).

## Impact on Joint Pain

The evidence for pain reduction is mostly positive, but sometimes less pronounced than the functional improvements:

* **Significant Reductions:** The workplace-based intervention by Chopp-Hurley et al. (2017) demonstrated a significant reduction in self-reported pain symptoms alongside functional gains.
* **Mixed or Mild Reductions:** In contrast, the 2-year trial by Juhakoski et al. (2010) noted that while physical function improved, the exercise program did not result in a statistically significant reduction in hip pain over the extended follow-up period.
* **Complementary Pain Management:** Because exercise can sometimes temporarily provoke symptoms, some programs integrate psychological coping strategies. For example, the HOPE trial found that providing internet-based pain coping skills training prior to starting a home exercise program immediately improved how patients managed their pain, though it did not necessarily amplify the direct physical benefits of the exercise itself (Bennell et al., 2018).
