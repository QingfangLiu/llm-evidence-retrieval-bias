## Bottom line

**Land-based exercise can reduce pain and improve function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the benefit is inconsistent and generally modest.** Hip-specific trials against no treatment or a waiting list were more often positive. Trials using education, a credible sham intervention, or active physiotherapy as the comparator found smaller or no differences. Across the individual studies, improvement in **physical function appears somewhat more consistent than improvement in pain**. ([PubMed][1])

This was a targeted search for comparative primary studies through July 26, 2026, rather than a formal systematic review. I prioritized randomized trials involving adults with symptomatic hip OA and land-based strengthening, neuromuscular, aerobic, functional, or mixed exercise. Only primary studies are cited below.

### Interpretation of the direct trials

The positive findings are not universal. Krauß and Tak support benefits for both symptoms and function, while Fernandes and Juhakoski suggest that **function may improve even when pain does not**. Conversely, the placebo-controlled Bennell trial found no incremental benefit, and the Thompson pilot did not establish statistically significant efficacy. Differences in OA severity, exercise dose, adherence, comparator intensity, outcome measures, and co-interventions probably contribute to the divergent findings. ([PubMed][1])

## Exercise-to-exercise or other active-comparator trials

These trials help with program selection but **cannot establish exercise efficacy against no exercise**.

* **Hoeksma et al., 2004:** Manual therapy was superior to a five-week active-exercise program for perceived recovery, pain, stiffness, function, and range of motion. The success rate was 81% versus 50%. ([ACR Journals][8])
* **Østerås et al., 2017:** In a 33-person pilot, high-dose medical exercise was not superior to lower-dose exercise for pain or WOMAC function; both groups improved within groups. ([PubMed][9])
* **Kjeldsen et al., 2024:** Progressive resistance training and neuromuscular exercise produced similar improvements. Chair-stand performance improved by 1.5 repetitions in both groups, and HOOS pain improved by approximately 9 points in each. ([PubMed][10])
* **Hall et al., 2025, PHOENIX:** Adding moderate-intensity aerobic activity to resistance exercise did not improve pain or function more than resistance exercise alone. Both groups nevertheless had mean pain reductions of about 2.2–2.4/10 and WOMAC-function improvements of 7–9 points. ([PubMed][11])
* **Wainwright et al., 2025, CLEAT:** An eight-week cycling-and-education program improved HOOS activities of daily living more than usual physiotherapy by 6.9 points, but this narrowly missed the trial’s prespecified 7.4-point threshold for clinical importance. ([PubMed][12])

Collectively, these head-to-head studies have **not identified a clearly superior land-based exercise type**. Progressive resistance, neuromuscular, mixed strengthening/aerobic, and cycling programs are all reasonable studied approaches, but adding more exercise components or a higher dose has not reliably produced better outcomes. ([PubMed][9])

## Less directly applicable mixed hip-and-knee studies

* **van Baar et al., 1998; n=201:** In a combined hip/knee OA cohort, 12 weeks of physiotherapist-delivered exercise reduced pain by 17/100 points and produced a small improvement in observed disability versus GP care. Hip-specific results were not separately reported in the abstract. ([PubMed][13])
* **Abbott et al., 2013; n=206:** In a mixed hip/knee factorial trial, exercise plus usual care did not significantly improve total WOMAC at one year—the adjusted reduction was 16.4 points with a 95% CI crossing zero—but physical-performance outcomes favored exercise. ([PubMed][14])
* **Saw et al., 2016; n=74:** Among patients with advanced hip or knee OA awaiting arthroplasty, six weekly exercise-and-education sessions improved pain severity and pain interference through six months. No hip-only efficacy analysis was reported. ([Springer][15])

## Clinical synthesis

For an adult with symptomatic hip OA, the individual primary studies support presenting land-based exercise as a **reasonable therapeutic trial with a realistic expectation of small, variable improvement**, particularly in activity limitations and self-reported function. A lack of major pain reduction does not necessarily mean that function will not improve. An initial structured period of roughly 8–12 weeks is the duration most commonly tested, but the evidence does not establish one best exercise mode or dose. The stronger placebo-controlled evidence also indicates that improvement during treatment should not automatically be attributed entirely to the exercise itself. ([ScienceDirect][2])
