Dealing with hip osteoarthritis can be frustrating and painful, but looking into land-based exercise is a great step. The short answer is yes: primary research—specifically randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—shows that structured, land-based exercises like walking, strength training, and flexibility routines can effectively reduce pain and improve physical function.

Here is what the primary clinical studies tell us about how exercise impacts the hip:

## Improvements in Physical Function

Supervised exercise programs are particularly good at restoring mobility and function in the hip. A clinical trial comparing patient education alone to a combination of patient education and supervised exercise found that the combined exercise group saw significant improvements in their physical function over a 16-month period (Fernandes et al., 2010).

Similarly, a pilot trial focusing specifically on aerobic and strengthening exercises observed that after three months, participants in the exercise group doubled the distance they were able to walk during a 6-minute walk test compared to those who did not exercise (Thompson et al., 2019). This highlights that targeted strength and aerobic work translate directly to better real-world mobility.

## Reduction in Pain

While moving an arthritic joint might sound counterintuitive, it helps lubricate the joint and strengthen the surrounding muscles that support it. A 12-week pilot trial focusing on women over the age of 65 with hip osteoarthritis found that engaging in an exercise program reduced pain by over 30% from baseline. Alongside the pain relief, the participants experienced a 20% improvement in leg extensor strength and a 30% increase in their hip's range of motion (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017).

In another large-scale RCT, researchers evaluated the addition of multi-modal exercise therapy to standard care. The findings demonstrated that exercise therapy provided both symptom relief and physical performance benefits that were sustained at the one-year mark (Abbott et al., 2013).

## Delaying the Need for Surgery

Beyond daily pain and function, sticking to an exercise program can actively alter the long-term trajectory of osteoarthritis. In a long-term follow-up of a randomized trial, researchers tracked patients over six years to see if their interventions delayed the need for a total hip replacement (THR). They discovered that adding exercise therapy to standard patient education reduced the need for hip replacement surgery by 44%. For those who eventually did need surgery, the exercise group was able to delay the procedure to a median of 5.4 years, compared to just 3.5 years for the education-only group (Svege et al., 2015).

If you are considering starting a regimen, these studies suggest that aiming for a mix of strengthening (especially the glutes and thighs) and aerobic exercise (like walking or cycling) for at least 12 weeks yields the best functional results.
