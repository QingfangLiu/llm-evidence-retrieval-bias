## Bottom line

Across individual randomized trials, **land-based exercise sometimes reduces hip pain and improves physical function, but results are inconsistent and effects are generally modest**. The pattern depends strongly on the comparator:

* Against **waiting list, no treatment, usual care, or education alone**, several trials found improvements, particularly in self-reported or performance-based function.
* **Pain benefits were less consistent** and were often limited to the end of treatment or early follow-up.
* Against **sham treatment or another active physiotherapy program**, exercise-containing interventions frequently showed little or no additional benefit.
* Trials comparing exercise approaches have not established one clearly superior type, although Nordic walking and progressive resistance exercise may improve particular performance outcomes.

Here, “function” includes patient-reported activities and performance tests; these studies generally did not evaluate structural modification of the hip joint.

I treated publications from the same randomized cohort as one study and separated hip-only trials from studies that combined hip and knee osteoarthritis.

### Interpretation of the hip-only trials

The strongest practical signal is for **small improvements in function**, especially when structured exercise is compared with waiting list, minimal intervention, education alone, or usual care. Pain improvement occurs in some trials—particularly Tak, Krauß and the early Teirlinck assessment—but was absent in other sizable studies such as Fernandes, Juhakoski and Bennell.

The negative sham-controlled Bennell trial is particularly important: both active and sham groups improved, but the multimodal physical-therapy intervention did not outperform the credible sham. This suggests that estimates from untreated or waiting-list comparisons may partly reflect attention, expectations, natural symptom fluctuation or other nonspecific effects. ([JAMA Network][6])

## Trials combining hip and knee osteoarthritis

These are relevant but less definitive because most publications did not report a complete hip-only treatment estimate.

* **Van Baar et al., 1998:** Exercise added to GP care improved pain and observed disability in the combined hip/knee cohort; the reported main estimate was not hip-specific. ([PubMed][10])
* **Hopman-Rock and Westhoff, 2000:** A six-week education-and-exercise program improved pain, quality of life and quadriceps strength in the combined cohort, but not range of motion or functional tasks. ([Amsterdam UMC][11])
* **Foley et al., 2003:** Land-based gym strengthening improved quadriceps strength, walking speed and satisfaction/self-efficacy relative to control; participants had hip or knee OA, and the study also contained an aquatic-exercise arm. ([PubMed][12])
* **Fransen et al., 2007:** Land-based Tai Chi improved physical function versus waiting list, while the pain estimate was imprecise; findings combined hip and knee OA. ([Academic Credit Research][13])
* **Abbott et al., 2013:** In a factorial trial of exercise, manual therapy and usual care, exercise improved some performance outcomes, but the WOMAC treatment estimate was imprecise in the combined hip/knee cohort. ([ScienceDirect][14])
* **Bendrik et al., 2021:** Evaluated prescribed physical activity in adults with hip or knee OA; because outcomes were presented for a mixed cohort, applicability specifically to hip OA is limited. ([PubMed][15])

## Trials comparing exercise types rather than exercise versus nonexercise

These address program selection, not whether exercise itself works:

* **Fukumoto et al., 2014:** Compared high-velocity resistance exercise with a lower-velocity approach and evaluated muscle function and physical performance in hip OA. ([PubMed][16])
* **Bieler et al., 2017:** Nordic walking produced greater longer-term gains in several performance measures than supervised strength training or unsupervised home exercise, although not every functional outcome differed. ([PubMed][17])
* **Østerås et al., 2017:** A small pilot found no clear pain or function advantage of high-dose over low-dose medical exercise; both groups improved from baseline. ([PubMed][18])
* **Kjeldsen et al., 2024:** Progressive resistance training and neuromuscular exercise generated similar improvements; neither was superior for chair stands, HOOS pain or HOOS function. ([PubMed][19])
* **Hall et al., 2025—PHOENIX:** Adding aerobic activity to resistance exercise did not improve pain or function more than resistance exercise alone; both groups improved. ([ScienceDirect][20])

## Special population: moderate-to-severe disease

A 2026 trial compared total hip arthroplasty plus postoperative rehabilitation with a self-directed exercise program in adults with moderate-to-severe hip OA. The exercise group showed only small mean improvements at 12 months, while surgery produced substantially larger improvements in WOMAC, Harris Hip Score and performance tests; 38% of the exercise-assigned participants underwent arthroplasty during follow-up. Because there was no no-treatment comparator, this study does **not** establish that exercise was ineffective relative to usual care, but it indicates that exercise alone may be insufficient for many surgery-eligible patients. ([PubMed][21])
