The evidence from individual primary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) regarding land-based exercise for adults with hip osteoarthritis (OA) is surprisingly mixed. While clinical guidelines universally recommend exercise, much of that recommendation is extrapolated from robust knee OA data. When isolating the primary literature specifically evaluating hip OA, the studies show more modest or statistically insignificant benefits over control groups.

Here is a breakdown of key primary trial findings:

### 1. Studies Showing Potential Benefit

Some smaller pilot trials suggest that land-based exercise can offer short-term relief and functional gains, though statistical significance can be limited by sample size:

* **Significant Short-Term Gains:** A 12-week pilot trial evaluating a progressive, multi-directional exercise program found that participants experienced a significant >30% reduction in pain alongside a 20% improvement in maximal leg extensor strength and a 30% increase in hip extension range of motion (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017).
* **Clinically Meaningful but Non-Significant Trends:** Another pilot RCT evaluating a 3-month hip-specific aerobic and resistance program found that the exercise group doubled the median improvement of waitlist controls on both the 6-minute walk test and WOMAC physical function scores. However, due to the small sample size, these functional improvements did not reach statistical significance, and neither group saw a significant reduction in pain (Thompson et al., 2019).

### 2. High-Quality Trials Showing No Benefit Over Placebo

Larger, rigorously controlled trials have struggled to prove that structured exercise definitively outperforms sham treatments for hip OA:

* **Active vs. Sham Physical Therapy:** A high-quality, placebo-controlled RCT evaluated a 12-week multimodal physical therapy program (which included specific home exercises targeting hip abductors and quadriceps alongside manual therapy) against a sham treatment (inactive ultrasound and inert gel). At the 13-week mark, the study found **no significant between-group differences** in either pain reduction or physical function improvement (Bennell et al., 2014).

### Clinical Takeaway

While structured land-based exercise is safe and can improve objective biomechanical measures like strength and range of motion, it is important to set realistic expectations with your patients. The primary trial evidence indicates that exercise yields inconsistent effects on subjective pain and physical function for hip OA, and it has not reliably outperformed robust placebo treatments.
