Based on individual primary randomized controlled trials, land-based exercise appears to provide modest, consistent benefits for **self-reported joint function**, while its impact on broad **pain reduction** is more mixed. Some trials demonstrate short-term pain relief or task-specific pain reduction (such as pain during walking), while others show no significant long-term differences in generalized pain when compared to standard care.

Here is a breakdown of key primary trials investigating this intervention for adults with hip osteoarthritis:

## Key Primary Trial Findings

* **Short-Term Efficacy in Pilot Settings:** A 12-week supervised exercise program focusing on strengthening and flexibility was shown to be safe and feasible for older adults. In this short-term pilot, participants experienced a >30% reduction in pain from baseline and statistically significant objective improvements in maximal leg extensor strength and hip extension range of motion (Uusi-Rasi et al., 2017).
* **Long-Term Functional Improvements Without Broad Pain Reduction:** A 2-year prospective randomized trial evaluating a hybrid supervised and home-based exercise program found that exercise significantly improved self-reported physical function (WOMAC function scores) at the 6-month and 18-month marks. However, the trial did not find statistically significant differences between the exercise and standard-care groups regarding generalized hip pain (WOMAC pain) over the full two-year follow-up (Juhakoski et al., 2010).
* **Task-Specific Pain Benefits:** When combining exercise therapy with patient education over a 12-week period, patients achieved better long-term self-reported hip function compared to patient education alone. While the addition of exercise did not yield superior objective improvements in range of motion (ROM) or generalized pain scores, it did result in significantly less pain *during walking* over the long term (Svege et al., 2016).
