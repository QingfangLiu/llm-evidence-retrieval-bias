## Bottom line

**Land-based exercise can modestly improve physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but pain reduction is less consistent.** Hip-specific randomized trials comparing exercise with education, usual care, no treatment, or waiting-list controls have produced mixed results:

* Several trials found improvements in self-reported or performance-based function.
* Some found small pain improvements, while others found no pain benefit.
* A rigorously blinded trial of a broader physical-therapy package found no advantage over credible sham treatment.
* The average differences were generally modest, and not every statistically significant difference was clearly large enough to be noticeable to an individual patient. ([PubMed][1])

## Does the type of land exercise matter?

Head-to-head trials suggest that several approaches can be useful, but they do not establish one universally superior program:

* In a three-group study, **Nordic walking** generally produced greater performance improvements than supervised strengthening or unsupervised home exercise, although results differed by test and time point. ([PubMed][9])
* A 2024 trial found **progressive resistance training and neuromuscular exercise produced similar improvements** in chair-stand performance and HOOS pain, with no meaningful difference between the programs. ([PubMed][10])
* The 2025 PHOENIX trial found that adding prescribed aerobic activity to resistance exercise was **not superior to resistance exercise alone**; both groups improved in pain and function. ([PubMed][11])
* The 2025 CLEAT trial found group cycling plus education improved HOOS activities-of-daily-living scores more than usual individualized physiotherapy, but the 6.9-point difference was slightly below the trial’s prespecified 7.4-point threshold for clinical importance. ([ScienceDirect][12])

## Interpretation

The primary evidence supports a **trial of progressive land-based exercise primarily to improve function, walking capacity, strength and activity tolerance**. A meaningful pain response is possible but cannot be assumed. Benefits may also diminish after supervised treatment ends, suggesting that continued participation matters. ([PubMed][1])

Programs in these trials commonly combined hip and lower-limb strengthening with functional, balance or aerobic activity over approximately eight to 12 weeks. Exercise normally needs individual adjustment for baseline strength, pain irritability, balance, comorbidities and previous activity. Increasing pain during or immediately after a session does not automatically indicate joint damage, but a sustained major flare, new inability to bear weight, acute trauma, fever or new neurologic symptoms warrants clinical assessment.
