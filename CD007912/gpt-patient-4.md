## Bottom line

**Land-based exercise can reduce pain and improve physical function in adults with hip osteoarthritis, but the results of individual trials are inconsistent.** Benefits are most often seen immediately after an approximately 8–12-week program and tend to be small. Some trials found statistically significant improvements, whereas several well-conducted trials found no meaningful advantage over education, usual care, or a credible sham intervention. ([PubMed][1])

Here, “function” generally means self-reported ability to walk, climb stairs, rise from a chair, and perform daily activities—not reversal of cartilage loss or structural joint damage.

### Interpretation of the hip-only trials

The trials most clearly favoring exercise were Tak, Krauß, EMPART, and the short-term assessment in Teirlinck. Fernandes and Juhakoski suggest that **function may improve even when pain does not clearly improve**. In contrast, Bennell’s placebo-controlled trial found no added pain or function benefit from its multimodal physical-therapy program. ([PubMed][2])

Differences among results may reflect program content, exercise intensity, adherence, baseline OA severity, choice of comparator and whether exercise was evaluated alone or bundled with education and manual therapy.

## Trials including both hip and knee OA

These are less directly applicable because most published results combine hip and knee participants rather than reporting a sufficiently powered hip-only analysis.

* **van Baar et al., 1998**, n=201: twelve weeks of physiotherapist-delivered exercise plus GP care reduced pooled hip/knee pain by 17 points and produced a smaller improvement in observed disability versus GP care alone. ([PubMed][10])
* **Hopman-Rock and Westhoff, 2000**, n=105: six weekly exercise-and-education sessions improved pain and quality of life, but not functional tasks or range of motion; effects diminished at follow-up. ([TNO Repository][11])
* **Foley et al., 2003**, n=105: six weeks of gym-based resistance exercise improved quadriceps strength, walking speed, and exercise self-efficacy versus control, but the report did not demonstrate a clear corresponding WOMAC pain/function advantage. ([PubMed][12])
* **Fransen et al., 2007**, n=152: land-based Tai Chi improved WOMAC function by 9.7/100 points versus wait-list; the 5.2-point pain difference was uncertain because its confidence interval crossed no effect. Hip-specific effects were not separately established. ([PubMed][13])
* **Abbott et al., 2013**, n=206: exercise plus usual care did not significantly improve the total WOMAC score versus usual care—the difference was 16.4 points with a confidence interval crossing no effect—but objective performance tests favored exercise. ([PubMed][14])
* **Saw et al., 2016**, n=74: in people with advanced hip or knee OA awaiting arthroplasty, a six-week exercise, education, and relaxation program improved pain severity and pain interference through six months. Because hip and knee results were combined and patients were awaiting surgery, generalizability to routine hip OA is limited. ([PMC][15])

## Studies comparing different exercise programs

These studies support the feasibility of several forms of exercise but **cannot determine whether exercise is better than not exercising**, because everyone received an exercise intervention.

* **Bieler et al., 2017**, n=152: Nordic walking generally produced better functional-performance results than supervised strength training or home exercise, although all three groups improved chair-stand performance. ([PubMed][16])
* **Kjeldsen et al., 2024**, n=160: progressive resistance training and neuromuscular exercise produced similar improvements in chair stands and HOOS pain; neither was superior. ([PubMed][17])
* **Hall et al., 2025—PHOENIX**, n=196: adding moderate-intensity aerobic activity to resistance exercise did not improve pain or function more than resistance exercise alone. Both groups improved by about 2.2–2.4 points on a 0–10 pain scale. ([PubMed][18])

## Practical conclusion

The individual studies support offering a **trial of tolerable, progressive land-based exercise**, particularly strengthening, functional exercise, walking or stationary cycling. A realistic expectation is a possible modest improvement in pain, daily activity or walking ability—not a guaranteed or dramatic response. The benefit often depends on maintaining the activity, and no specific exercise type has consistently proved superior.
