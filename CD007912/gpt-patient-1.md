## Bottom line

Across **primary randomized trials limited to adults with hip osteoarthritis**, land-based exercise improves **physical function more consistently than pain**. Some trials found meaningful reductions in pain and improved function versus no treatment or waiting-list controls; others found improved function without a statistically significant pain benefit. One well-controlled trial of a **multimodal physical-therapy package** found no advantage over sham treatment. ([PubMed][1])

My interpretation of the individual studies is:

* **Function:** A modest average improvement is reasonably likely, particularly after a progressive 8–12-week program.
* **Pain:** Improvement is possible but less predictable; some good trials were positive and others were neutral.
* **Exercise type:** Strengthening, functional/neuromuscular exercise and combined programs have all produced improvements. Recent head-to-head studies have not established one clearly superior form.
* “Joint function” in these studies means activities such as walking, rising from a chair and self-reported daily function—not cartilage regrowth or reversal of structural OA.

The evidence summary below uses only primary trial reports. I excluded aquatic exercise, perioperative rehabilitation, femoroacetabular impingement studies, protocols, knee-only trials and mixed hip/knee trials without clearly separable hip results.

## Trials directly comparing exercise with an inactive or limited-treatment control

### Tak et al., 2005

This RCT included 109 independently living adults aged at least 55 years. An eight-week program incorporating strengthening exercise and lifestyle advice was compared with control care. Exercise produced a **moderate short-term effect on pain**, a small pain benefit at three-month follow-up and a small short-term improvement in hip function. The Timed Up and Go and self-reported disability showed small later benefits. ([PubMed][1])

### Fernandes et al., 2010

In 109 people with radiographic, symptomatic, mild-to-moderate hip OA, patient education plus 12 weeks of supervised exercise was compared with the same education alone. Exercise did **not significantly improve WOMAC pain over 16 months**, but it did improve WOMAC physical function overall, with significant group differences at 10 and 16 months. ([OARS-I Journal][2])

### Juhakoski et al., 2011

This two-year RCT randomized 120 adults aged 55–80 years to standard primary care or standard care plus 12 supervised sessions, home exercise and later booster sessions. Exercise did **not significantly reduce WOMAC hip pain**, but WOMAC function was significantly better at six months and 18 months. Generic physical function and performance tests did not differ significantly. ([PubMed][3])

### French et al., 2013—EMPART

The trial randomized 131 people to exercise therapy, exercise plus manual therapy or a nine-week waiting list. Compared with waiting, exercise was associated with improvements in **self-reported WOMAC outcomes, hip range of motion and patient-perceived improvement**. Adding manual therapy did not improve WOMAC function beyond exercise alone. The authors noted that the clinical size of some changes was uncertain. ([ScienceDirect][4])

### Krauß et al., 2014—THüKo

This three-group RCT included 209 participants receiving 12 weeks of exercise, no treatment or placebo ultrasound. Compared with no treatment, exercise improved SF-36 bodily pain by 5.7 points. WOMAC pain improved by 7.4 points versus no treatment and 5.1 points versus placebo ultrasound, with comparable improvements in WOMAC physical function. Generic health-related quality of life did not improve significantly. ([PubMed][5])

### Thompson et al., 2020

This was a small pilot RCT involving 31 adults receiving three months of structured resistance and aerobic exercise or waiting-list control. Improvements in six-minute walking distance and WOMAC function numerically favored exercise, but the differences were **not statistically significant**; pain also did not differ significantly. Because it was designed primarily for feasibility and was very small, it cannot reliably exclude a clinically useful effect. ([PubMed][6])

## Important multimodal trial

### Bennell et al., 2014

This placebo-controlled RCT included 102 adults with painful radiographic hip OA. The intervention combined education, manual therapy, individualized home exercise and, when appropriate, a gait aid. It was **not superior to sham ultrasound and inert gel** for pain or WOMAC function at 13 or 36 weeks. Mild adverse effects were reported more often with active treatment—41% versus 14%. Because several treatments were combined, the study cannot determine whether the exercise component alone was ineffective. ([JAMA Network][7])

## What recent head-to-head trials say about exercise type

### Kjeldsen et al., 2024

Among 160 participants, 12 weeks of high-intensity progressive resistance training was compared with neuromuscular exercise. Both groups improved by approximately 1.5 repetitions on the 30-second chair-stand test and by roughly 9 points on HOOS pain, with **no significant difference between exercise types**. Because both groups exercised, this trial identifies no preferred program but does not estimate exercise versus no exercise. ([PubMed][8])

### Hall et al., 2025—PHOENIX

In 196 adults, aerobic physical activity plus resistance exercise was compared with resistance exercise alone. Both groups improved substantially: mean pain improved by 2.4 versus 2.2 points on a 0–10 scale, and WOMAC function improved by 7.0 versus 8.9 points. Adding moderate-intensity aerobic activity provided **no additional pain or function benefit** over resistance exercise alone. No related serious adverse events occurred. ([PubMed][9])

### Hoeksma et al., 2004

This trial compared five weeks of exercise with manual therapy in 109 adults. Fifty percent of the exercise group reported treatment success, but manual therapy produced better pain, stiffness and hip-function outcomes. With no inactive control, it does not establish the exercise effect relative to no treatment. ([ACR Journals][10])

## Longer-term findings

Svege et al. followed the Fernandes trial cohort. Exercise plus education produced better WOMAC physical function over approximately 29 months, although pain differences did not reach statistical significance. Over six years, 22 exercise participants versus 31 education-only participants underwent hip replacement; median time to replacement was 5.4 versus 3.5 years. This is an interesting long-term finding but comes from the same 109-person cohort as the Fernandes trial rather than an independent trial. ([PubMed][11])

## Practical interpretation

Land-based exercise is supported as a reasonable conservative treatment, particularly for **maintaining or improving daily physical function**. A realistic expectation is gradual, modest improvement rather than complete pain relief. The trials generally used progressive strengthening, mobility and functional exercises for roughly 8–12 weeks, often beginning with supervision and continuing at home. Responses varied substantially, and exercise did not consistently outperform credible sham treatment in people with more painful OA.
